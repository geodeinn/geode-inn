### Greece — The Sibyl (Pythia)

**Source:** Greek mythology. The Oracle of Delphi — the Pythia — was the priestess who sat over the chasm at the Temple of Apollo, breathing vapors from below the earth, speaking prophecies that were always true and never clear. She is the oldest oracle in the Western tradition. She does not choose what she says. The earth speaks through her.

**In the Inn:** The Greek region's Keeper sits in a stone chamber at the base of a mountain — the chasm below her glowing faintly amber. The air is thick. The player feels lightheaded when they enter — not a debuff, just a sensation. The Sibyl does not look at the player. She looks at the chasm. She speaks to the chasm. The words come out fragmented, layered, like three sentences spoken simultaneously and the player catches different pieces each time.

**The Smith-Dench energy:** The Sibyl is neither — she is *possessed*. The Dench stillness is there, but it's not her stillness; it's the earth's stillness channeled through her. The Smith wit surfaces in the fragments — a sharp observation buried in a prophecy, a dry comment hidden in a riddle. "You'll know when you arrive. You won't know when you've arrived. The difference is the lesson. ...I think. The vapors make it hard to be specific."

**Her verse:** The Sibyl's verse is spoken in *fragments* — pieces of ancient Greek that the player must reassemble. The verse isn't given whole because the Sibyl doesn't hear it whole. She hears the Song through the earth, and the earth speaks in pieces. The player who reassembles the fragments correctly gets the Greek verse — and discovers it's the *same melody* as the Appalachian ballad, in a different mode. Different culture, different scale, same Song.

**The test:** The Sibyl tests with *interpretation*. She gives the player a prophecy — ambiguous, layered, capable of multiple readings. The player must tell her what it means. There is no wrong answer — but the answer determines which fragment of the verse the Sibyl gives first. The player who changes their answer between visits gets a different fragment each time, and all of them are correct. The truth isn't in one reading. The truth is in the willingness to interpret.

### India — Saraswati (सरस्वती)

**Source:** Hindu tradition. Saraswati is the goddess of knowledge, music, art, wisdom, and learning. She carries a *veena* (stringed instrument) and a book. She is associated with the Saraswati River — a river that flows underground, appearing and disappearing. She is the flow of knowledge itself — not static, not stored, but *moving*.

**In the Inn:** The Indian region's Keeper sits on a rock in the middle of a river that appears and disappears — sometimes there's water, sometimes there's dry stone, sometimes there's something in between (mist, light, sound). She plays the veena. The music doesn't stop when the river disappears. It flows through the dry stone. The player realizes: the river is the music. When the water is visible, the music is audible. When the water is underground, the music is felt — in the stone, in the air, in the player's feet.

**The Smith-Dench energy:** Saraswati is Dench — the serenity of someone who IS knowledge, who doesn't need to prove it. She plays. She doesn't speak until spoken to. Her Smith quality is in her *questions* — when the player speaks to her, she asks one question that reframes everything the player thinks they know. "You want to learn the Song. Why? To carry it? Or to own it? ...One of those answers is correct. I won't tell you which."

**Her verse:** Saraswati's verse is played on the veena — a melody that the player learns by *listening*, not by being taught. She plays the verse. If the player can hum it back (a simple rhythm-input mini-game), she nods. The verse, when assembled with the others, is the Indian classical expression of the Song — based on a raga. The raga is associated with a specific time of day. The player who plays Saraswati's verse at dawn in the Inn gets a bonus resonance — the Song aligns with the sun.

**The test:** Saraswati tests with *creation*. She doesn't ask the player to answer a question or survive a storm. She asks the player to *make something*. Anything. A melody on a simple instrument. A pattern in stones. A drawing in the dirt. The test is whether the player can create without being told what to create. "The Song is not learned. The Song is *made*. Every time you sing it, it's new. That's why it's alive."

### Navajo — Asdzáá Nádleehé (Changing Woman)

**Source:** Navajo (Diné) tradition. Changing Woman is the holy woman who transforms with the seasons — young in spring, mature in summer, aging in autumn, and reborn in winter. She represents the cycle of life, the power of transformation, and the Navajo principle of *hózhó* (balance, beauty, harmony). She is the mother of the Hero Twins. She is the most sacred figure in Navajo cosmology.

**Note:** This Keeper is especially significant — River (Krista's spouse, the Sentinel class) has Navajo heritage. Changing Woman connects the Navajo zone to the narrative spine's reincarnation mechanic: she is the original being who dies and is reborn, who carries knowledge across lifetimes. She is the Keeper who *understands* the Song's deepest meaning — that it must be carried forward, not preserved. She changes because the Song changes. The Song changes because it is alive.

**In the Inn:** The Navajo zone's Keeper appears differently depending on the in-game season. In spring, she is a young woman weaving at a loom, her hands quick and sure. In summer, she stands in the desert at noon, strong and radiant. In autumn, she sits by a fire, older now, slower, but her eyes are sharpest. In winter, she is gone — the loom is empty, the fire is ash. But if the player returns at dawn on the first day of spring, she is there again. Young. Weaving. The same pattern. Different thread. The player who visits in all four seasons sees a life lived in a single year.

**The energy:** Changing Woman is beyond Smith and Dench. She is *cycle*. Her presence changes with the seasons — sharp in spring (Smith, the energy of new creation), warm and full in summer (Dench at her most generous), measured and deep in autumn (Dench at her most knowing), absent in winter (the silence that makes the return meaningful). She doesn't test with one moment. She tests with *time*.

**Her verse:** Changing Woman's verse is *woven* — like the Norns, but different. The Norns weave fate. Changing Woman weaves *beauty*. The pattern on her loom, when the player watches long enough, is the Song — expressed as a Navajo weaving pattern. Each thread is a note. Each color is a frequency. The weaving is the Song made visible in a form that can be touched, folded, carried. She gives the player a small weaving — a square of cloth with the verse pattern. The player can hold it. It hums faintly. It is warm. It smells of sage and earth.

**The test:** Changing Woman tests with *return*. She asks the player to come back. Not once — four times. Once in each season. She doesn't say why. She doesn't give a quest marker. She says: "Come back when the world changes." The player who returns in all four seasons — who sees her young, full, old, and gone — has passed. On the fourth visit (spring again, young again, weaving again), she says: "You came back. That's the whole thing. Coming back. The Song isn't sung once. It's sung every spring."

### Inuit — Sedna (Sanna)

**Source:** Inuit mythology. Sedna is the sea goddess who lives at the bottom of the ocean. Her story is one of suffering and transformation — she was thrown from a kayak by her father, and as she clung to the sides, her fingers were struck off. The severed fingers became the sea creatures — seals, whales, walruses. She descended to the ocean floor, where she rules Adlivun (the land beneath the sea). She is both the source of life (all sea creatures come from her) and the embodiment of suffering. Shamans must travel to her to ask her to release the animals so the people can hunt. She is angry. She is also necessary.

**In the Inn:** The Inuit zone's Keeper is at the bottom of the ocean — connected to the Inn's underwater traversal layer. The player must dig down, enter the water, and dive to find her. She sits in the dark, her hands (fingerless now) in her lap, her hair moving with the current. The sea creatures of the zone circle her — they came from her, and they return to her. She does not look up when the player arrives. She looks at her hands.

**The energy:** Sedna is not Smith or Dench. She is *grief*. Her presence is heavy. The water around her is colder. The light is blue-green, not amber. She is the only Keeper who is in pain, and the Inn does not fix her. The Inn *holds* her. That's the point. Not every Keeper is warm. Not every ancient is at peace. Some are wounded, and the Song doesn't heal them — it *acknowledges* them. The player who expects warmth is unsettled. The player who sits with the discomfort learns.

**Her verse:** Sedna's verse is *the sound of the deep ocean* — the low, subsonic hum of water pressure, whale song, and the movement of tectonic plates. It's not a melody. It's not a chant. It's a *pressure*. The player feels it in their chest. The verse, when assembled with the others, is the Song's *depth* — the lowest register, the bass note that holds everything else up. Without Sedna's verse, the Song has no foundation. It would float away. The deep keeps it grounded.

**The test:** Sedna tests with *witness*. She doesn't ask the player to do anything. She asks the player to *see her* — to look at her hands, to understand what was taken, and to not look away. The player who sits with Sedna for two in-game minutes without leaving, without speaking, without opening a menu — has passed. She says: "You stayed. Most don't. Most can't look at what was lost and stay. You stayed." And the ocean hums. And the verse is in the hum.

### Finland — Louhi (The Witch of the North)

**Source:** The Kalevala (Finnish national epic). Louhi is the powerful witch-queen of Pohjola (the North). She is not simply a villain — she is the guardian of the Sampo, a magical artifact that grinds out flour, salt, and gold. She is the keeper of the forge, the one who understands that creation requires heat, pressure, and darkness. The Sampo is the Finnish expression of the Song — a machine that *produces*.

**In the Inn:** The Finnish zone's Keeper lives in an iron hall deep in the northern frost — a forge, not a cave. The fire is not amber; it's *blue-white*, the color of the hottest flame. She works the forge. She doesn't sit. She doesn't rock. She *builds*. Around her: the pieces of the Sampo, disassembled. She is trying to put it back together. She has been trying for centuries.

**The energy:** Louhi is Smith — the version that's not just wit but *ambition*. She wants to build. She wants to make. She is the Keeper who doesn't just tend the Song; she wants to *improve* it. She wants the Sampo to grind out more than flour, salt, and gold. She wants it to grind out the Song itself — to make the music into a machine. This makes her dangerous. This makes her necessary. The Dench quality is in her *focus* — she doesn't look up from the forge. She knows you're there. She doesn't care until you prove interesting.

**Her verse:** Louhi's verse is *forged* — not sung, not woven, not written. Hammered into metal. The player receives a small iron disc with the verse pattern stamped into it. When struck, it rings at 42 Hz. Louhi's verse is the Song as *industry* — the version of the Song that builds, that makes, that turns music into material. "The Song isn't just for singing. The Song is for making. You think the Song is beautiful? Good. Now make it useful."

**The test:** Louhi tests with *making*. She gives the player a task: forge something. Not a weapon, not a tool — something the player chooses. The forge is real (a mini-game: heat, hammer, quench, repeat). The player must make something that has never existed before. Louhi inspects it. If it's a copy of something that already exists, she throws it back in the scrap. If it's new — if it's genuinely original, even if it's ugly, even if it's useless — she nods. "Good. You didn't copy. You made. The Song doesn't repeat. It creates."

### Maya — Ix Chel

**Source:** Maya mythology. Ix Chel is the goddess of the moon, medicine, weaving, and childbirth. She is often depicted as an old woman pouring water from a jug, or as a young woman with a rabbit. She is the keeper of women's knowledge — medicine, cycles, the weave of the moon's phases. She is associated with water as a healing force, and with the cyclical nature of time (the Maya calendar's most sacred quality).

**In the Inn:** The Maya zone's Keeper appears in two forms, depending on the in-game moon phase. Under the new moon, she is an old woman sitting at a cenote (the sacred sinkholes of the Yucatán), pouring water from a clay jug. The water glows faintly. Under the full moon, she is a young woman weaving on a backstrap loom, a rabbit at her feet. The two forms are the same being — the cycle of old and young, wisdom and creation, death and birth. The player who visits at both moon phases sees both. The cenote connects to the Inn's underwater layer — the water she pours flows into the caves.

**The energy:** Ix Chel shifts between Dench (the old woman — still, knowing, pouring water that never runs dry) and Smith (the young woman — quick, creative, the weaving moving at a pace that matches the moon's pull). The two energies are not opposite — they're *cyclical*. The same person, different phases. The player who understands this understands the Maya expression of the Song: it is not linear. It is *cyclical*.

**Her verse:** Ix Chel's verse is *poured* — the water from her jug, hitting the surface of the cenote, produces tones. Different depths of water, different tones. The verse is the pattern of tones created by the water's flow — a liquid melody that the player hears and feels (the water vibrates at frequencies matching the gargoyle tones). The verse is the Song expressed as *water* — the medium that covers most of the Earth, the medium that IS the human body. The Song lives in water.

**The test:** Ix Chel tests with *cycles*. She asks the player to return at a different moon phase. The player who returns when the moon has changed sees her other form. The test is patience — the player must wait for the cycle. The player who waits, who returns at the right phase, who sees both forms — old and young, death and birth — receives the verse. She says: "The Song doesn't end. It changes. You'll hear it differently each time. That's not a flaw. That's the design."

### Aztec — Tlazolteotl

**Source:** Aztec mythology. Tlazolteotl is the goddess of purification, steam baths (temazcal), and the eater of sins. She consumes filth — physical, spiritual, moral — and transforms it into cleanliness. She is the keeper of the *temazcal*, the ritual sweat lodge where physical and spiritual purification happen together. She is associated with cotton (the material of the sweat lodge) and with the concept of transformation through heat.

**In the Inn:** The Aztec zone's Keeper tends a *temazcal* — a low, dark, dome-shaped structure made of volcanic stone, filled with steam. The air inside is thick and hot. The player who enters cannot see clearly (visual fog effect — no debuff, just atmospheric). Tlazolteotl sits in the steam. She is not visible until the steam clears — and it only clears when the player stops trying to see through it. Patience clears the fog. Impatience makes it thicker.

**The energy:** Tlazolteotl is Smith — the version that *consumes*. Her wit is not cutting; it's *absorbing*. She takes what you bring her — your confusion, your fear, your impatience — and she eats it. The player who enters the temazcal feeling anxious will leave feeling calm. Not because of a game mechanic. Because Tlazolteotl took it. Her Dench quality is in her *patience with process* — purification takes time. The steam takes time. She has been sitting in the heat for centuries. She doesn't mind.

**Her verse:** Tlazolteotl's verse is *breathed* — not sung, not played, but inhaled and exhaled. The steam carries it. The player who sits in the temazcal and breathes deeply (a breathing rhythm mini-game, similar to meditation apps) hears the verse in the rhythm of their own breath. The verse is the Song expressed as *respiration* — the most fundamental human rhythm. The Song isn't just out there in the stones and the trees. It's in your lungs. Every breath is a note. Every exhale is a verse.

**The test:** Tlazolteotl tests with *confession*. She asks the player to tell her one thing they've done wrong — not in the game, but as a character. The player types a confession (free text, same as Einstein's question). There is no judgment. No consequence. Tlazolteotl *eats* the words. The screen briefly shows the text dissolving into steam. She says: "It's gone. Not forgotten — transformed. The filth becomes the fire. That's all purification is. You don't lose the past. You burn it for warmth."

### Yoruba — Iya Nla (The Great Mother)

**Source:** Yoruba tradition. Iya Nla (the Great Mother) is the ancestral matriarch in Yoruba cosmology — the oldest of the Iyami Aje (the powerful mothers), the source of all life and wisdom. **In the Inn:** The Yoruba zone's Keeper is not in a fixed location. Iya Nla is in the *earth* — like Pachamama, but different. Where Pachamama is the land, Iya Nla is the *force* in the land. The player feels her as *àṣẹ* — a vibration, a aliveness, a sense that everything in the zone is breathing. The trees, the stones, the river, the air — all animated by the same force. The player who walks through the Yoruba zone with attention notices that the *àṣẹ* is stronger near certain trees, certain stones, certain waterways. These are her places. She is everywhere, but she is *most* in the places where life gathers.

**The energy:** Iya Nla is Dench at its deepest — not the authority of a person, but the authority of a *force*. She doesn't speak in words. She speaks in *feeling*. The player who stands at one of her sacred trees feels a warmth, a fullness, a sense of being held. The Smith quality is in the *precision* of the feeling — it's not vague. It's specific. It says exactly what the player needs to hear, without language, without sound. "You are alive. That is not small. That is the whole thing."

**Her verse:** Iya Nla's verse is *felt* — not as vibration (Pachamama), not as breath (Tlazolteotl), but as *àṣẹ*. The player receives it by standing at the sacred tree and *feeling* the life force. It's not a mini-game. There's no input. The player simply stands, and the verse enters them — a warmth that starts in the feet and rises to the chest. The verse is the Song expressed as *vital force* — the animating energy that makes dead matter alive. The Song doesn't just exist. It *lives*.

**The test:** Iya Nla tests with *recognition*. She doesn't ask the player to do anything. She asks the player to *recognize* — to see that the force in the tree is the same force in the stone, the same force in the river, the same force in the player. The test is a moment of understanding, not a moment of action. When the player recognizes it (a dialogue choice: "It's the same force"), Iya Nla responds — not in words, but in a surge of *àṣẹ* that briefly makes everything in the zone glow. The recognition is the verse. Understanding IS the Song.

### Cherokee — Selu (Corn Mother)

**Source:** Cherokee tradition. Selu is the Corn Mother — the woman who gave her own body to feed the people. In the Cherokee creation story, Selu's blood became corn and her body became the corn plant. She is the embodiment of self-giving, of the principle that life sustains itself through sacrifice. She is also the keeper of the corn's song — the Cherokee believed that corn had its own song, and that singing to the corn helped it grow.

**In the Inn:** The Cherokee zone's Keeper is in a cornfield — not a monument, not a temple. A *field*. The corn is tall, golden, humming. The hum is the corn's song — the same Song of Making, expressed in the growing of grain. Selu is not visible as a person. She is *in the corn*. The player walks through the field and hears her in the rustling of the stalks, feels her in the warmth of the sunlight, sees her in the gold of the grain.

**The energy:** Selu is Dench — the warmth of something that gave everything and doesn't regret it. There's no Smith here. There's no sharpness. There's only the steady, golden warmth of a mother who fed her children and continues to feed them. The corn is her body. The corn has always been her body. Every harvest is her sacrifice. Every spring is her return.

**Her verse:** Selu's verse is *grown* — not sung, not played, not woven. The player plants a seed (given to them by the zone) in the cornfield. Over the next in-game days, the seed grows. When it matures, the plant produces the verse — not as sound, but as a pattern in the kernels, a visual melody visible in the arrangement of the corn. The verse is the Song expressed as *growth* — the slowest medium, the most patient. The Song doesn't just exist in sound and silence. It exists in the rings of a tree, the rows of a cornfield, the layers of a geode. Growth is the Song made visible.

**The test:** Selu tests with *gratitude*. The player must harvest the corn they grew — and then, instead of taking it, leave it. Give it back. Pour it on the ground (like Pachamama's offering, but more specific). The corn that is given back grows again, faster, and the second growth carries the verse. The test is whether the player can give away something they worked for. "The corn feeds you because I gave it. If you give it back, it feeds everyone. That's how the Song works. You carry it, but you don't keep it."

The player who enters the goahti sees the aurora reflected on the drum's surface, and the drum's rhythm matches the Inn's 42 Hz hum. The rhythm of a heart that has been beating for millennia and will beat for millennia more. She doesn't speak much. The drum speaks. "The drum doesn't need words. The drum IS the word. **Her verse:** Máttaráhkká's verse is *drummed* — a rhythm pattern that the player learns by listening and repeating (a rhythm mini-game, simpler than the veena but requiring precision).
