# Geode Inn — The Bayou and the Buried Cities
## Swamp, Colonial Gothic, and the Door to What Was There Before

---

## The Structure

Three layers, descending:

1. **THE SWAMP** (surface) — Spanish moss, bayou, humidity, the American South. The Don't Starve-type survival game is played here. The swamp is the GATEWAY — you survive it to reach the structure.
2. **THE COLONIAL GOTHIC STRUCTURE** (mid-layer) — A colonial-era church or mission, Gothic in style, half-consumed by the swamp. Reached by completing the survival game. The structure is the OVERLAY — the colonial layer built on top of what was there before.
3. **THE BURIED CITIES** (deep layer) — Ancient cities beneath jungles and rainforests, accessed through a door in the structure's basement. The cities are the TRUTH — the pre-colonial civilizations that the jungle swallowed and colonialism erased.

The descent is the thesis: the colonial structure is the surface. The buried cities are the reality. The basement door is the Inn's refusal to let the colonial overlay erase the pre-colonial truth.

## Layer One: The Swamp

### The Environment

The swamp is the American South — the Louisiana bayou, the Atchafalaya, the cypress-tupelo wetlands that stretch across the Gulf Coast. The swamp is FLAT — no elevation, no vantage point, no horizon. The sky and the water are the same color (grey-green on overcast days, black-silver on clear nights). The trees are cypress — tall, straight, their trunks swollen at the base, their knees (pneumatophores — root projections that rise above the waterline like wooden fingers) breaking the surface of the still water. The water is SLOW — not stagnant, but patient. The water moves at the pace of the bayou: inches per day, the slowest current in the Inn, slower than the desert sand, slower than the geological time. The swamp's time is BIOLOGICAL time — the time of growth, of decay, of the cycle that turns dead leaves into soil and soil into trees and trees into dead leaves. The swamp is the Inn's COMPOST — the place where death feeds life, where the buried cities are being absorbed and transformed into the living jungle.

**Spanish moss** (Tillandsia usneoides) hangs from every branch — the iconic grey-green curtains that drape the cypress trees like funeral veils. Spanish moss is an EPIPHYTE — it grows on other plants but doesn't take nutrients from them. It absorbs water and nutrients from the AIR. Spanish moss has no roots in the soil. Spanish moss lives entirely in the air, hanging, absorbing the atmosphere.

In the Inn's frequency system, Spanish moss is the swamp's FREQUENCY CATCHER — the epiphyte that absorbs aerial frequencies, the way the singing vines catch the Song in the garden and the impatient moss catches time in the stones. Spanish moss catches the AIRBORNE Song — the frequencies that propagate through the atmosphere, the 42Hz Song's aerial transmission. The moss hangs from the trees in long, grey curtains, and when a frequency passes through the air, the moss VIBRATES — the curtains sway, the strands tremble, the moss makes the invisible audible (visible, tactile — the frequency is felt in the moss's movement). The swamp's Spanish moss is the Inn's most VISIBLE frequency medium — the air frequencies made into hanging, swaying, touchable curtains.

The moss's grey-green color is the color of the swamp's frequency — the color of the airborne Song filtered through humidity and biological time. The moss is grey because the swamp's frequency is MUTED — the 42Hz Song, propagated through the dense, humid air, is dampened, softened, made into a murmur. The swamp doesn't SING (the way the garden's vines sing). The swamp MURMURS. The swamp is the Inn's quietest frequency zone — the place where the Song is most muffled, most absorbed, most transformed by the biological medium it passes through. The player who is overwhelmed by the Inn's louder frequencies (the forge's ring, the music room's compositions, the cosmology's crystal spikes) can find the swamp's murmur CALMING — the frequency turned down, the Song made into background, the volume at its lowest.

### The Aesthetic

The swamp's visual direction is GOTHIC SOUTHERN — the aesthetic of decay, of beauty in decomposition, of grandeur being consumed by nature. Think: abandoned plantation gardens reclaimed by wisteria. Iron gates rusted open. Stone steps green with moss. A chandelier visible through the broken roof of a ballroom, trees growing through the floor. The aesthetic is NOT horror — it's the beauty of RETURN. The swamp is returning everything to the earth. The colonial structure is being returned. The buried cities were returned long ago. The swamp is the RETURN itself — the patient, biological, humid process of taking the built and making it grown.

The Don't Starve connection: the swamp's game aesthetic is Don't Starve's hand-drawn, dark, Tim Burton-esque visual style — 2D, papercraft-adjacent, with exaggerated shadows and a slightly unsettling charm. The game's visual style is the swamp's NATIVE aesthetic — the way O'Keeffe's simplification is the Desert Bone's native lens. Don't Starve's style IS the swamp's way of seeing: the world rendered in dark ink and watercolor, the trees as angular silhouettes, the moss as hanging strokes of a dry brush, the water as black mirror with grey reflections. The style is GOTHIC but not grim — the same way the Inn is Cozy Gothic, not horror. The swamp is unsettling but not hostile. The swamp is STRANGE but not dangerous (if you know the rules). The Don't Starve aesthetic is the swamp's lens — the FEELING of the swamp rendered in hand-drawn dark beauty.

### The Game: "The Reclamation"

A Don't Starve-type survival crafting game played in the swamp. The player gathers resources (cypress wood, moss, water plants, clay from the bayou floor), crafts tools and shelter, and maintains SANITY — the key mechanic.

**Sanity in the swamp:** The swamp is disorienting. The flat terrain, the lack of landmarks, the humidity, the darkness under the canopy, the sounds (insects, water, the moss murmuring) — all of these press on the player's sanity. The Don't Starve sanity mechanic (the more you're in the dark, the more the shadows press in, the more your mind generates threats that aren't there) is the swamp's core challenge. The player must maintain their sanity by:
- **Light** — crafting lanterns from cypress wood and swamp gas (the bioluminescent bacteria in the bayou water). The light keeps the shadows at bay. But the light also ATTRACTS the swamp's creatures (the way light attracts insects). The player must balance: light for sanity, darkness for stealth.
- **Fire** — the swamp's fires are GREEN. The swamp gas (methane from decomposition) burns green. The green fire is the swamp's native light source — not the warm amber of the Inn's hearths, but the eerie, biological green of chemical luminescence. The green fire is the swamp's contribution to the Inn's light vocabulary — the light that comes from DECAY, not from combustion. The green fire is decomposition made visible. The green fire is the swamp's thesis: death produces light.
- **Shelter** — crafting a raised platform (the way the real bayou dwellers built raised houses on stilts to avoid the water). The shelter must be above the waterline. The water RISES at night (the swamp's tide — the bayou's water level fluctuates with the Gulf tides, even miles inland). The player must build high enough to stay dry. The building mechanic is the game's CRAFTING core — the player constructs a shelter from cypress wood, Spanish moss (insulation), and clay (sealant).
- **Navigation** — the swamp has no paths. The player navigates by the moss (the Spanish moss hangs in the direction of the frequency — the moss always leans toward the nearest frequency node). The moss is the swamp's COMPASS — the way the Pulse Line is the Inn's compass. The player who reads the moss can find their way. The player who ignores the moss gets lost.

**The goal:** The player must survive long enough to reach the colonial Gothic structure. The structure is visible from certain points in the swamp — a dark silhouette against the grey sky, the Gothic spires rising above the cypress canopy, the Spanish moss draped over the steeple. The structure is the GAME'S OBJECTIVE — the thing the player is surviving TOWARD. The structure is the light at the end of the survival — not a reward, but a DESTINATION. The player doesn't "win" the survival game by scoring points. The player wins by ARRIVING.

**Completion reward:** Reaching the colonial structure completes "The Reclamation." The player earns the STONE: **Anthracite** (the highest-grade coal — the metamorphic stone that burns the longest and cleanest, the stone that is the most COMPRESSED form of ancient biological matter — the swamp's ancient vegetation, compressed over millions of years into the hardest coal). Anthracite is the swamp's heart — the geological record of the swamp's own past. The anthracite connects the biological swamp (surface) to the geological swamp (deep time — the coal formed from ancient swamps millions of years ago). The anthracite is the stone that links the present swamp to the ancient swamp to the buried cities: the same biological process that buries the cities (jungle absorption) created the anthracite (ancient vegetation compressed into stone). The stone is the PROOF that the swamp's biological process has been burying things for millions of years.

The swamp has its own Fae — the beings of the wetland, the creatures of the in-between (not land, not water, not air — the swamp is all three at once).

**The Moss-folk:** The Fae of the Spanish moss. Tiny, grey-green, plant-like beings that live IN the moss, the way the Little People live in the Appalachian moss. **The Rougarou's cousin — the Honey Island Swamp Monster:** A less known but locally real cryptid — the Honey Island Swamp Monster, a bipedal creature reported in the Honey Island Swamp since the 1960s. ## Layer Two: The Colonial Gothic Structure

### The Building

A colonial-era CHURCH — specifically, a Spanish colonial mission church built in the Gothic Revival style, the kind of building that the Spanish colonizers built in Louisiana in the late 1700s, with pointed arches, a steep-pitched roof, a bell tower (the bell long gone — the tower empty, the Spanish moss growing inside it), and thick stone walls. The church is HALF-CONSUMED by the swamp — the water has risen to the foundation, the cypress knees have grown UNDER the walls, the Spanish moss hangs through the broken windows. The church is still STANDING — the stone walls are thick, the construction is solid, the colonial builders built for eternity. But the swamp is patient. The swamp will win. The church is the swamp's SLOWEST meal — a structure that will take centuries to fully consume.

The church's interior: the pews are gone (rotted, reclaimed by the water). The altar remains — stone, carved, the colonial mason's work visible in the carvings (Catholic iconography, the colonial aesthetic). The floor is stone, cracked, with water seeping through the cracks. The walls are damp. The air is heavy. The acoustics are EXTRAORDINARY — the stone walls, the empty space, the high ceiling, the absence of furniture — the church is a RESONANCE CHAMBER. Every sound the player makes echoes, sustains, decays slowly. The church is the swamp's MUSIC ROOM — the place where the swamp's frequencies are amplified, where the muted murmur becomes a sustained tone, where the absorbed Song becomes audible.

The church's frequency: the stone walls resonate at the frequency of the buried cities BELOW. The church was built on top of the door, and the door leads to the buried cities, and the buried cities' frequencies LEAK UP through the stone. The church's resonance is not the church's own — it's the buried cities', channeled through the stone, amplified by the architecture. The colonial builders didn't know this. The colonial builders built a church that inadvertently became an amplifier for the civilizations they were trying to erase. The irony is not lost on the Inn.

### The Gothic Element

The Gothic architecture is not decoration — it's FUNCTIONAL. Gothic architecture (pointed arches, ribbed vaults, flying buttresses) was designed to be LIGHT — to allow tall walls of glass, to create vertical space, to lift the worshipper's eyes to heaven. The Gothic church in the swamp does the OPPOSITE — it channels DOWN. The pointed arches direct the frequencies DOWNWARD, into the stone, into the basement, into the door, into the buried cities. The Gothic structure, designed to reach UP to God, has been repurposed by the Inn to reach DOWN to the ancestors. The colonial architecture, built to worship the colonial God, has been inverted by the swamp into a passage to the pre-colonial dead.

The Gothic aesthetic is the colonial layer's visual language — the pointed arches, the tracery, the steep gables, the dark stone. The Gothic is the European aesthetic applied to the swamp, the way colonialism is the European system applied to the indigenous world. The Gothic structure is beautiful — and the beauty is the colonial overlay's most effective tool. The player who enters the church is struck by the beauty, the grandeur, the weight of European architecture. The player who finds the basement door understands: the beauty is the SURFACE. The door is the PURPOSE.

### The Basement

The church has a basement — a stone chamber beneath the altar, accessible through a trapdoor behind the altar (the trapdoor is hidden — the colonial builders sealed it, or the swamp obscured it, or the building's decay revealed it). The basement is DRY — the one dry space in the swamp, the one place the water hasn't reached. The dryness is UNNATURAL — the swamp should have consumed the basement along with everything else. The dryness is the DOOR's effect — the door, below the basement, keeps the water out. The door is not a normal door. The door is a THRESHOLD.

The door: set in the basement floor, a stone slab carved with pre-colonial glyphs — NOT colonial carvings, NOT European script. The glyphs are the indigenous writing of the buried cities — the Maya glyphs, the Khmer script, the writing of the civilizations that the jungle swallowed. The door predates the church. The church was built ON TOP of the door. The colonial builders found the door and built their church over it — either to protect it (some colonial builders recognized the sanctity of the pre-colonial sites) or to suppress it (building a church over a sacred site is the oldest colonial tool of erasure). The Inn doesn't judge which — the Inn presents both possibilities and lets the player decide.

The player opens the door. The door leads DOWN. The stairs are stone, wet (the water returns below the door — the door was the dry threshold, and below it, the water resumes), dark (the player needs the green fire — the swamp gas lantern — to see). The stairs descend for a long time — the buried cities are DEEP, below the swamp, below the colonial structure, below the water table, in the geological layer where the ancient cities were swallowed by the jungle and compressed under millions of years of growth.

## Layer Three: The Buried Cities

### What They Are

The buried cities are the ancient civilizations of the jungles and rainforests — the cities that the jungle SWALLOWED. Not buried by sand (Egypt) or by volcanic ash (Pompeii) or by earthquake (Atlantis). Buried by LIFE. The jungle grew OVER the cities. The trees grew INTO the temples. The roots CRACKED the stones. The vines COVERED the carvings. The moss ATE the surfaces. The jungle didn't destroy the cities — the jungle ABSORBED them. The cities are still there — intact, preserved by the jungle's embrace, the way a body is preserved in a bog. The jungle is the world's most patient archivist — it doesn't bury, it doesn't destroy, it GROWS OVER. The cities are under the jungle the way the Examiner's fragments are under the sand — present, waiting, readable (if you know how to read what the jungle has covered).

The buried cities include:
- **The Maya cities** — Tikal, Palenque, Copán, Caracol, Calakmul. The pyramids under the jungle canopy, the temples with trees growing from their tops, the plazas filled with root systems, the ball courts covered in moss.
- **The Khmer cities** — Angkor Wat, Angkor Thom, Ta Prohm (the temple where the trees have grown INTO the stone — the roots becoming structural, holding the temple together while tearing it apart). Ta Prohm is the buried cities' ICON — the temple where the jungle and the stone have become ONE, where you can't tell where the tree ends and the temple begins.
- **The Amazonian cities** — the recently discovered (LIDAR-revealed) urban complexes beneath the Amazon rainforest. The cities that the European colonizers said didn't exist because they couldn't see them through the canopy. The cities that the jungle hid. The cities that LIDAR found. The Inn's version: the cities that the Moss-folk always knew were there.

### The Aesthetic

The buried cities' visual direction is TEMPLE-RUPTURE — the aesthetic of ancient architecture being simultaneously consumed and held by living jungle. The trees are STRUCTURAL — they've grown into the stone, their roots becoming load-bearing, the trees holding the temples up while the roots crack the stones apart. The buildings are half-alive — the stone is ancient, the trees are young, the combination is neither old nor new but BOTH. The aesthetic is the opposite of the Desert Bone (where erosion reveals the form). Here, GROWTH conceals the form — the jungle covers the cities, and the player must peel back the growth to see the architecture. The buried cities are the anti-Desert Bone — where the Desert Bone teaches that erosion reveals, the buried cities teach that growth conceals. Both are true. Both are the Inn's thesis: the truth is under the surface, whether the surface is sand or jungle.

The Don't Starve aesthetic CONTINUES underground — the hand-drawn dark style persists, but the palette shifts. The swamp's grey-green gives way to the buried cities' deep GREEN — the green of jade, of jungle canopy filtered through stone, of bioluminescent fungi growing on the temple walls. The green fire (the swamp gas lantern) casts a green light on the pre-colonial stone, and the stone is GREEN — the jade green of the Maya, the green of the moss that has been growing on the temples for centuries. The buried cities are the Inn's most GREEN zone — the color of life, of growth, of the jungle that swallowed the cities and is now their guardian.

The buried cities have their OWN frequencies — the frequencies of the pre-colonial civilizations, preserved in the stone the way the Desert Bone's frequencies are preserved in the Egyptian stone. But where the Desert Bone's frequencies are DRY (preserved by aridity, by the absence of water), the buried cities' frequencies are WET (preserved by the jungle's humidity, by the presence of water). The wet preservation is different from the dry preservation: dry preservation keeps the frequency EXACT (the stone doesn't change, the frequency doesn't shift). Wet preservation keeps the frequency ALIVE (the stone changes — the moss grows, the roots shift, the water seeps — and the frequency shifts with the changes). The buried cities' frequencies are LIVING frequencies — they change over time, they grow, they evolve. The buried cities are the Inn's only EVOLVING frequency zone — the frequencies are not fixed (the way the Egyptian frequencies are fixed) but growing (the way the jungle grows).

The buried cities' frequencies connect to:
- The **mycorrhizal network** — the jungle's root system IS the network, and the buried cities are connected to the Inn through the roots that consumed them. The buried cities are the network's DEEPEST nodes — the most ancient, the most buried, the most connected (the roots have been growing through the cities for centuries, the network is densest here).

The basement door is the zone's THESIS made into a physical object: the colonial overlay (the church) was built on top of the pre-colonial truth (the door). The chicken-or-egg question, answered by the door: the pre-colonial civilizations were there first. The colonial overlay came after. The door is the PROOF.
