La Corriveau: Perhaps the most infamous figure in Quebec lore. She was a real 18th-century woman executed for murdering her husbands, whose spirit—and iron cage—became the center of endless ghostly legends. [1, 2, 3, 4]

The Loup-Garou: A uniquely Canadian twist on the werewolf. In Quebec, a man could become a loup-garou if he broke Lent for seven consecutive years. To become human again, someone must draw blood from the beast.

The Gougou: A terrifying giant, woman-shaped monster that was said to inhabit the region of the Gaspé Peninsula and Miscou Island, swallowing ships and people whole.

La Chasse-Galerie (The Flying Canoe): The most beloved of all tales. Desperate to see their sweethearts, isolated fur trappers (voyageurs) make a pact with the Devil to fly their wooden canoe through the night sky. They must avoid uttering the name of God and keep clear of church steeples to avoid crashing.

The Devil at the Dance: A common moral warning. The legend states that at a village dance, a handsome, mysterious stranger would arrive. If a girl danced with him, she would eventually realize he had hooves, and the Devil would claim her soul.

Rose Latulipe: A cautionary tale of a vain young woman who danced with the Devil at a Mardi Gras celebration. Her life was only spared because a quick-thinking priest intervened.

Ti-Jean (Little John): A generic peasant hero based loosely on European fairy tales who uses his cunning and humor to outsmart the Devil, giants, and wild animals.

Joe Mufferaw (Jos Montferrand): A real-life, larger-than-life lumberjack and folk hero known for his incredible strength. He protected French-Canadian loggers and was said to be able to jump over multiple horses.

Les Veillées (The Gatherings): Traditional evening get-togethers where raconteurs (storytellers) would share legends, play traditional fiddle music, and perform step dancing to keep spirits alive during long winters.
