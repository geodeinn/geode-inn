Long before science understood sleep paralysis, Newfoundlanders blamed "The Old Hag". People would wake up feeling a heavy, withered old woman sitting directly on their chests, preventing them from breathing or moving. To protect themselves, locals would tie a red ribbon to their bedposts. [1, 2, 3]

Fairies and the "Good People"

Unlike the whimsical, winged fairies of pop culture, Newfoundland fairies are mischievous and potentially dangerous entities brought over by 17th-century European settlers. They are known to lead people astray in the woods, kidnap children, and steal livestock. To ward off fairies, locals traditionally carried a pocketknife, wore their jackets inside out, or left bread at their doorsteps.

The Bread Pocket: A hollow air pocket or bubble inside a loaf of homemade bread was seen as an open grave, indicating that someone close to the family would soon die.

The Ghost Train: Hearing the whistle of the ghost train on the Avalon Peninsula meant someone you knew would die, while actually seeing it meant your own death.

Cressie: The nickname for a legendary, eel-like lake monster said to inhabit Crescent Lake in Robert's Arm.

The Phantom Trapper: A spirit haunting Labrador who is doomed to drive a team of 14 white huskies through the snow eternally, though he is believed to guide lost travelers to safety.

Tall Tales: The province boasts an extensive history of tall tales, including legends of 800-foot icebergs lingering in St. John’s Harbour for two years.
