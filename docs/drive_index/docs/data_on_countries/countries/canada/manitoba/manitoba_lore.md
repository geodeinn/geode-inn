Manipogo: Said to inhabit the depths of Lake Manitoba, this creature is often described as a 12 to 50-foot serpentine serpent with a horse-like or sheep-like head. Sightings date back to the 1800s, with a famous 1948 report describing a large creature emerging from the water accompanied by a dinosaur-esque cry. The legend is taken so seriously that the area hosts a yearly Manipogo Festival in the community of St. Laurent.

Winnipogo: Its counterpart, spotted in the waters of Lake Winnipegosis, is thought to be an identical or closely related species.

The Origin: In the oral traditions of the Anishinaabe, the creature is known as gichi ginebig ("great serpent").

Perhaps the most fearsome entity in Northern Manitoba Cree mythology is the Wendigo (sometimes spelled Witikow).

The Legend: It is a malevolent, supernatural being cursed by the north wind or spirit of ice with a ceaseless, overwhelming hunger for human flesh.

Description: Possessing superhuman strength, the Wihtikow grows to a gigantic size and has a heart made of ice, making it nearly impossible to kill. These stories served as powerful cultural warnings against isolation, selfishness, and the dangers of starvation in the harsh winter wilderness.

The Rougarou: A shapeshifting creature that takes the form of a black mare, a dog, or a human-horse hybrid. According to lore, if you meet one, you must throw a skeleton key and hit it between the eyes to turn it back into a man.

Little People: Known as Memeguayiwahk (to the Métis) or May-may-quay-so-wuk (to the Cree), these are benign yet mischievous little people who live underground, in rocky areas, and under the water. They are often credited with making the ancient pictographs along the edge of the Canadian Shield.

Legends from Indigenous communities across Manitoba explain the origins of natural phenomena and seasons, relying on deep spiritual connections to the land and sky.

The Northern Lights: The Cree view the aurora borealis as the spirits of their ancestors dancing in the sky, acting as a bridge between the living and the afterlife.

Legends of the Sky & Land: Stories like the journey of Ocek (the fisher) who retrieved summer, and Mista Maskwa (the giant bear), form the core of Indigenous storytelling.
