Mīmīkwīsiwak (Little People): Found in Plains Cree oral tradition, these harmless but mischievous little people occupy coulees and riverbanks. They play tricks on non-believers and are often spotted near waterways. [1]

The Legend of Qu’Appelle: The Qu'Appelle Valley gets its name from a Cree legend. A young brave, returning from a hunt, heard someone calling out to him and replied in French "Qu'Appelle?" (Who calls?). The echo repeated, and upon returning home, he learned his fiancé had died, calling out his name with her last breath.

Métis Folklore

The Roogaroo: A shape-shifting entity rooted in French-Canadian and Métis folklore. Often described as a creature that lurks near shorelines and bushland, it acts as a warning to those who break the strict rules of the community or travel alone.

Fiddling and the Frying-Pan Jig: Métis cultural traditions and celebrations are deeply tied to fiddle music and dancing, with stories of dancers literally outlasting the night at rural house parties.

The Outlaw Caves of Big Muddy: The rugged Big Muddy Badlands in southern Saskatchewan were infamous hideouts for notorious American gangs, including Sam Kelly's gang and Butch Cassidy’s Wild Bunch. The area is riddled with natural caves and escape hatches.

Mock Weddings: A classic Saskatchewan folk drama, mock weddings are parodies in which community members dress up and stage a fake marriage ceremony, historically performed during milestone wedding anniversaries.

Wathen Lake UFOs: Modern folklore in the province includes frequent UFO sightings, such as a widely reported 2006 event where a night watchman at Waterhen Lake watched hovering spaceships land on the ice and zip away.

Scotty the T-Rex: Paleontology meets modern local legend in the Eastend region of the province. Unearthed in 1991, Scotty is the largest, heaviest Tyrannosaurus Rex skeleton ever found in the world.
