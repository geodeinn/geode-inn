Early Estonian mythology was deeply connected to the environment, featuring sacred groves (hiis), springs, and erratics. [1]

Taara: The supreme god of nature, primarily worshipped by the Oeselians (people of Saaremaa).

Tharapita: A warlike god, said to have been born on a mountain in Virumaa in northern Estonia.

Lindu: The daughter of the sky god Uko, who cared for the birds and migrated them; she became the Milky Way.

Kalevipoeg: The national epic, compiled by Friedrich Reinhold Kreutzwald. It follows the giant Kalevipoeg, who performs superhuman feats of strength, battles the devil, and serves as the defender of the land.

Suur Tõll: A giant and legendary protector of the island of Saaremaa, known for defending his people from enemies and even fighting the devil.

Estonian folklore includes a spirit or haldjas for virtually every element of the natural world.

Kratt (or Tulihänd / Pisuhänd): A magical servant made from straw or household tools and brought to life through a drop of the creator’s blood and a deal with the devil. It travels as a fiery serpent or dragon to steal grain and treasures for its master. It must be kept constantly busy, otherwise it will turn on its creator.

Vanapagan: Literally meaning "the old pagan." He is a clever, yet highly gullible and malicious devil figure who is frequently outsmarted by peasants in comic tales.

Näkk: A dangerous shapeshifting water spirit that haunts rivers and lakes, often luring victims into the water with music.

Metsaema and Metsaisa: The forest mother and father who govern the woods and its wildlife.

Estonian folk music and poetry are famous for the regilaul (runic songs). These ancient, monophonic, and unrhymed folk songs were historically chanted by choirs in call-and-response form. They cover every aspect of life—from work and weddings to calendar holidays—and the Estonian folklore archives house over 133,000 recorded songs.
