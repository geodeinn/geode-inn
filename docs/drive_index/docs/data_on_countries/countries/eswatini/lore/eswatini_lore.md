Nkosi (The Creator): The supreme deity believed to be the creator of life, nature, and the universe. [1]

Ancestral Spirits (Emadloti): Central to the belief system is the profound respect for ancestors. They act as intermediaries between the living and Nkosi, watching over their descendants and guiding daily life.

Sacred Waters: Water spirits feature prominently in many regional myths, balancing the life-giving properties of rivers with their dangerous, destructive sides.

The Nyanyabulembu: A legendary monster or ogre that lives in deep, sacred pools. In folklore, a brave youth must hunt it to use its skin for a sacred, ceremonial cloak for the next rightful king.

The Cloud Princess: A fable of the sacred feminine where a luminous princess falls in love with a poor farmer. She generously rains down nourishment and abundance, teaching the lesson that life is sustained by giving regardless of merit.

The Rooster's Kraal: A tale warning against the dangers of greed and wicked sorcery. It tells the story of an evil cannibal king who tricks and transforms an entire opposing kingdom's population into roosters and hens.

Teaching Life Lessons: Traditional fables use talking animals (like the clever hare, Chakijane) to teach children about wit, courage, and avoiding danger.

Addressing Modern Crises: In the modern era, UNICEF and Swazi storytellers have revived and adapted traditional fables to communicate vital contemporary issues like HIV/AIDS and child abuse, helping children learn how to recognize and avoid danger.

Feminist Folktales: Several tales center on themes of escaping domestic abuse, where a wronged mother takes her children into the wilderness, defeats monsters, and uses magical eggs to find safety and found a new kingdom.

Folklore in Eswatini is not just confined to stories; it is actively lived out through major national ceremonies.

Incwala: The supreme Kingship Ceremony (often called the First Fruits Ceremony) timed by the sun and moon. It involves sacred rituals, the fetching of water by the "water people" (bemanti), and the tasting of the new harvest.

Umhlanga (Reed Dance): An annual cultural ceremony where thousands of unmarried women gather reeds to repair the Queen Mother's residence, celebrating womanhood, discipline, and unity.
