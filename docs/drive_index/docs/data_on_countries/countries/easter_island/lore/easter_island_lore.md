The Creation: According to Rapa Nui mythology, the creator god Make-Make (along with the god Haua) fertilized the red clay earth to create the first man. Finding the man lonely, he created the woman from the man's rib. [1]

Hotu Matu'a: The foundational migration epic tells of King Hotu Matu'a, who fled his sinking or embattled homeland of Hiva (often associated with the Marquesas Islands in French Polynesia). Guided by prophetic dreams, he sent seven scouts ahead before arriving with his people in two large canoes at Anakena Beach.

The giant statues, which were carved to embody the spiritual power or mana of important ancestors, are central to the island's folklore:

Walking Statues: According to oral tradition, the massive stone figures were not pushed or rolled; instead, the Moai "walked" to their ceremonial platforms (ahu) across the island using the power (mana) of high-ranking priests and divine words.

The Story of the Woman: One local legend recounts a time when a powerful female king had the Moai constructed. When the people rebelled and refused to provide the carvers with food, she commanded the statues to stand up and walk to the tops of the hills, where they remain today.

Another popular narrative in Rapa Nui folklore describes two distinct groups of people who inhabited the island: the Hanau Epe (Long-Ears) and the Hanau Momoko (Short-Ears).

According to the legends, the Long-Ears were the master stone carvers and attempted to enslave the Short-Ears.

This conflict culminated in a legendary war where the Short-Ears trapped the Long-Ears in a large ditch dug along the Poike peninsula. The Short-Ears set the ditch on fire, nearly wiping out the Long-Ears clan entirely.

The Birdman Cult

As deforestation, famine, and resource wars led to the collapse of the traditional ruling class, the worship of the Moai gave way to a new religious and political order:

The Tangata Manu: In the later centuries, a fierce competition called the Birdman Cult (Tangata Manu) arose in honor of the god Make-Make.

The Competition: Representatives from rival clans would scale the sheer, shark-infested cliffs of the Rano Kau volcano and swim across dangerous open waters to the rocky islet of Motu Nui. They would wait for the migratory sooty tern to lay the first egg of the season.

Victory: The first man to secure the egg and bring it back to the cliff-top village of Orongo became the sacred "Birdman" for the year, granting his clan ultimate power, food rights, and immense prestige.
