King Solomon's Ring: Folklore states that Mount Karthala, the massive volcano on Grande Comore, was created when King Solomon chased by jinn dropped his enchanted ring. The ring supposedly sank into the ocean and was swallowed by a fish. [1]

The Flooded Village of Lac Salé: In the north of Grande Comore lies Lac Salé, a saltwater lake in an old volcanic crater. Legend says the lake was formed when an entire village was flooded with lava because the locals refused to give water to a holy sharif.

Winged Serpents: The striking basalt rock formation Dos du Dragon (Dragon's Back) on Grande Comore is tied to myths of scaled, winged monsters that guarded the early waters of the Indian Ocean.

The Magic of the Sea: Tales of cunning fishermen being lured by mermaid-like sirens or interacting with magical sea spirits are frequently used in Comorian literature to explain the perils and bounties of their island geography.

Mtswa-Mwindza: According to legend, the people of the islands dispatched an emissary named Mtswa-Mwindza to Mecca in 632 upon hearing of Islam. Although the Prophet Muhammad had passed away by the time the emissary arrived, Mtswa-Mwindza returned to Grande Comore to build a mosque in Ntsaweni and lead the islanders to Islam.

The Portuguese Presence: Because the Portuguese were the earliest Europeans to dominate the Indian Ocean, they hold a special place in Comorian historical myth. Some oral histories trace local genealogies back to early Portuguese castaways or view them as legendary founding figures.

Comorian folklore is heavily characterized by "trickster" themes, where humble underdogs, cunning boys, and clever animals outwit fearsome witches, arrogant devils, or greedy rulers. These stories are often passed down via oral traditions and re-enacted during communal dances, such as the deba and mbiwi, which celebrate ancestral heritage and local lore.
