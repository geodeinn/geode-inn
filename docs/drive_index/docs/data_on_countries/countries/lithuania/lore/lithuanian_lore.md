Eglė the Queen of Serpents: The most famous Lithuanian fairy tale. A mortal woman, Eglė, marries the Serpent King. After a tragic betrayal, her family is cursed, and she and her children are transformed into different trees (the spruce, oak, ash, birch, and aspen).

Jūratė and Kastytis: The legendary tale of an underwater sea goddess (Jūratė) who falls in love with a mortal fisherman (Kastytis). Angered by the union, the thunder god Perkūnas destroys her amber palace, and the sea glass that washes ashore on the Baltic coast is said to be the palace's remains.

The Iron Wolf: The myth behind the founding of the capital city, Vilnius. Grand Duke Gediminas dreamed of a giant iron wolf howling on a hill, which a pagan priest interpreted as a prophecy of a great and invincible city.

Perkūnas: The supreme god of thunder and war, similar to the Norse Thor.

Saulė: The sun goddess and protector of orphans.

Laima: The goddess of fate and fortune, who often determines a person's destiny at birth.

Gabija: The revered goddess of the home and the hearth fire.

Žemyna: The earth goddess who represented fertility.

Žaltys: Sacred grass snakes kept in the home as a blessing of health and prosperity.

Kaukai: Tiny, helpful house spirits that bring wealth and ensure that household provisions last longer.

Velniai (Velnias): The ancient gods of the underworld and chaos. Unlike the Christian Devil, the traditional Lithuanian Velnias is often depicted as a mischievous, sometimes foolish, trickster rather than pure evil.

Maumai and Baubai: Bogies and monsters that lurk in the dark (under wells or in dark rooms) and were traditionally used to frighten misbehaving children.
