Shandong is the legendary cradle of Chinese civilization, home to the Yellow River, Mount Tai, and the origins of Confucianism. Its rich folklore features creation myths, tales of outlaws, and sea deities that continue to shape regional culture and traditional arts. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

1. Mythological Origins & The Sacred

Shandong is historically associated with the Dongyi people and foundational deities:

Shennong (The Divine Farmer): Celebrated in carvings in Jiaxiang County, this legendary emperor is credited with teaching agriculture, inventing the plow, and tasting hundreds of herbs to discover traditional medicine.

Mount Tai: The most sacred mountain in Taoism, serving as a mythological bridge between earth and the heavens.

3. Bald-Tailed Old Li

Originating in the Jiaodong peninsula, this is one of Shandong's most celebrated regional legends. It tells the story of a black dragon with a severed tail who became a rain-bringer. This folklore reflects the historical "Migration to Northeast China" from Shandong, using the dragon's journey to symbolize the migrants' longing for their homeland. [1]

4. Water Margin (Shui Hu Zhuan)

One of China's Four Great Classical Novels, Water Margin is deeply rooted in Shandong's folk traditions. It romanticizes a band of 108 outlaws who gathered at Mount Liang (in Liangshan County) during the Song Dynasty to fight corrupt officials. It established archetypes of heroism and brotherhood in Chinese storytelling.

5. Han Ba (The Drought Demon)

A terrifying Shandong folk monster associated with dryness and stiff corpses (Jiangshi). Historically, when villages suffered severe droughts, locals would search for moist graves to dig up, beat, and burn these entities as part of indigenous rainmaking customs.
