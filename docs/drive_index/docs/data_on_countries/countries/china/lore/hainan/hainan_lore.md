The Li Mother Spirit: According to ancient oral traditions, the Li people (Hainan’s earliest inhabitants) originated from a magical encounter between a Neolithic hunter and a coconut or a goddess, reflecting their deep reverence for nature. [1, 2, 3]

The Firewood-Jumping Dance: Li folklore states that when ancient ancestors were building thatched huts, the bamboo poles kept sliding off the roof. To keep the poles in place without getting their feet stabbed, people had to constantly jump. This amusing situation evolved into the iconic bamboo stick dance.

Boat-Shaped Houses: To honor their seafaring ancestors who crossed the sea, the Li people build their traditional houses in the shape of boat canopies. This circular arch design deflects powerful typhoons, while elevated floors protect against moisture and jungle "miasma".

Sanyuesan Festival: Known as the Double Third Festival (held on the 3rd day of the 3rd lunar month). For the Han Chinese, it is an ancestor-worship event, but for the Li and Miao, it is a massive island-wide day of love, featuring campfire singing, courtship, and bamboo dancing.

108 Brothers & Sea Gods: Hainan fishermen historically navigated the dangerous South China Sea and developed a distinct marine folklore. Joint worship of gods is prominent, with the worship of the 108 Brothers—often believed to be righteous fishermen lost at sea—being the most important aspect of their sacrificial ceremonies.

Junpo Festival: Celebrated widely, particularly in regions like Hainan's northern rural areas, this indigenous festival blends Taoist rituals with village parades, where performers engage in spirit mediumship, fire-walking, and exorcisms to cleanse the community of bad luck.

Temple of the Five Lords (Wugong Temple): Located in the capital city of Haikou, this temple honors five prominent imperial officials who were banished to Hainan during the Tang and Song Dynasties. These exiled scholars brought mainland culture and folklore to the island.

Gibbon Taboos: The deep emerald rainforests of Hainan—home to the highly endangered Hainan gibbon. Local traditional forest lore includes cautionary tales of hunters who kill gibbons only to suffer sudden illness or blindness, ensuring a cultural respect for local wildlife.
