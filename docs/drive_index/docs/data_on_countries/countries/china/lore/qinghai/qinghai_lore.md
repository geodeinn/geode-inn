Qinghai Lake (Kokonor): Considered one of the most holy lakes in Tibetan Buddhism. Devotees perform a clockwise pilgrimage around the lake, tossing ritual items like jewelry, khata (silk scarves), and grains into the waters to bless their families. [1, 2, 3, 4]

The Four Dragon Kings: In Chinese mythology, Qinghai Lake is historically revered as the residence of the Dragon King of the West Sea, who rules over rain and local waters.

Kunlun Mountains: Qinghai's Kunlun range is the setting for the foundational Kunlun myth system. These myths are deeply intertwined with the prehistoric painted pottery of the Majiayao culture, creating a rich visual and spoken narrative of ancient spiritual civilization.

Queen Mother of the West (Xiwangmu): Associated with the Kunlun Mountains, she is a central mythological figure in Chinese lore who holds the secrets to immortality and the elixir of life.

Princess Wencheng & the Daotang River: A famous legend tells of Princess Wencheng of the Tang Dynasty travelling through Qinghai to marry King Songtsen Gampo of Tibet. Overwhelmed with homesickness near Riyue Mount, her tears supposedly flowed backward, forming the Daotang (Reversed-Flow) River.

The White Camel Migration: The Salar people, a Muslim ethnic group in Qinghai, hold a migration legend. Folklore claims their ancestors followed a white camel from Central Asia to Xunhua 800 years ago, carrying one of the world's oldest handwritten Qurans.

Hua'er Songs: A highly famous style of unaccompanied, improvisational folk music shared by several ethnic groups in the region. It usually features competitive, topical lyrics often centering around courtship and daily life.

Guozhuang Dance: A traditional circle dance common to Tibetan communities. In these ceremonies, dancers imitate the soaring and hopping of eagles, moving rapidly to celebrate or honor religious figures.

Cat Demons (Maoguishen): In Tu (Monguor) folklore, there are tales of the maoguishen (cat spirit). Historically, these spirits were sometimes believed to bring wealth to a devotee's household—but would require a shaman's exorcism ritual if they caused mysterious misfortunes.
