The Mossi (or Moose) people of the central Moogo region heavily feature animals to teach vital lessons.

The Hare and Hyena: Mba-Soamba (the hare) continually outwits Mba-Katre (the hyena) by using wit and intellect, teaching children that cleverness triumphs over brute strength or gluttony.

The Secret of the Moon's Light: A celebrated folktale tells of a brave girl named Kadi who journeys into the dark to confront a spirit named Nyamba. By showing wisdom, she secures the Moon's silver light to protect her village forever, emphasizing the power of kindness and courage.

The Bagre Myth: The Dagaaba and Lobi peoples pass down the Bagre, an epic oral tradition recited during months-long initiation ceremonies. It details how God created humanity using soil, okra, water, and the semen of flies and cats. It dictates how humans must negotiate sustainable, respectful relationships with the spirit and natural realms.

Traditional folklore in Burkina Faso is deeply animistic. Masks are treated not as costumes, but as living supernatural entities that bridge the gap between the earth and the realm beyond.

Animal Spirits: Warthog masks represent the "dance of happiness," bird heads are used in calls for rain, and owl masks act to protect the community.

Supernatural Beings: French photographer Jean-Claude Moschetti extensively documented the magical world and "spirit travellers" of Burkina Faso, detailing how these entities emerge during harvests, rites of passage, and funerals.

The Kassena people of the southern village of Tiébélé express their folklore directly on their homes. Women paint the mud walls with distinct, natural symbols representing their values and myths:

Red: Signifies courage.

White: Represents honesty and purity.

Black: Symbolizes the unseen world of the night and spirits.

Symbols: Snakes and crocodiles are painted on the walls to ward off illness, while arrows mark the homes of warriors.
