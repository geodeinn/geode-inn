Zanahary and Ratovantany: In traditional creation myths, Zanahary created the souls of humans, while Ratovantany (an earth deity) molded human bodies from clay. When humans die, their souls return to the heavens, and their bodies return to the earth.

The Ibonia Cycle: This is the most famous epic poem in Madagascar. It tells the tale of Ibonia, a superhuman hero who is born already speaking and holding an axe. He must battle the wicked stone monster Ramamba and complete impossible trials to claim his destined bride, Volanoro.

Rapeto: A massive, landscape-shaping giant in Malagasy legend. Folklore states that when a peasant once tried to chop down a tree on his leg, Rapeto thought it was just a mosquito bite. The giant bled, and his blood formed Madagascar's fertile rivers.

Kalanoro: A famous humanoid cryptid said to live in the deep rainforests. Described as short, hairy, and having fangs, they have backward-facing feet and glowing red eyes. They can be malevolent child-snatchers or helpful guardians, depending on how humans treat the forest.

Tokandia (Tokantongotra): A legendary nocturnal, man-eating predator. Its most bizarre feature is that it only has two legs—one in the front and one in the back—yet it was said to be incredibly fast.

Lalomena: A colossal, bright-red, horned water-beast. Every step it takes causes earth-shaking tremors, and its whistle is said to conjure storm clouds. Paleontologists suggest this myth is a cultural memory of the extinct Malagasy pygmy hippopotamus (Hippopotamus lemerlei).

Songòmby: A patient, ox-sized, carnivorous beast with flaring nostrils and floppy ears that ambushes its prey from the base of trees.

The Vazimba: Believed to be the primordial, ancient inhabitants of Madagascar. They are now venerated as forest and water spirits.

Tompondrano: A water snake blessed by the Vazimba that is known to be an excellent swimmer.

Fady (Cultural Taboos): Folklore heavily dictates the relationship with nature. Entire species of animals, specific forests, or certain lakes are protected by fady, which act as traditional environmental laws.

Baobab Trees: Known locally as reniala (mother of the forest), these ancient giants are believed to house the spirits of the ancestors that watch over the land and its people.
