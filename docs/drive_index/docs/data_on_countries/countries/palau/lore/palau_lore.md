The Legend of Uab: A voracious, rapidly growing boy/giant consumed all the food in his village on Angaur, threatening to sink the land. The villagers trapped and burned him; as he thrashed and fell, his body parts formed the islands of Palau (his torso became Babeldaob and his feet/toes became the Rock Islands). [1, 2, 3, 4]

The Legend of Milad: Following a great flood sent by the gods, the goddess Milad (or Iluochel) emerged to restart human life. She gave birth to four children (three sons and one daughter) who became the cornerstones and namesakes of key ancestral villages and regions in Palau.

The Legend of Dilukai: After a disgraced woman named Dilukai faced shame, village carvers placed a striking wooden sculpture of her with exposed genitals above the entry beam of the traditional bai (meeting house) to serve as a moral warning and protective fertility guardian for women.

Meduu Ribtal (The Breadfruit Goddess): A celestial being who descended to teach early Palauan women the sustainable cultivation of taro and breadfruit, emphasizing community generosity and proper care for the earth.

The Monster of Chelechui: A terrifying giant serpent defeated not by physical force, but by a wise young man named Dirrab who tricked the beast into swallowing heated stones wrapped in leaves.

Traditional Palauan lore functions as sophisticated ecological management. Rules derived from custom (bul) dictate speaking softly near freshwater springs to avoid disturbing spirits, waiting after eating greasy foods before entering the ocean, and sparing female or young animals during hunts to preserve nature's balance.
