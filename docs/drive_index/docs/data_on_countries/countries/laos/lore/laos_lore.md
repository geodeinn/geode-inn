The Great Gourds: The primary Laotian creation myth tells of two giant gourds growing at Meuang Thaeng (Dien Bien Phu). Rulers pierced a gourd with a hot iron, and out poured the Lao Thoeng (the indigenous highlanders). [1]

The Phra Lak Phra Ram: A deeply revered Laotian adaptation of the Ramayana. It roots the history and land of the region into Hindu-Buddhist cosmology, mapping regional geography to the heroic adventures of Phra Ram.

Naga (Nak): These benevolent, serpent-like dragons dwell in the Mekong River. They are revered as the bringers of fertility, rain, and wisdom.

Phi Pob: A notorious malevolent spirit that possesses humans and devours their souls or organs from the inside out.

Kongkoi: A mischievous, one-legged jungle spirit.

Phi Tai Hong: The restless, angry ghost of a woman who dies during childbirth.

Xiangmiang: He is Laos' most famous trickster. Though born a peasant, his sharp wit and clever riddles constantly outsmart the king, nobles, and even the snail. [1, 2]

The Clever Hare: Many animal fables feature a brilliant hare who easily outwits larger, predatory beasts. In one popular tale, the hare tricks Uncle Elephant and Uncle Catfish into a tug-of-war against each other.

The Twelve Sisters (Thao Chet Hai): A classic epic of a poor woodcutter who abandons his twelve daughters in the forest. They are captured by an ogress, escape, and face deep betrayals. The story is a staple in moral storytelling, highlighting bravery and filial piety.

Phra Mae Thorani: The Earth Goddess. She is prominently featured in Lao folklore and temple reliefs, famously wringing an ocean of water from her long hair to drown the army of Mara (the tempter of Buddha).
