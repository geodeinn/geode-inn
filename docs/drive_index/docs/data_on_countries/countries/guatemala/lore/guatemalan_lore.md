La Llorona: "The Weeping Woman" is a tragic spirit doomed to wander rivers and bodies of water at night, endlessly weeping and searching for the children she drowned.

El Sombrerón: A tiny, mysterious man dressed in black with a huge hat and heeled boots. He rides into farms to braid the manes of horses and enchants young women with his beautiful singing voice, eventually causing them to die of infatuation.

La Siguanaba: A beautiful seductress who haunts ravines and isolated rivers. She lures wandering, unfaithful men deep into the wilderness before turning around to reveal a horrifying horse-like or skull face.

Deeply rooted in indigenous communities (especially around Lake Atitlán), ancient traditions and shapeshifting tales are still actively practiced and spoken about.

Los Nahuales: Belief that every human has an animal counterpart, spirit, or totem. Shamans (or curanderos) frequently communicate with a person's nahual to heal illnesses or solve mysteries.

El Cadejo: Two supernatural dogs that roam the night. A benign white dog protects women and children, while a malevolent black dog watches over drunks, keeping them safe on their walk home but leading them down a dark path.

Los Aluxes: Mischievous, invisible forest dwarfs who are guardians of the land. Farmers often build small houses for them in exchange for protecting their crops.

Maximon (or San Simón): A famous and controversial folk saint worshiped heavily in Santiago Atitlán. He represents a syncretism of Maya gods, Spanish conquerors, and Catholic saints. Devotees offer him cigars, alcohol, and money in exchange for protection, good luck, and love.
