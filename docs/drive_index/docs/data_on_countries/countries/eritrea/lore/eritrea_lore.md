Eritrean mythology is populated by a rich variety of spirits, fairies, and omens passed down by elders:

Deki Hidrtna: Benevolent female fairies or woodland spirits said to reside in rivers, canyons, and deep forests. They are known to play music, share food, and occasionally guide humans.

Buda: The pervasive belief in the "evil eye". In traditional folklore, this power is often associated with metalworkers or those from different social groups, and it is warded off through specific blessings and rituals.

Zar Spirits: Unseen, spiritual entities that can interact with or possess humans, which are treated through traditional healing customs and holy water ceremonies.

Storytelling traditionally takes place around a fire to pass on wisdom and cultural values. Common tales include:

Trickster Tales: Tales featuring clever figures like Abunawas (a coastal trickster), whose stories are used to teach lessons about clever justice, wit, and fairness over rigid rule following.

Animal Fables: Stories featuring hyenas, leopards, and foxes are used to pass down life lessons. For example, fables about leopards attacking weaker prey serve as metaphors for the cycle of revenge and the need for careful observation.

Everyday life and social customs are heavily guided by inherited beliefs:

The Coffee Ceremony: A deeply ingrained social and spiritual ritual. The burning of incense rocks during this ceremony acts as a physical and spiritual method to repel bad spirits.

Counting Households: Traditional village elders often avoid counting the exact number of members in their household. It is superstitiously believed that if the devil learns the exact count, he might reduce the number.

Wedding Traditions: Dancing is viewed as a language of emotion. Guests show appreciation and offer financial support to the newlyweds by sticking money to the foreheads or shirts of dancers.
