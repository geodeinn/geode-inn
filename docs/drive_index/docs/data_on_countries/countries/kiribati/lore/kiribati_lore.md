Na Areau (The Spider God): The ultimate creator. In ancient times, he wove the cosmos from a vast, endless space, bringing the first sky and earth into being.

The Tree of Life: Te Kaintikuaba is the sacred tree from which the first spirits originated in Samoa before migrating north to populate the coral atolls.

Separating the Earth and Sky: To make room for the world to grow, Na Areau had Riiki (the Eel) stretch himself between the darkness of the earth and the heavens to push the sky upwards.

Nei Tituaabine: A beautiful red-haired woman and tree goddess. When she passed away, three sacred trees grew from her resting place: a coconut from her head, a pandanus from her heels, and an almond from her navel.

Auriaria: A red-haired giant demigod who fell in love with Nei Tituaabine, representing the divine connection between the people, the land, and the sea.

The Singing Fish: Folktales often highlight the harmony between humans and the ocean. Legends like the Singing Fish teach I-Kiribati children about environmental respect, gratitude, and the consequences of human greed.

Trickster Magic: Stories often revolve around clever underdogs—such as the "brave little tailor" archetype—who use trickery and resourcefulness to outsmart man-eating witches (hag), monsters, or greedy giants.

Kinship and Survival: Many tales focus on siblings and families relying on cooperation and ingenuity to endure harsh environments and threatening entities.

The Maneaba: Much of this folklore was passed down orally under the roof of the maneaba (traditional meeting house) by village elders (Unim'ane).

Dance (Te Kaimatoa & Te Buki): Traditional dances mimic the movements of the frigate bird and ocean waves. The bird itself serves as a central symbol in many coastal tales, acting as a divine navigator and a sign of peace.
