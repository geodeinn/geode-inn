Astarte & Adonis: A famous Phoenician love myth. Adonis, a handsome mortal hunter, was fatally wounded by a boar. The goddess of love, Astarte (Aphrodite), mourned his death, and the blood-red river, Nahr Ibrahim (the Adonis River), is said to flow from his wounds every spring.

Jinn & Ghouls: Folk beliefs frequently feature Jinn (shapeshifting spirits) residing in abandoned places, like the famously eerie village of Wata Sfarta. Ghouls (flesh-eating creatures) haunt graveyards and isolated areas.

Abu Keis (Father of the Bag): A spooky figure who wandered the streets with a large sack, snatching up children who stayed outside too late.

Em R3di (Mother of Thunder): A terrifying character used to scare kids into coming indoors during severe thunderstorms.

Monsieur Sale: A bogeyman figure used to warn children about the dangers of being unhygienic or playing in the dirt.

The Hakawati: Traditional storytellers who used to entertain crowds in local cafes with epics like 1001 Nights, typically keeping listeners hooked with dramatic cliffhangers.

Dabke: The legendary traditional line dance performed at weddings and community gatherings. It is accompanied by folk instruments like the oud (lute) and mijwiz (wind instrument).

Folklore Attire: While almost everyone wears modern clothing today, traditional costumes include the Sherwal (baggy pants for men) and the historical Tantour (a tall, cone-shaped headdress historically worn by women).

Pearls on a Branch: A prominent collection of 100 translated Arabic folktales collected from elderly female storytellers across Lebanon.

Proverbs: Traditional Lebanese sayings are central to the culture. They are usually satirical or parodic and provide insight into the historic social and patriarchal structures of the country.
