ranian folklore and mythology is a rich tapestry of epic heroism, cosmic battles between light and darkness, and chilling supernatural creatures passed down through thousands of years. Much of these traditional tales originate from the Zoroastrian holy book, the Avesta, and are immortalized in the Shahnameh (Book of Kings). [1, 2, 3]

At the heart of Iranian mythology is the eternal battle between Ahura Mazda (the Wise Lord of creation, light, and truth) and Angra Mainyu/Ahriman (the destructive spirit of darkness and evil). Other major deities include Mithra (god of the sun, truth, and covenants) and Anahita (goddess of water and fertility).

The Shahnameh blends history and myth, following the lives of Persia's greatest warriors and kings:

Rostam: The ultimate Persian champion. He rides the magical, intelligent horse Rakhsh and is aided by the Simurgh, a benevolent, wise bird resembling a phoenix with the head of a dog and the claws of a lion.

Zahhak: A tyrannical king with two snake-shoulders that sprouted after being kissed by the devil, requiring him to feed human brains to the serpents every day.

Kaveh the Blacksmith: A legendary figure who led an uprising against Zahhak using his blacksmith's apron as a revolutionary flag (the Derafsh Kaviani).

Mythical Beasts and Spirits

Iranian folklore is populated by a wide array of creatures, fairies, and monsters: [1]

Deevs: Malevolent giants or demons, often depicted with horns and massive strength, who serve as the primary antagonists in heroic tales.

Aal: A terrifying demon resembling a scrawny old woman with long, iron-like claws and fire in her eyes. Folklore warns that she preys on pregnant women and newborns.

Pari: Beautiful, winged, fairy-like spirits that act as guides or helpers to humans, occupying a middle ground between angels and demons.

Sang-e Sabur (The Patient Stone): A magical, empathetic stone that listens to the deepest sorrows and secrets of the person holding it. It is believed to absorb the trauma until it eventually bursts into pieces from the weight of grief.

The Fortune Teller of Old: Folktales often feature cunning tricksters, wise old women (such as Pirezan), and genies (Jinn) that interact with mortals in the desert or remote mountain villages.
