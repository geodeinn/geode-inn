Maine folklore blends Native American traditions, colonial maritime myths, and 19th-century logging camp lore. It features cryptids like the kangaroo-like Billdad and the ape-like Agropelter, legends of the Saco River curse, and Indigenous tales of the giant Glooskap and mountaintop spirit Pamola.

The state's mythological landscape breaks down into a few distinct traditions:

1. Wabanaki & Indigenous Legends

Glooskap: A benevolent creator and warrior who shaped the mountains, islands, and rivers.

Pamola: A fierce, winged, night-dwelling creature that guards Mount Katahdin. He is feared and respected, with legends warning against disrespecting his mountain.

Chenoo: A terrifying, cannibalistic monster with a heart of ice, born of human greed or evil.

2. Lumberjack "Fearsome Critters"

The Agropelter: A wiry, monkey-like ape found in the deep north woods that hurls heavy branches and dead limbs with deadly precision.

The Tote Road Shagamaw: A bizarre beast that is half-bear, half-moose, known to switch between walking on its front and hind legs.

Paul Bunyan: The legendary giant lumberjack is widely celebrated in Bangor, Maine, where giant storks purportedly delivered him as a baby, and his bootprints are said to have created Maine's vast lakes.

3. Curses & Coastal Ghosts

The isolated rocky coastlines and swift inland waters harbor numerous local ghost stories and curses:

The Saco River Curse: A prevalent legend claims the Saco River annually claims the lives of three people (historically, three white settlers) due to a curse placed by Chief Squando following the tragic drowning of his infant son by early colonists.

Ram Island Woman: A ghostly "Woman in White" is rumored to haunt the lighthouse and shores of Ram Island in Casco Bay.

The Ghost of Colonel Buck: Located in Bucksport, the grave of Colonel Jonathan Buck features a strange, permanent leg-shaped stain on the granite monument that locals say appeared there as a curse for executing an accused witch.
