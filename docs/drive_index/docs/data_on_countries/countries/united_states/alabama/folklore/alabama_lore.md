Alabama’s folklore blends Native American myths, Civil War history, and ghostly legends. Notable tales include Railroad Bill (an outlaw folk hero), the Alabama White Thang (a hairy beast), Dead Children’s Playground (a famously haunted Huntsville cemetery), and the Boyington Oak in Mobile, which grew from the grave of an wrongfully executed man. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

Top Alabama Legends and Lore

Railroad Bill: An African American outlaw in the late 19th century who terrorized the L&N Railroad in Atmore, Alabama. He was a Robin Hood figure who stole goods and gave them to the poor before being killed in 1896. [1]

The White Thang: A legendary, apox-like cryptid said to roam the rural woods of northern Alabama and the rural areas near Evergreen (known as the Bigfoot Capital of the state). It is described as a tall, white, hairy creature with glowing red eyes. [1]

The Boyington Oak: In Mobile, Charles Boyington was executed for murder in 1834. He proclaimed his innocence and insisted an oak tree would grow on his grave as proof. Today, a massive oak tree stands directly over his resting spot in the Potter's Field section of Church Street Graveyard. [1, 2, 3]

Dead Children's Playground: Located just outside Huntsville's historic Maple Hill Cemetery, this playground is rumored to be where the spirits of departed children come to play at night, with locals often reporting swings moving on their own. [1, 2]

Atipa-Tcoba (Big Man-Eater): Rooted in deep Indigenous lore, this is a giant, cannibalistic monster from Muskogean and Creek (Alabama) traditions. The legends often describe it as an elephant-like giant, potentially tied to ancient mammoth fossils discovered in the region. [1]
