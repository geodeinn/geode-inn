1. Famous Cryptids & Monsters

The Wampus Cat: A terrifying Appalachian creature—half-dog, half-cat, with glowing yellow eyes—said to roam the eastern hills and sometimes stalk University of Tennessee campuses.

The Beast of the Land Between the Lakes: A Bigfoot- or "dog man"-style creature reported in the dense woodlands along the Tennessee/Kentucky border. [1]

The White Bluff Screamer: A cryptid originating from a 19th-century circus train derailment in Dickson County and Montgomery Bell State Park, known for an unearthly scream and terrorizing livestock.

2. Historical & Hauntings

The Bell Witch (Robertson County): One of America's most famous poltergeists, which tormented the John Bell family from 1817 to 1821 and supposedly poisoned the patriarch.

Old Green Eyes (Chickamauga): A spectral figure with glowing green eyes and missing hair, believed to roam the Chickamauga & Chattanooga National Military Park in Walker and Catoosa counties.

The Ghost Bride of Rotherwood (Kingsport): The tragic tale of Rowena, a young bride haunting the Rotherwood Mansion estate who threw herself into the Holston River.

3. Native American & Ancient Legends

The Tlanuwa (Blount County): Giant, mythic birds said to swoop down from Citco Creek cliffs on the Little Tennessee River to carry off dogs and small children.

Tennessee Pygmies: 19th-century folklore stemming from the discovery of small, ancient stone graves across Middle Tennessee, leading to tall tales about a lost race of miniature people.

Tsul ’Kalu: A giant, hairy Cherokee figure who roamed the Appalachian Mountains long before modern "Bigfoot" lore.

4. Folk Beliefs & Superstitions

Rural Tennessee holds onto generations of weather lore and everyday superstitions, such as believing that sleeping in moonlight causes insanity or carrying a pocket acorn prevents aging. It's also steeped in Appalachian "winters" terminology—classifying late cold snaps after spring has started by the blooming plant, like Dogwood Winter or Blackberry Winter.
