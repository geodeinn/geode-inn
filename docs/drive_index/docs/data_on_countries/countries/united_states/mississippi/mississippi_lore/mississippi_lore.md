The Yazoo Witch (Yazoo City)

One of the most enduring ghost legends in the state centers on a "witch" who lived along the Yazoo River. According to legend, a vengeful old woman who lured fishermen to their doom was cornered in 1884 by the local sheriff. Before sinking into the quicksand and dying, she supposedly cursed the town, warning she would return in 20 years. Her grave in Glenwood Cemetery is infamous for its heavy, broken iron chains, which locals claim hold her spirit in check. Exactly twenty years later, in 1904, a massive, mysterious fire swept through Yazoo City, destroying over 300 buildings.

The Devil at the Crossroads (Clarksdale)

Mississippi is widely celebrated as the birthplace of the Delta Blues, and with it comes one of the most famous musical urban legends in the United States. Legend holds that blues pioneer Robert Johnson took his guitar to a desolate crossroads in Clarksdale, Mississippi, at midnight and sold his soul to the devil in exchange for unprecedented musical talent. This supernatural tale has become a massive cornerstone of both American folklore and blues history.

The Witch's Dance (Natchez Trace)

Located south of Tupelo off the Natchez Trace Parkway, the "Witch's Dance" is a wooded area that local folklore claims was a ceremonial gathering place for witches in the late 1700s. Legends suggested that the ground where the witches danced turned black, and nothing would ever grow there. The site's ominous reputation was even documented by Andrew Jackson in his journal, who noted the cursed, barren soil.

Haunted Locations & Ghosts

King's Tavern (Natchez): Dating back to 1789, this structure is the oldest standing building in Natchez. Folklore claims it is haunted by Meline, a mistress of the tavern's original owner who was reportedly murdered and walled up in the tavern's cellar, as well as by the notorious, red-haired Harp brothers, who were early 19th-century bandits. McRaven House (Vicksburg): Known as "Mississippi's Most Haunted House," the grounds served as a Confederate campsite and field hospital. At least eleven Confederate soldiers are reportedly buried on the property, and the ghost of Mary Elizabeth Howard—who died in the home in 1836—is said to be highly active.

Cryptids and River Myths

The Three-Legged Woman of Nash Road (Columbus): Legend states that if you drive down Nash Road at night and turn off your car, a phantom three-legged woman will appear.

The Pascagoula River Mermaid: Along the Gulf Coast, the Pascagoula River—also known as the "Singing River"—is famous for a Native American legend about a group of local Pascagoula tribes who chose to march into the river and drown themselves singing, rather than face enslavement by a hostile tribe. Some variations of the lore include sightings of a siren or mermaid figure.
