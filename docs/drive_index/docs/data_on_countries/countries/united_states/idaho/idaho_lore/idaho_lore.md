Idaho folklore blends deep Indigenous oral traditions with rowdy pioneer tales of the frontier, mining booms, and the untamed wilderness. It is rich with cryptids like the "Sharlie" lake monster in McCall, cannibal dwarves in the Owyhee Mountains, the sorrowful Water Babies, and humorous tall tales of jackalopes.

Wilderness Cryptids & Spirits

Sharlie (The Payette Lake Monster)

Residing in the deep, icy waters of Payette Lake in McCall, Sharlie is Idaho’s most famous lake monster. First reported by loggers and Indigenous tribes in the early 1920s, the creature is frequently described as a 35-to-60-foot serpent with multiple humps. Sightings skyrocketed in the 1940s, and today, she serves as more of a beloved local mascot than a fearful beast.

The Bear Lake Monster

Straddling the Idaho-Utah border, Bear Lake is the supposed home of an aquatic cryptid dating back to the 1800s. Originally featured in early Mormon pioneer accounts, the monster was described as serpent-like, swimming incredibly fast, and occasionally snatching people from the water's edge.

The Owyhee Cannibal Dwarves

According to local Indigenous (Shoshone) legends, tiny, twisted creatures only a couple of feet tall once roamed the Owyhee Mountains. Known as the Nimarigar, these beings were said to have long tails, sharp teeth, and a penchant for eating children and unsuspecting travelers.

Tragedy & The Supernatural

Water Babies of the Bear River

Perhaps one of the most haunting and tragic pieces of Idaho folklore centers on the Great Basin tribes' tales of Water Babies. The legend tells of a time of severe starvation, during which some mothers sorrowfully placed their babies in the river to spare them a lifetime of hardship. According to the oral tradition, the spirits of these children never left the water. Campers near the river and the Great Salt Lake often report hearing the faint sounds of crying or giggling on quiet, breezy nights.

Frontier & Mining Lore

The Legend of the Iron Door

One of Idaho’s most famous lost treasure myths takes place in the steep mountains near Malad City. According to the tale, a group of bandits who robbed a bank in the 1800s hauled a heavy, iron bank vault door into a remote mountain cave in southeastern Idaho to hide their loot. Prospectors claim the door has been located over the decades, but it either cannot be opened or the cave disappears when they leave to fetch heavy tools.

Jackalopes and Snipe Hunts

Like much of the American West, Idaho has its fair share of playful tall tales used to prank newcomers.

The Jackalope: A legendary cross between a jackrabbit and an antelope.

The Snipe Hunt: A classic rural prank where eager newcomers are given a burlap sack and told to stand in a pitch-black field in the Idaho wilderness to wait for "snipes" to be driven toward them by the rest of the group.
