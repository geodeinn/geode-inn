Aya Huma (Diablo Huma): The central spirit of the Inti Raymi (Sun Festival). He wears a two-faced mask representing good and evil, day and night, and the past and future, topped with a hat that features four horns representing the four winds and sun rays. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]

El Chullachaqui: An Amazonian shapeshifter resembling a dwarf, with one normal foot and one backward-facing foot or hoof. He mimics the voices of loved ones to lure unsuspecting wanderers deep into the jungle.

La Tunda: An ugly, swamp-dwelling monster from Afro-Ecuadorian and Andean lore with a foot resembling a root of a quinoa plant. She hypnotizes people with the scent of roasted shrimp to kidnap them.

Tsunki & Mama Tungurahua: Spiritual entities that reflect Ecuador's geography. The Tsunki are benevolent Achuar water spirits, while Taita Chimborazo and Mama Tungurahua are Andean deities personifying the country's towering volcanoes.

La Dama Tapada (The Veiled Lady): A ghostly, elegantly dressed woman who appears on dark, lonely streets in Quito, smelling strongly of perfume. She lures in men who wander the night, only to lift her veil and reveal a rotting, skull-like face—serving as a cautionary tale against lust.

Cantuña: The most famous Quiteño legend, telling the story of an indigenous architect who made a pact with the devil to finish the atrium of San Francisco Cathedral by dawn to save his life. He tricked the devil by hiding a single loose stone, leaving the contract unfulfilled.

María Angula: A chilling tale from Quito about a girl who, after stealing a neighbor's tripe to make a soup, is haunted when the ghost of the dead man comes back demanding his guts returned.

La Caja Ronca (The Snoring Coffin): A phantom, glowing coffin carried by invisible spirits accompanied by a loud, rattling noise, believed to predict death or punish the sinful.

Sanjuanito: The most famous traditional rhythm and dance in Ecuador, recognized by its joyful, comic twists. It is frequently performed during indigenous celebrations.

Yumbo: A ritual dance featuring stamping feet and instruments like the pinkullo (Andean flute). It mimics the movement of native Amazonians bringing trading goods to the highlands.

La Diablada de Píllaro: A famous January festival where locals don elaborately carved devil masks and dance through the streets, a tradition blending indigenous rebellion with Spanish religious influence.

Año Viejo: Every December 31, Ecuadorians burn large, hand-crafted effigies stuffed with sawdust, representing bad memories, politicians, or pop-culture figures, to cleanse the coming year of negativity.
