Iraqi folklore is deeply rooted in ancient Sumerian, Akkadian, and Babylonian traditions. These early myths established themes that resonate throughout regional storytelling today.

The Epic of Gilgamesh: Perhaps the world’s oldest written story, it recounts the adventures of the King of Uruk as he searches for immortality.

The Descent of Inanna: This myth recounts how the goddess Inanna travels through the seven gates of the underworld to visit her sister Ereshkigal, setting the stage for folklore based on underworld journeys.

Atra-Hasis & Enuma Elish: Foundational creation myths where gods clash, create humanity out of clay, and send a great flood to reduce human population.

Iraqi folklore is perhaps best known internationally for its connections to Baghdad, the historical hub of the Abbasid Caliphate.

The One Thousand and One Nights (Alf Layla wa-Layla): Put together in Iraq, this massive collection features stories told by Scheherazade. It is the origin of legendary characters like Sinbad the Sailor, Ali Baba, and tales of Aladdin.

The Tanabilah: A classic Baghdadi folk story about the first documented elder care home, which featured sheltered beds, gardens, and 3 meals a day for elders without support.

The Tantal: A menacing, shape-shifting spirit in Iraqi lore. Often living near water, it can shower a person with wealth or pull them to a watery grave.

Folktales from Iraq: Collections, such as those compiled by C.G. Campbell in southern Iraq, focus on quick-witted heroes, sultans, fishermen, and magical happenings.

Northern Iraq Folklore: A diverse blend featuring Aramaic-speaking Syriac Christians and Kurdish Muslims, highlighting fables of animals (like the dog, ewe, and wolf) and stories of resilience.

Mājīnā: A popular Iraqi folkloric tradition that acts as a "trick-or-treat" style custom, where children go door-to-door in neighborhoods to sing traditional songs and receive treats.

Superstitions: It is widely considered good luck if a stork builds a nest on your home's roof. Conversely, people with blue eyes or those who have had no children were historically kept away from birth celebrations to prevent harm to the baby.
