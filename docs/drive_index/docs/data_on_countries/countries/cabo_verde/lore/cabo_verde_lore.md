Nhô Lobo & Chibinho (The Wolf and the Goat): The most famous fables feature Nhô Lobo (the wolf) and his various prey, particularly Chibinho (the goat/kid). Similar to West African Anansi stories, these tales use humor and sharp wit to show how the clever, smaller creature outsmarts the stronger predator.

The Fantasma da Pedra: A famous Sal Island myth about the ghost of a person who tragically died in the island's colonial-era salt flats.

Tchuba: A wandering, nocturnal female spirit or shape-shifter who seeks out the milk of nursing mothers, a classic adaptation of African night-feeding spirits.

A Encamisada: A chilling spectral procession seen wandering the streets of historical towns (like Cidade Velha, Santiago) at the stroke of midnight. The spirits wear white shrouds and carry candles. It is said if an onlooker accepts a candle from a spirit, by morning it turns into a human bone—a grim omen of their imminent death.

Escondido Oru & Drake's Treasure: Centuries of hope and speculation surround buried treasures on the islands, most notably a lost hoard supposedly buried by the 16th-century English pirate Sir Francis Drake. Locals continue to search for hidden gold coins or jewels in sea caves and colonial ruins.

Cura de Sete Fontes: A sacred spring ritual often found on the rugged, mountainous islands like Santiago and Santo Antão. Pilgrims trek for hours through vertical terrain to find "living water" (águia viva) in a challenging physical journey meant to heal both spiritual and bodily ailments.

The Mastro Ceremony: A unique religious tradition honoring Catholic saints. Locals erect a tall mast, colorfully decorated with fruits, as a community celebration and devotion.

Sodade & Morna: Because early generations of enslaved people were forbidden to speak openly, folklore and grief were expressed through song. The Morna is a soulful, melancholic folk style—championed globally by Cesária Évora—that deals directly with the themes of poverty, longing (sodade), and the harsh realities of island droughts.

Batuque: An African polyrhythmic call-and-response genre, heavily preserved by women, reflecting the islands' deep ancestral roots.
