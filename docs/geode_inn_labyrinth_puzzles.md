# Geode Inn — Labyrinth Puzzle System: "The House of the Double Axe"
## Zone: Greek Catacombs (Knossos Reconstruction)
## Quest: "The Bull and the Labyrinth" (Quest 3 of The Drowned Question)

### Design Philosophy

The labyrinth is not a maze. It's a palace. You don't escape it — you learn to read it.

Every other game that does "the labyrinth" makes it about finding the exit or killing the minotaur. We're doing something different: the labyrinth is a text written in architecture, and the puzzles are acts of literacy. Each puzzle teaches the player to understand a different aspect of Minoan civilization — engineering, art, religion, economics — by engaging with it as a spatial problem.

The minotaur isn't a monster. It's a misunderstanding. The "monster" is what happens when one culture can't read another culture's symbols and fills the gap with fear.

---

## The Labyrinth Structure

The Knossos reconstruction in the catacombs is a 3D instance — multi-story, connected rooms, built from real Minoan architectural principles. The layout is based on the actual excavated plan of Knossos:

**Ground Level (Entry)**
- The West Court (entrance plaza, kouloures — ritual pits)
- The Theatral Area (stepped viewing area)
- The West Magazine block (storage pithoi)
- The Corridor of the Procession (frescoed entrance hallway)

**Middle Level (Public/Ceremonial)**
- The Throne Room (stone seat, griffin frescoes, lustral basin)
- The Great Hall (murals of bull-leaping)
- The Shrine Room (snake goddess figurines, offering tables)
- The Pillar Hall (pillar crypts, carved double axes)

**Upper Level (Private/Administrative)**
- The Grand Staircase (two flights, light well, frescoes)
- The Residential Quarters (megalons, bathrooms, toilets)
- The Workshop (pottery, seal stones, fresco painting)
- The Treasury (seal stones, ivory, gold)

**Sub-level (The Puzzle Core)**
- The Drainage System (underground water channels)
- The Foundation Crypt (carved pillars, the deepest room)

## Puzzle 1: "The Corridor That Isn't Lost"

**Location:** Ground Level — Corridor of the Procession
**Mechanic:** Fresco restoration as navigation

The player enters the corridor. The walls are covered in frescoes depicting a procession — figures carrying offerings, leading a bull, musicians playing. But the frescoes are damaged. Sections are missing, and the corridor branches in multiple directions.

The puzzle: the frescoes are a map. The procession shows a route through the palace, and the figures in the fresco are walking toward specific destinations (the throne room, the shrine, the magazines). The player must identify which figure is going where, then follow the path that figure is walking toward.

**How it works:**
- Each fresco fragment shows a figure with distinctive attributes (a priestess carrying snakes → shrine, a bearer with grain → magazines, a bull-leader → the great hall, a guard with a double axe → throne room)
- The corridor branches — each branch is decorated with a different motif
- The player must match the figure's destination to the branch's decorative motif
- Wrong branches dead-end (not damage — just empty rooms that say "this isn't where they were going")
- Right branches lead deeper into the palace

**Difficulty:** Low — this is the tutorial puzzle. It teaches the player that the Minoans communicated through images, not text, and that reading the art is how you navigate the space.

**Cultural Teaching:** Minoan palaces were decorated with frescoes that told stories and marked spaces. The "Corridor of the Procession" is a real feature of Knossos. Minoan art was not decorative — it was functional. The frescoes told you where you were and what happened there.

## Puzzle 2: "The Labrys Path"

**Location:** Middle Level — Pillar Hall
**Mechanic:** Symbolic logic with the double axe

The player enters the Pillar Hall — a room with carved stone pillars, each bearing the double axe symbol (labrys). The room has multiple doors, all closed. Each pillar has a labrys carved in a different orientation (upward, downward, left-leaning, right-leaning, horizontal).

The puzzle: the labrys orientation is a code. Each door corresponds to a pillar, and the orientation of the labrys tells you what's behind the door:
- **Upward labrys** = ascending (leads to upper level)
- **Downward labrys** = descending (leads to sub-level)
- **Left-leaning** = west wing (residential)
- **Right-leaning** = east wing (workshops)
- **Horizontal** = the main hall (ceremonial)

But there's a twist: one pillar has two labrys carved — one upward, one downward. This is the pillar that leads to the Grand Staircase, which both ascends and descends.

**How it works:**
- The player must read the carvings and choose the correct door for their objective
- The objective changes as they progress — sometimes they need to go up, sometimes down, sometimes to a specific wing
- A wrong choice doesn't kill them — it sends them to an interesting room with a memory fragment (so wrong choices still reward exploration)
- The double-pillar (two labrys) is the key discovery — it unlocks the Grand Staircase, which is the main route between all levels

**Difficulty:** Medium — requires the player to learn and apply a symbolic system. The "wrong" doors still have content, so there's no punishment for exploration.

**Cultural Teaching:** The labrys (double axe) is the symbol that gives the labyrinth its name. "Labyrinth" = "place of the labrys." The double axe was a real Minoan religious symbol, not a weapon — it appears on pillars, seals, and in shrines throughout Knossos. Its meaning is debated, but it's associated with authority and the divine.

## Puzzle 3: "The Bull's Path"

**Location:** Middle Level — Great Hall
**Mechanic:** Pattern-matching / timing challenge based on bull-leaping

The Great Hall contains a large fresco of bull-leaping (taurokathapsia) — athletes grabbing a charging bull by the horns, flipping over its back, and landing behind it. The fresco is animated (the Inn's magic — the art moves).

The puzzle: the player must "read" the bull-leaping sequence to learn a route through the palace. The bull-leaper's path through the air traces a pattern — a specific sequence of rooms to visit in order. The fresco shows three different leapers, each tracing a different pattern. Only one pattern leads to the Shrine Room (the player's objective).

**How it works:**
- The player watches the animated fresco — three bull-leapers, each performing a leap
- Each leaper's trajectory traces a path on the floor (glowing trail, like a constellation map)
- The paths cross and overlap — the player must identify which one reaches the Shrine Room
- The correct path visits rooms in a specific order: Entry → Great Hall → Pillar Hall → Shrine
- The player must physically walk that path through the palace rooms
- Walking the wrong path resets the fresco (the leapers start over)

**Advanced layer (for higher difficulty):**
- The bull-leaping fresco also encodes timing — the speed of the leap determines how long the player has to walk each segment
- A faster leap = less time to reach the next room
- The player must match their walking pace to the leaper's trajectory speed

**Difficulty:** Medium-High — requires observation, pattern recognition, and spatial memory. The timing layer adds challenge for players who want it.

**Cultural Teaching:** Bull-leaping (taurokathapsia) is one of the most iconic Minoan practices, depicted in frescoes at Knossos and other sites. Both men and women participated. It was a ritual, not a sport — the bull was sacred, and the leap was an act of communion with the divine bull, not a conquest of it. The frescoes show the leap in three phases: approach, vault, and landing — which is the pattern the puzzle uses.

## Puzzle 4: "Water That Knows Where to Go"

**Location:** Sub-Level — Drainage System
**Mechanic:** Water routing / engineering puzzle

The player descends into the drainage system beneath Knossos — a network of terracotta pipes, channels, and settling basins. The Minoans had the most advanced plumbing in the ancient world — closed pipes, water pressure systems, flush toilets. The system is partially blocked (2,000 years of sediment).

The puzzle: the player must route water through the drainage system to reach three specific locations:
1. **The Throne Room lustral basin** (needs water for ritual purification)
2. **The Residential bathroom** (needs water for the flush toilet)
3. **The Workshop** (needs water for pottery and fresco painting)

The water enters from a mountain spring at the top. The player must open and close gates (operating the ancient Minoan valve system) to direct the flow. But the system has constraints:
- Total water pressure is limited — you can't send full flow everywhere at once
- Some pipes are damaged (clogged with sediment) — the player must clear them first
- The pipes connect in specific ways — water follows gravity, not the player's wishes
- There's a timing element: the lustral basin needs to fill slowly (ritual), the bathroom needs a quick burst (flush), the workshop needs steady flow (production)

**How it works:**
- The drainage system is visualized as a top-down network diagram (like a pipe puzzle game, but rendered as actual Minoan terracotta channels)
- The player operates valve stones (carved stone plugs that Minoans actually used) to open/close channels
- Clearing sediment is a mini-interaction (tap/click on blocked sections)
- The player must balance water pressure across the three destinations
- Correct routing fills all three — and the act of filling them activates the rooms above (the lustral basin glows, the toilet flushes, the workshop's kiln lights up)

**Difficulty:** Medium — classic water-routing puzzle, but with real Minoan engineering as the context. The "aha" moment is when the player realizes the Minoans had better plumbing than most of the world until the 19th century.

**Cultural Teaching:** Minoan plumbing is genuinely remarkable. Knossos had terracotta pipes with tapered joints (to maintain water pressure), stone-lined drainage channels, a flushing toilet in the residential quarter, and lustral basins for ritual purification. The system was gravity-fed from mountain springs. This is real engineering, not mythological embellishment.

## Puzzle 5: "The Snake Goddess's Riddle"

**Location:** Middle Level — Shrine Room
**Mechanic:** Logic puzzle / riddle using Minoan religious symbols

The Shrine Room contains the snake goddess figurine (a woman holding snakes in both hands, a cat on her head). Around her are offering tables with different objects: a double axe (labrys), a bull figurine, a horn of consecration (bucranium), a bundle of grain, and a clay seal.

The snake goddess speaks (or rather, the shrine does — her voice is in the walls):

*"I hold two serpents — one of earth, one of water. My cat watches the boundary between living and dead. My offerings are five, but only two will open my door. Choose what you would offer a goddess who holds death in her hands and life on her head."*

The puzzle: the player must select two of the five offerings to place on the offering table. The correct combination is:
1. **The horn of consecration (bucranium)** — the bull's horn symbol, representing the sacred bull and the ritual of bull-leaping (life, vitality, communion with the divine animal)
2. **The bundle of grain** — representing agriculture, the cycle of planting and harvest, the earth's fertility (the earth serpent)

**Why these two:**
- The snake goddess holds snakes (earth + water) and wears a cat (boundary guardian). She represents the cycle of life and death in nature.
- The double axe (labrys) is HER symbol — offering it back to her is redundant
- The bull figurine is too literal — the bull is sacred, but the *horn of consecration* is the abstracted symbol, and Minoan religion worked in abstractions
- The clay seal is administrative — it represents the palace bureaucracy, not the divine
- The grain is the correct earth offering — it connects to the earth serpent she holds

**How it works:**
- The player picks up and examines each offering (examination reveals a short text about what it symbolized in Minoan culture)
- The player places two offerings on the table
- Wrong combinations trigger different responses:
  - Labrys + anything → "You offer me my own symbol. I already have it."
  - Bull figurine + grain → "You bring me the animal instead of the idea. Think more carefully."
  - Clay seal + anything → "That belongs to the administrators, not to me."
  - Bucranium + grain → correct — the shrine door opens to the Foundation Crypt
- The player can try multiple times — there's no penalty, just different responses

**Difficulty:** Medium — requires understanding of Minoan symbolic logic, which the player has been learning through the previous puzzles. The "aha" is understanding abstraction — the Minoans didn't worship the bull itself, they worshipped what the bull represented.

**Cultural Teaching:** The snake goddess figurines are real Minoan artifacts — found at Knossos, they depict a woman (or women) holding snakes, with a cat or panther on her head. Their exact meaning is debated, but they're associated with earth/fertility religion and the household shrine. The horn of consecration (bucranium) is a real Minoan symbol — stylized bull horns carved on stone blocks, found throughout Knossos and other palaces. The "logic" of the puzzle is based on how Minoan religion actually worked — through abstraction and symbolic layering, not literal representation.

## Puzzle 6: "The Last Seal"

**Location:** Upper Level — Treasury / Workshop
**Mechanic:** Seal stone decoding / pattern matching

The Treasury contains Minoan seal stones — small carved stones used to press images into clay (the Minoan equivalent of signatures/authentication). Each seal stone has a unique design: a dolphin, a bull, a lily, a ship, a griffin, a labrys, a saffron flower, a goat, a lion.

The Workshop has clay tablets with seal impressions — but some are partial, damaged, or layered (one tablet has multiple overlapping impressions). The player must match the seal stones to their impressions on the tablets.

The puzzle has three layers:

**Layer 1 — Simple matching:** Match undamaged seals to clear impressions (tutorial — easy)

**Layer 2 — Partial impressions:** Some tablets only show part of the seal — the player must identify the seal from a fragment (requires understanding the visual vocabulary of Minoan seals)

**Layer 3 — The administrative record:** The tablets are arranged in a sequence that tells a story — an inventory record of goods received and shipped. The player must decode the sequence using the seal impressions to identify which official authorized which shipment. The sequence reveals:
- A large shipment of olive oil and grain left Knossos for a port
- The shipment was sealed by three officials (three different seals)
- One of the officials' seals appears on a tablet in the Foundation Crypt — the last seal
- The player must take the correct seal stone to the Foundation Crypt to unlock the deepest room

**Difficulty:** Medium-High — the first two layers are pattern matching, but the third layer requires the player to understand the administrative system. The "aha" is realizing the Minoans had a bureaucratic civilization with authentication systems — they were organized, literate (Linear A, undeciphered), and complex.

**Cultural Teaching:** Minoan seal stones are real and incredibly sophisticated — carved from semi-precious stones (agate, carnelian, jasper), they were used for trade, administration, and personal authentication. The Minoans had a writing system (Linear A, still undeciphered) and a complex administrative bureaucracy that tracked goods, shipments, and personnel. The "palace" at Knossos was as much an administrative center as a ceremonial one.

## Puzzle 7: "The Foundation"

**Location:** Sub-Level — Foundation Crypt (the deepest room)
**Mechanic:** Synthesis — all previous puzzle knowledge required

The Foundation Crypt is the deepest room in the labyrinth — a small chamber with carved pillars, each bearing multiple labrys symbols in every orientation. There are no doors — only a central stone basin and the carved pillars.

The final puzzle requires the player to synthesize everything they've learned:

1. **From the frescoes (Puzzle 1):** The player recognizes the fresco fragments on the walls — they show the same procession from the corridor, but from the *inside* — the perspective of the people in the procession, not the viewer. The player must identify which figure they've been following all along.

2. **From the labrys (Puzzle 2):** The pillars have labrys in every orientation. The player must orient the correct pillar — the one with two labrys (upward + downward) — by rotating it (physical interaction). When aligned, the pillar reveals a hidden channel.

3. **From the bull-leaping (Puzzle 3):** The trajectory of the correct bull-leaper, traced on the floor of the upper levels, converges at this point. The player must stand at the exact convergence point (the spot where the leaper "lands").

4. **From the drainage (Puzzle 4):** The hidden channel in the pillar connects to the drainage system. The player must have previously routed water to the sub-level (which they did in Puzzle 4, or they can go back and do it). Water flows into the central basin.

5. **From the snake goddess (Puzzle 5):** The player must place the bucranium and grain (carried from the Shrine Room) into the water-filled basin.

6. **From the seal stones (Puzzle 6):** The player must press the correct seal stone (the one identified from the administrative record) into the wet clay at the basin's edge — the "last seal."

**The result:** When the seal is pressed, the water in the basin changes — it becomes clear, then deep, then infinite. The basin becomes a window into the past — a vision of Knossos at its height. The player sees the palace alive: people in the corridors, bull-leaping in the great hall, priests in the shrine, ships in the harbor. The vision holds for 30 seconds. Then the water goes still.

Ariadne's voice, from the walls: "Now you have read the palace. You know what it was. You know what it lost. The mountain woke, and all of this became a shape under the ash. But you read it. That is what the labyrinth was for. Not to keep people out — to teach people to read."

**Difficulty:** High — this is the culmination puzzle. It requires understanding of all previous puzzle mechanics and the cultural knowledge they encode. But it's not "hard" in a reflexive or combative sense — it's hard in the way that understanding is hard. You have to have been paying attention.

**Cultural Teaching:** The synthesis puzzle teaches that Minoan civilization was a complete system — art, engineering, religion, administration, and architecture were all interconnected. The "labyrinth" wasn't a maze or a monster's lair — it was a complex, organized civilization that can only be understood by engaging with all of its aspects. The vision at the end makes the abstraction real — you see the people who lived there, not just their ruins.

## Puzzle Design Principles

1. **No punishment for exploration.** Wrong paths lead to interesting rooms with content, not dead ends with nothing.
2. **The puzzle IS the culture.** Every puzzle teaches something real about Minoan civilization. The player learns by doing, not by reading info dumps.
3. **The labyrinth is a text.** The space communicates through architecture, art, and symbolism. The player's job is to learn to read it.
4. **The "monster" is the misunderstanding.** There is no minotaur to fight. The minotaur was always a story told by people who couldn't read the palace.
5. **Synthesis, not repetition.** The final puzzle requires understanding of all previous puzzles, not just repeating one mechanic.
6. **The reward is understanding.** The final vision isn't a treasure or a boss fight — it's a moment of seeing what was lost. The emotional payoff is knowledge, not power.

## Implementation Notes

- **Engine:** Godot (2D isometric or top-down, consistent with the catacomb instances)
- **Art style:** Fresco textures for walls, warm stone palette, the Inn's amber/gold lighting. Animated frescos use the same style as the static art but with subtle motion.
- **Audio:** The drainage puzzle features water sounds. The bull-leaping puzzle has the bull-leap rhythm from the music direction (the Minoan section). The Foundation Crypt is nearly silent until the vision — then a single deep tone (64Hz, The Listener's frequency) resonates as the vision plays.
- **No combat.** None of these puzzles involve combat. The Greek quest chain is the scholar's arc — it's about thinking, not fighting.
- **Replayability:** The puzzles are the same on replay, but the vision at the end changes subtly based on which memory fragments the player collected (Puzzle 3) and which interpretation of Atlantis they chose (Quest 7 of the main chain). The palace remembers what the player noticed.
