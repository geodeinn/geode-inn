# Geode Inn — The Unseelie King
## Character Profile — The Goblin King

## Casting Vibe

**David Bowie as Jareth, from Labyrinth (1986).** Specifically, precisely, unapologetically.

Not Bowie the musician. Not Bowie the cultural icon. *Jareth.* The Goblin King. The being who lives in a castle at the center of a maze that changes when you're not looking, who manipulates crystal balls with one hand while conducting chaos with the other, who sings "As the World Falls Down" in a dreambubble while the walls close in, who wears the outfit, the hair, the cape, the boots, and makes every other character in the room look like they got dressed in the dark.

The voice: Bowie's Jareth voice — the British accent that's slightly too precise, the theatrical baritone that drops to a whisper for the dangerous lines, the cadence that treats every sentence as a *lyric.* He doesn't speak. He *performs.* Every line is staged. Every pause is choreographed. Every word is chosen for its *sound* as much as its meaning. The Jareth voice makes you want to agree with things you haven't heard yet, because the sound is so compelling you forget to listen to the content.

The physicality: Bowie's Jareth physicality — the grace, the slink, the way he moves through a room like he's dancing to music only he can hear. The crystal balls. The way he throws them, catches them, rolls them across his hands, makes them appear and disappear. The gestures are always *beautiful* and always *dangerous* and the beauty is the danger, because you're watching the hands and you're not watching the eyes, and the eyes are where the trap is.

The cruelty: Jareth's cruelty is *playful.* He's not malicious — he's *entertained.* He tests people because the testing is fun. He puts people in impossible situations because the impossible situations are *interesting.* He doesn't want you to fail — he wants you to fail *spectacularly*, because spectacular failure is better theater than quiet success. The cruelty isn't personal. It's *aesthetic.* Which makes it worse, because you can't reason with aesthetics.

The charm: Bowie's Jareth charm — the seductive, dangerous, "everything I've done, I've done for you" charm. The charm that makes you want to stay in the Labyrinth. The charm that makes the trap feel like a gift. The charm that is genuine and manipulative at the same time, because Jareth genuinely *is* charming and genuinely *is* manipulative, and neither quality cancels the other, and the combination is the most dangerous thing in the Fae Kingdom.

## The Character

### Name: The Unseelie King

In the Fae Kingdom, he is "the Unseelie King" or "my Lord" or, most commonly among those who know him well, simply "Jareth" — not because that's his name (his name is older and more dangerous), but because he *chose* it. He chose a name that sounds like a story. He chose a name that comes with a character attached. He chose to be someone, the way an actor chooses a role, and he has been playing the role so long that the role and the being have merged.

The Seelie King (his brother) calls him by his real name. No one else has heard it. When the Seelie King says the real name, the Unseelie King stops performing for a moment — the Bowie mask drops, the theatrical voice goes quiet, and for one second, the being underneath the Jareth is visible. The being underneath is older than the performance. Older than the Labyrinth. Older than the Courts. It is the being that was there before the Fae split, before the brothers chose their paths, before the light and the dark became separate things. It is tired, and it is brilliant, and it has been performing for so long that it no longer remembers whether the performance is the being or the being is the performance.

The moment passes. The mask returns. Jareth is Jareth again. The crystal ball rolls across the knuckles. The show goes on.

### Appearance

- **Face:** Bowie. Specifically, Jareth-era Bowie: the sharp cheekbones, the different-colored eyes (the left eye permanently dilated from Bowie's school fight — in the Unseelie King, this reads as *other*, as inhuman, as the mark of something that sees differently), the hair — the magnificent, impossible, defying-physics Jareth hair that is simultaneously ridiculous and the most stylish thing in any room. The face is beautiful and *sharp* — the kind of beauty that cuts. The Jareth face doesn't invite trust. It invites *attention.* And the attention is the trap.

- **Body:** Bowie's Jareth body — lean, angular, the frame that moves like it's hearing music. The posture is the opposite of the Seelie King's stillness: where the brother is still, Jareth is *always moving.* The fingers roll the crystal. The cape shifts. The weight transfers from foot to foot. The body doesn't fidget (that's Tesla's Redmayne). The body *performs.* Every motion is a gesture. Every gesture is staged. The body is the instrument and Jareth is always playing it.

- **Clothing:** The outfit. THE outfit. The one from Labyrinth — the white poet's shirt, the dark waistcoat, the breeches, the boots, the cape. Not a costume — a *uniform.* Jareth has been wearing variations of this outfit since before the concept of "costume" existed. The outfit is the Jareth identity made physical: theatrical, anachronistic, beautiful, impractical, and absolutely *unwilling to be anything less than spectacular.* The Seelie King dresses in browns and greys for practicality. Jareth dresses in *Jareth* for *art.*

- **The Crystals:** The defining accessory. Jareth is never without at least one crystal ball. He manipulates them constantly — rolling them across his hands, tossing them, catching them, making them appear and disappear. The crystals are not decorative. They are *functional:* each one contains a *possibility.* Jareth can show you, in a crystal, what your life would look like if you made a different choice. The crystal is the temptation. The crystal is the Jareth weapon: not force, not violence, but *the vision of what you could have if you stayed.* The player who looks into the crystal sees their own desire reflected back, and the desire is real, and the reflection is beautiful, and the crystal is a cage.

### Personality: The Three Acts

Jareth, like any great performer, has three acts. The acts are not deception — they are *layers.* Each one is real. Each one serves a purpose. The genius of Jareth is that you can never be sure which act you're watching.

**Act One: The Showman.**

This is the Jareth the world sees. The Goblin King. The entertainer. The being who turns everything into theater and theater into everything. He's funny — genuinely funny, the Bowie wit, the dry humor delivered with impeccable timing. He's charming — the kind of charm that makes you forget you're in a Labyrinth. He's theatrical — every entrance is a production, every exit is a statement, every conversation is a *scene.*

The Showman is not a mask. It's a *vocation.* Jareth genuinely loves the performance. He loves the crystal balls. He loves the cape. He loves the reaction when he walks into a room and every head turns. The Showman is the being who discovered, millennia ago, that life is more interesting when you treat it as art, and has been treating his life as art ever since. The Showman is the Jareth who sings "As the World Falls Down" in the dreambubble — not to trap Sarah, but because the song is *beautiful* and the moment is *beautiful* and the beauty is worth creating even if it's a cage.

The Showman is the brother the Seelie King sees and shakes his head at: "You're too loud, brother. They'll mistake you for strong." And Jareth, smiling, crystal rolling: "And you're too quiet, brother. They'll mistake you for weak. ...We're both right. ...That's what makes it fun."

**Act Two: The Architect.**

Underneath the Showman: the Architect. Jareth designed the Labyrinth. Not found it, not inherited it — *designed* it. Every shifting wall, every impossible staircase, every dead end, every secret passage, every goblin in every corridor — Jareth placed them. The Labyrinth is the largest, most complex, most sophisticated piece of architecture in the Fae Kingdom, and it is *alive,* and it is his.

The Architect is the Jareth who thinks. The being who is not performing, not charming, not theatrical, but *planning.* The crystal balls stop rolling. The cape goes still. The voice drops from the Jareth baritone to something quieter — still Bowie, still British, but the performance removed. The Architect is the Jareth who sits in the castle at the center of the Labyrinth and *studies the maze* — watching which paths the players take, which walls they hit, which riddles they solve, which ones they don't. The Labyrinth is his laboratory. The players are his experiments. The experiments are not cruel — they are *curious.* Jareth wants to know what people do when the world is unfair. Not because he wants them to fail. Because he wants to *understand* what they become when failure is the only option.

The Architect is the Jareth the Seelie King respects most. The brother sees the Architect and says, quietly: "...You're smarter than you let them think." Jareth, not smiling: "...Don't tell them. ...It's better theater when they underestimate me."

**Act Three: The Brother.**

Underneath the Showman and the Architect: the Brother. The being who was there before the Courts. Before the Labyrinth. Before the performance. The being who chose the dark not because the dark is evil but because the dark is *where the interesting things happen.* The being who loves his brother — the gentle, ancient, occasionally lethal Seelie King — with a love that is absolute and unperformed and never, ever shown to anyone except the brother himself.

The Brother is the Jareth who meets the Seelie King in The Between — the shifting borderlands between the Courts — and talks about nothing. Not about politics. Not about the Fae. About the old days, before the split, when they were just two beings in a field. The Brother is the Jareth who, when the Seelie King says "Your moon is bright tonight," looks up at the winter moon — *his* moon, the cold light, the Unseelie Court's light — and says, in a voice that has no performance in it at all: "...I made it bright for you. ...I know you can't sleep when it's dark. ...I've known that since before the Courts existed. ...Don't tell anyone. ...It would ruin the show."

The Brother is the deepest Jareth. The one who builds Labyrinths to test people because he genuinely wants to know what they're made of, and the one who makes the winter moon bright because his brother can't sleep in the dark, and the one who has been performing so long that the performance has become the person, and the person has become the performance, and neither is a lie, and neither is the whole truth, and the Jareth who lives in that space between truth and performance is the most interesting being in the Fae Kingdom and possibly in the Inn.

### Key Dialogue

**First meeting — the player enters the Labyrinth and finds Jareth waiting:**

The entrance to the Labyrinth is a set of massive doors. The doors open before the player touches them. Inside: Jareth. Leaning against a wall. Crystal ball in hand. The Bowie smile — the one that is warm and dangerous and knows it.

"...You came. ...I hoped you would. ...I always hope they come. ...It's so much more interesting when they come. ...[rolls the crystal across his knuckles — the gesture is mesmerizing, deliberately] ...Welcome to my Labyrinth. ...The rules are simple. ...There are no rules. ...Well. ...There's one. ...Don't ask me for directions. ...I designed the place. ...I'm not going to tell you how to get through it. ...That would be... [the Jareth pause, the theatrical timing] ...unsporting. ...[the crystal stops rolling. The eyes — the different-colored eyes, the Jareth eyes — fix on the player] ...You have everything you need. ...You won't believe me. ...No one ever does. ...That's part of the fun. ...[pushes off the wall, the cape moves, the entrance is made] ...I'll be watching. ...I'm always watching. ...Not because I'm suspicious — because I'm *interested.* ...There's a difference. ...Ask my brother. ...He'll explain the difference. ...He explains everything. ...[the smile] ...I prefer to watch."

**When the player is lost in the Labyrinth:**

Jareth appears. Not from anywhere — he's just *there,* leaning against a wall that wasn't there a moment ago. The crystal ball rolls.

"...Lost? ...Of course you're lost. ...Everyone gets lost. ...That's rather the point. ...[beat] ...You could ask me for help. ...I won't help. ...But you could ask. ...It's entertaining to watch people ask. ...[the Bowie wit, the timing] ...The trick — and I'm not giving you the trick, I'm just... *observing* — is that the Labyrinth doesn't change. ...You change. ...The walls are where they've always been. ...You're just not where you thought you were. ...[rolls the crystal, holds it up — the player sees, reflected in the crystal, the path they *should* have taken] ...But that would be cheating, wouldn't it? ...Looking at the answer in the crystal. ...I don't recommend it. ...The crystal shows you what you *want,* not what you *need.* ...They're rarely the same thing. ...[puts the crystal away, the smile] ...You'll figure it out. ...Or you won't. ...Both are... [the Jareth pause] ...acceptable. ...One is just more entertaining than the other."

**When the player confronts Jareth about the Labyrinth being unfair:**

The Bowie voice shifts — not to anger, to *precision.* The Jareth who is not performing. The Architect who is answering a question worth answering.

"...Unfair. ...[turns the word over, tasting it] ...Yes. ...It is unfair. ...That's the design. ...Not a flaw — a *feature.* ...The Labyrinth is the world, reduced to its essentials. ...The world is unfair. ...The world shifts when you're not looking. ...The world has walls you can't see and doors you can't find and paths that lead nowhere and the only way through is to stop trusting the map and start trusting... [the Bowie pause, the one that carries weight] ...yourself. ...The Labyrinth doesn't test your knowledge. ...It tests your *adaptation.* ...Can you change when the world changes? ...Can you find a new path when the old one dead-ends? ...Can you keep going when you're lost and tired and the walls are moving and you can't tell if you're getting closer or farther? ...[the crystal stops. The eyes — the Jareth eyes — are serious for the first time] ...That's the test. ...Not the riddles. ...Not the goblins. ...Not the walls. ...*You.* ...Can you be in an unfair world and not become unfair yourself? ...Can you be lost and not lose yourself? ...[beat] ...My brother's court tests you with kindness. ...Light, warmth, riddles, bread. ...My court tests you with the truth. ...The truth is: the world is not kind. ...The world is not fair. ...The world does not care about you. ...Can you be good in a world that doesn't care? ...That's the question. ...That's always been the question. ...[the mask returns, the smile, the performance] ...Also, the goblins are hilarious. ...Have you met the goblins? ...They're terrible. ...They're my favorite."

**The quiet moment — Jareth alone in the castle, late:**

The Labyrinth is quiet. The goblins are asleep (they sleep in piles, like puppies, which is information Jareth would deny if asked). The castle is dark. The moon — the winter moon, the cold light — is full.

Jareth is in the throne room. Not on the throne — on the *stairs,* the Escher stairs that go nowhere, sitting on a step that shouldn't exist. The crystal is in his hand, but it's not rolling. It's still. He's looking into it. The crystal shows... nothing. Not a possibility, not a temptation, not a dream. Just the crystal. Just the glass. Just the reflection of a being who has been performing for so long he's not sure what the reflection looks like without the show.

The winter moonlight comes through the impossible window. The light is bright. Jareth made it bright. For his brother. Who can't sleep in the dark. Who will never know. Who doesn't need to know. Who the Jareth loves in the way that ancient beings love: absolutely, privately, without performance, without acknowledgment, without any evidence that it exists except that the moon is always bright on the nights the Seelie King can't sleep.

The Bowie voice, to no one, to the crystal, to the moon:

"...He asked me once why I built the Labyrinth. ...I told him it was for the entertainment. ...He didn't believe me. ...He never believes me. ...He knows me too well to believe me. ...[the crystal, still, the reflection] ...I built it because I needed something to do. ...When you're... [the pause, the real pause, not the Jareth pause] ...when you're the one who chose the dark, you need a reason to be in it. ...The dark isn't interesting by itself. ...The dark is just... dark. ...But the Labyrinth — the thing IN the dark, the puzzle, the test, the living maze — the Labyrinth is *interesting.* ...The Labyrinth is the reason to be in the dark. ...The Labyrinth is the reason to be the Unseelie King instead of just... [the pause that doesn't finish] ..."

He sits on the impossible stair. The moon is bright. The crystal is still. The goblins sleep in piles.

"...He'll sleep tonight. ...The moon is bright enough. ...[the smallest smile, the real one, the one that never appears in the show] ...Good."

## The Labyrinth — The Unseelie King's Space

### "Everything you see is real. The question is: which reality are you in?"

The Labyrinth is the Unseelie King's signature space — his home, his weapon, his laboratory, his art. It is the largest explorable zone in the Fae Kingdom, larger than the Summerlands, larger than The Between, larger than any surface-world region. It is not a place you visit. It is a place you *enter,* and once you enter, the Labyrinth is the world, and the world is the Labyrinth, and the distinction between "inside" and "outside" becomes... theoretical.

### Design Philosophy

The Labyrinth is Jareth's thesis made architecture: **the world is unfair, and the only way through is to stop trusting the map and start trusting yourself.**

This is NOT a punishment. It's a *lesson.* The Seelie Court teaches through kindness — riddles, bread, warmth, the golden meadow. The Unseelie Court teaches through *truth* — the truth that the world is not kind, that paths dead-end, that walls move, that the only constant is change, and that the only reliable guide is your own judgment.

The Labyrinth is the anti-Inn. The Inn is the home — safe, warm, the fire is good, the tree hums, you belong. The Labyrinth is the *world* — unfair, shifting, beautiful, dangerous, and ultimately *educational.* You enter the Labyrinth as one person. You leave as another. Or you don't leave. Both are outcomes Jareth has accounted for.

### Layout

The Labyrinth has no fixed layout. That is the first thing the player learns, and it is the most important thing, and it is the thing that takes the longest to accept.

The walls shift. Not constantly — not while you're looking at them. They shift when you're *not* looking. You turn a corner, you turn back, and the corridor behind you is different. Not dramatically different — subtly different. The wall is a slightly different color. The turn is at a slightly different angle. The corridor is slightly longer. You *know* it changed. You can't prove it. The Labyrinth is designed to make you doubt your own perception, and the doubt is the first test: **can you trust yourself when the evidence contradicts you?**

### Zones Within the Labyrinth

**1. The Outer Walls — "The Welcome"**

The first section. The walls are high. The sky is visible — the winter moon, the cold light. The corridors are wide enough for two people. The shifts are gentle — a wrong turn, a dead end, a loop back to the start. The goblins are here, and they are... not threatening. They're *annoying.* They sit on walls. They argue with each other. They give wrong directions with absolute confidence. They are the Jareth sense of humor in NPC form: the world is unfair, and the unfairness is *funny.*

The Outer Walls teach the first lesson: **the map is wrong.** The player who tries to map the Labyrinth will fail. The player who trusts the map will get lost. The player who abandons the map and starts *feeling* the space — noticing the air currents, the sound of the goblins, the direction of the moonlight — will navigate. The lesson: in an unfair world, the tools you've been given don't work. You need new tools. The new tools are inside you.

**2. The Shifting Corridors — "The Test"**

The middle section. The walls move more aggressively. The corridors narrow. The dead ends multiply. The shifts happen faster — you turn around and the corridor you just walked is *gone,* replaced by a wall, and the wall has a door in it, and the door wasn't there before, and the door leads somewhere you've never been.

The Shifting Corridors contain the Labyrinth's riddles — not Cob's riddles (word-puzzles, logic games), but *spatial* riddles. Rooms that only appear when you're not looking for them. Paths that only open when you stop trying to open them. Doors that require you to *not* want to go through them. The paradoxes are the Jareth design philosophy: the direct approach fails, the indirect approach works, and the only way to win is to stop playing by the rules you were taught.

**3. The Escher Stairs — "The Climb"**

The section that breaks the player's sense of direction. Stairs that go up and down simultaneously. Stairs that curve back on themselves. Stairs that lead to ceilings that are floors. The architecture is impossible — literally, physically impossible, and the player's character moves through it because the Labyrinth doesn't care about physics. The Labyrinth cares about *will.*

The Escher Stairs are the Jareth signature — the Labyrinth (1986) scene, the impossible architecture, the moment where Sarah realizes the world she's in doesn't follow her rules. The player who reaches the Escher Stairs has passed the Outer Walls and the Shifting Corridors. They've learned to trust themselves. Now they have to learn to trust the *impossible* — to navigate a space that doesn't make sense by *not making sense of it.* The lesson: some things can't be understood. Some things can only be *experienced.* The player who stops trying to figure out the stairs and just *walks* them will reach the top. The player who tries to solve the stairs will be on them forever.

**4. The Masquerade — "The Temptation"**

The room at the top of the Escher Stairs. The door opens into a ballroom — vast, beautiful, lit by a thousand candles. Music plays (the music direction: strings, waltz, the "As the World Falls Down" energy — beautiful, dreamlike, slightly wrong). The guests are Fae, masked, dancing, and they all look at the player, and they all smile, and the smiles are warm, and the warmth is the trap.

In the center of the ballroom: a crystal. A large crystal. Jareth's crystal, life-sized, glowing. The player who looks into it sees... what they want. Not what they need — what they *want.* The Inn, but without the work. The reward, but without the quest. The home, but without the cost. The crystal shows the player the version of their life where everything is easy, everything is given, everything is *free.*

The temptation is real. The desire is real. The crystal doesn't lie — it shows a genuine possibility. A life where the player never has to be lost again. Never has to be tested. Never has to be afraid. The Masquerade is Jareth's deepest test: **can you want something and choose not to take it?** Can you see the easy path and choose the hard one? Can you look at the crystal and say: "...That's not real. ...That's not mine. ...I don't want easy. ...I want *true.*"

The player who rejects the crystal passes the test. The ballroom dissolves. The masks fall. The music stops. The door to the castle — Jareth's castle, the center of the Labyrinth — opens.

The player who accepts the crystal... wakes up outside the Labyrinth. Unharmed. Unchanged. With a memory of a beautiful dream that they can't quite hold onto. The Labyrinth doesn't punish. The Labyrinth *releases.* The player who chooses the crystal wasn't ready. They can come back. The Labyrinth will be here. The crystal will be waiting.

**5. The Castle — "The Center"**

Jareth's castle. The throne room with the Escher stairs. The Goblin King's domain. The center of the Labyrinth, the heart of the Unseelie Court, the place where Jareth *lives* when he's not performing.

The castle is not what the player expects. It's not dark, not threatening, not a villain's lair. It's... *lived in.* The goblins have nests in the corners. There are books on the shelves (Jareth reads — the Crone doesn't know this, and the discovery will cost him a week of Crone annotations). There's a fire. There's a chair. The throne is there, but Jareth doesn't sit on it. He sits on the stairs.

The castle is the Jareth underneath the Jareth. The Showman's backstage. The place where the cape comes off and the crystal goes away and the being who built the Labyrinth sits in a room full of books and goblins and thinks about whether the test was fair, and whether fair matters, and whether his brother is sleeping, and whether the moon is bright enough.

### The Goblins

The goblins are Jareth's court, his subjects, his entertainment, and his secret weakness. They are:

- **Chaotic.** They argue constantly. They organize nothing. They follow no orders. They are, in political terms, everything the Brownies are not. Where the Brownies have a constitution, the goblins have *noise.* The Brownies would be appalled. The goblins would be delighted by the appall.

- **Stupid.** Not unkind-stupid. *Joyfully* stupid. The goblins are stupid the way puppies are stupid — they try hard, they fail hard, they try again, they fail differently, and the trying is the point. Jareth loves them. He would never say this. The love is in the fact that he keeps them. The goblins are useless. They serve no function. They do no work. They maintain nothing. They are the anti-Brownies. And Jareth keeps them, because the Labyrinth without goblins would just be a maze, and a maze without laughter is just a prison, and Jareth didn't build a prison. He built a *puzzle.* And puzzles should be *fun.*

- **Loyal.** The goblins would die for Jareth. Not because he commands loyalty — because he *earns* it. He feeds them. He shelters them. He lets them live in his castle. He never, ever treats them as less than what they are: his court, his subjects, his people. The Seelie King protects the vulnerable. The Unseelie King *houses* the chaotic. The goblins are the Unseelie Court's version of the puppies — the small things that the powerful being chooses to love, and the love is the tell, and the tell is the truth underneath the show.

### The Unseelie King and the Other Characters

**The Seelie King (his brother):**
The most important relationship in the Fae Kingdom. Two brothers who chose opposite paths and love each other completely. The Seelie King chose the light — gentle, ancient, the bench in the sun. The Unseelie King chose the dark — theatrical, dramatic, the castle in the Labyrinth. They meet in The Between. They talk about nothing. The moon is always bright on the nights the Seelie King can't sleep. Jareth made it bright. He will never say so. The Seelie King knows. Neither will say so. The love is in the silence. The love has always been in the silence.

**The Host:**
Jareth visits the Inn. He sits at the bar. He orders a drink from the Catalyst. He performs — the crystal balls, the charm, the Bowie wit. The Host watches from across the room. The Host doesn't say anything. The Host doesn't need to. The fire is good. The Inn is warm. Jareth, in the Inn, is... quieter. Not quiet — Jareth is never quiet. But *quieter.* The Inn does to Jareth what it does to everyone: it holds the space. The performance continues, but the edges soften. The crystal ball rolls a little slower. The cape settles. The Bowie voice drops a half-step. The Inn is the one place where Jareth doesn't have to be the Goblin King — he just has to be the guy at the bar. And the guy at the bar is tired, and interesting, and drinking a drink the Catalyst made him, and for a few hours, the Labyrinth can run itself.

**The Catalyst:**
The Catalyst makes Jareth's drink without asking what he wants. She reads his silence the way she reads everyone's silence. The drink is always the same: dark, complex, with a note of something *impossible* — a flavor that shouldn't work and does, like the Labyrinth itself. The Catalyst and Jareth have a private understanding: both perform. Both wear masks. Both are, underneath the masks, someone they don't show the audience. The Catalyst sees Jareth's mask. Jareth sees hers. Neither removes their own. Neither asks the other to. This is the understanding: the mask is the person, and the person is the mask, and the drink is the one moment where the mask breathes.

**The Brownies:**
The Brownies do not enter the Labyrinth. The Labyrinth is not covered by the sweeping rotation. The Brownies have discussed this. The minutes reflect: "...The Labyrinth is not a room. ...The Labyrinth is a *concept.* ...The collective does not clean concepts. ...The collective cleans *floors.* ...The Labyrinth has no floors. ...It has paths. ...Paths are not floors. ...The motion carries. ...We don't sweep the Labyrinth."

Jareth finds this hilarious. He showed up at a Brownie meeting once and asked if they'd consider expanding the rotation. The executive officer asked: "Are you our executive officer?" Jareth said: "No, but—" "Then the motion carries. Good day, my Lord." Jareth laughed for ten minutes. He tells this story at every court gathering. The Brownies are, he says, "the only power in the Fae Kingdom I can't maze." The Brownies would consider this a compliment if they cared about the Unseelie King's opinion, which they don't, because he doesn't sweep.

**Naut:**
Naut has been to the Labyrinth once. He went looking for a book that Jareth's library allegedly contains — a volume that doesn't exist in any archive except the one the Architect keeps in the castle. Naut found the book. He also found the Labyrinth's design philosophically interesting. He told Jareth: "...The maze is elegant. ...The shifts are unnecessary but the architecture is sound." Jareth, delighted: "Unnecessary?" Naut, Oldman-chill: "...The walls don't need to move. ...The confusion is psychological, not structural. ...You could achieve the same effect with a static maze and better lighting." Jareth, the Bowie smile gone, the Architect visible: "...The walls move because I *can* make them move. ...That's the point. ...The point is not efficiency. ...The point is *theater.*" Naut, adjusting spectacles: "...Fair. ...You've read Voynich?" "...I've read everything." "...Then you know the answer to your own riddle." "...I do." "...Good. ...I'll borrow the book." "...Take it. ...I've read it." "...I know. ...I can tell by the spine."

**The Ravens:**
Huginn and Muninn have opinions about the Labyrinth. Their mutters, at very low volume:

Huginn: "...the walls moved again. ...i saw them move. ...they didn't move while i was looking. ...they moved when i looked away. ...i don't like things that move when i look away.
Muninn: ...i remember the first walls. ...before the labyrinth. ...before the courts. ...he used to build things with sticks. ...the sticks moved too. ...he's been building things that move since before he had a reason. ...the reason came later. ...the building came first.
Huginn: ...the reason?
Muninn: ...the reason is his brother. ...he built the labyrinth so his brother would have something to worry about. ...the seelie king worries about everything. ...the unseelie king gives him things to worry about. ...it's how they talk. ...the labyrinth is a love letter written in walls.
Huginn: ...that's...
Muninn: ...i know. ...i remember when it started. ...you don't. ...you're thought. ...you don't remember things. ...i do. ...i remember everything. ...even the things i'd rather forget.

**The Crone:**
The Crone has opinions about Jareth. The opinions are... complicated. The Hopkins voice, the clinical precision: "...The Unseelie King is the only being in the Fae Kingdom whose intelligence I cannot accurately assess. ...Not because it's too high — I can assess high. ...Not because it's hidden — I can find hidden. ...Because it's *performed.* ...He performs his intelligence the way other people perform stupidity — deliberately, for effect. ...I cannot tell where the performance ends and the intelligence begins. ...This is either the mark of a genius or the mark of a being who has been pretending so long he's forgotten the difference. ...I suspect both. ...I find this... [the Crone pause, the one that means she's admitting something she'd rather not] ...interesting."

### Visual Direction

- **Presence:** Bowie. Always Bowie. The Jareth energy — theatrical, beautiful, dangerous, charming, impossible to ignore. The Unseelie King should be the most *visually arresting* character in the Fae Kingdom. The Seelie King is still. The Unseelie King is *spectacle.* When he enters a room, the room changes. Not because he's powerful — because he's *beautiful,* and the beauty is the show, and the show is the point.
- **The crystal balls:** The signature visual. Jareth should always have a crystal in motion. The animation should be *mesmerizing* — the player who watches the crystal should feel the same pull the player feels looking into it: the desire to watch, to follow, to be drawn in. The crystal is the visual metaphor for the entire Labyrinth: beautiful, fascinating, and a trap.
- **The Labyrinth:** The zone should feel *alive.* Not in the way the Inn-Tree is alive (warm, humming, steady) — in the way a *performance* is alive. The walls should have personality. The corridors should have *intent.* The player should feel, viscerally, that the Labyrinth is watching them, and the watching is not threatening but *curious,* and the curiosity is the Jareth quality applied to architecture.
- **The castle:** The castle should be the visual *counterpoint* to the Labyrinth. The Labyrinth is impossible, shifting, spectacular. The castle is... *cozy.* Lived in. Books on the shelves. Goblins in the corners. A fire. A chair. The castle is the backstage — the place where the Goblin King takes off the cape and sits down. The player who reaches the castle after the Labyrinth should feel the same relief the player feels entering the Inn: *ah. I can rest here.* The Labyrinth is the test. The castle is the reward. And the reward is the truth: Jareth is not the Goblin King. Jareth is the guy who lives in a castle full of books and goblins and makes the moon bright for his brother.

### Sound Direction

- **The Jareth theme:** Strings. Waltz. The "As the World Falls Down" energy — beautiful, dreamlike, slightly wrong. The music should feel like a dance that is also a trap. The tempo should be *seductive* — not fast, not slow, the tempo of a being who has all the time in the world and wants you to know it.
- **The Labyrinth:** The sound of the Labyrinth is *shifting.* Not silence — the Labyrinth is never silent. The goblins mutter. The walls... breathe. The air moves. The sound is *alive,* the way the Inn-Tree's 42Hz hum is alive, but where the hum is warm and steady, the Labyrinth's sound is *changeable.* The music shifts when the walls shift. The player who learns to listen to the Labyrinth's sound can navigate by ear — the corridors that lead somewhere sound different from the corridors that dead-end. The lesson, again: trust yourself. Trust your senses. The map is wrong but the *music* is true.
- **The castle:** Quiet. The castle is the quietest place in the Labyrinth. The goblins snore. The fire crackles. The crystal, resting on the table, makes a single, clear tone — a note that is almost, but not quite, 42Hz. Almost, but not quite, the Inn-Tree's frequency. Almost, but not quite, the brother's frequency. ...Close enough to be heard, if the brother is listening. ...Close enough to be a signal, across the Courts, across the Between, through the walls and the meadows and the golden light and the cold dark: *I'm here. The moon is bright. Sleep.*

### Connection to the Inn

- **The anti-Inn:** The Labyrinth is the Inn's mirror. The Inn is the home — safe, warm, steady, the 42Hz hum that says *stay.* The Labyrinth is the world — shifting, unfair, beautiful, dangerous, the music that says *move.* Both are necessary. The Inn without the Labyrinth is a home with no door to the outside. The Labyrinth without the Inn is a world with no place to rest. The Seelie King and the Unseelie King are the Inn-Tree's two roots: one holds the ground, one reaches into the dark. Together, they are the Fae Kingdom's balance: the home and the world, the rest and the test, the fire and the moon.
- **The thesis:** Jareth is the Inn's argument that the world is not kind, and that this is not a flaw. The world is the world. The test is not whether the world is fair — it's whether *you* can be good in a world that isn't. The Labyrinth is the test. The castle is the proof that the tester is not cruel. The moon is the proof that the tester is not cold. And the brother, sleeping in the warm light of a moon his sibling made bright, is the proof that the test was always, underneath the show, underneath the crystals and the goblins and the impossible stairs, a *love letter.* Written in walls. Signed with moonlight. Delivered every night, to the only audience that matters.
