## Phase 2: Detective/Mystery Book Candidates

Added in response to Krista's love of Agatha Christie — since Christie's works remain under copyright until January 1, 2043, these public-domain detective classics fill the same niche.

---

### 7. The Murders in the Rue Morgue — Edgar Allan Poe (1841)
**Setting:** Paris, 1840s
**Themes:** Rationality vs. the irrational, the birth of detection, the locked room
**Significance:** The first detective story ever written. C. Auguste Dupin invented the archetype that became Sherlock Holmes, Poirot, and every detective who followed.
**Scenes:**
1. "The Reading Room" — Dupin and the narrator in their darkened apartment (introduction, atmosphere)
2. "The Newspaper" — Reading the impossible crime report (exploration — examine the details)
3. "The Crime Scene" — The locked room at Rue Morgue (exploration — clue-finding)
4. "The Interrogation" — Dupin questions witnesses (dialogue — language barriers are the clue)
5. "The Deduction" — Dupin reveals his reasoning (puzzle — player must follow the logic)
6. "The Capture" — Confronting the culprit (choice — how to handle the truth)
**Reward Stone:** Fluorite (associated with mental clarity and analytical thinking)
**Characters:** C. Auguste Dupin (NPC investigator), The Narrator (player character), Adolphe Le Bon (suspect), Prefect of Police
**Special:** Pure puzzle scene structure — the player must examine clues, notice inconsistencies, and follow Dupin's reasoning. This is the template for all detective Living Books.

---

### 8. The Moonstone — Wilkie Collins (1868)
**Setting:** Yorkshire, England — 1840s framing
**Themes:** Stolen inheritance, the curse of a sacred gem, multiple unreliable narrators
**Significance:** Considered the first full-length detective novel in English. A stolen diamond (the Moonstone) from a country house, with multiple suspects and shifting perspectives.
**Stone Connection:** The Moonstone itself is a gem — a sacred diamond looted from India. This directly ties to the Inn's stone database and themes of colonial theft vs. sacred ownership.
**Scenes:**
1. "The Diamond" — The Moonstone's history and theft (narrated introduction)
2. "The Birthday Party" — Rachel Verinder receives the stone (dialogue — meet the suspects)
3. "The Morning After" — The stone is stolen (exploration — examine the room, find clues)
4. "The Investigation" — Sergeant Cuff arrives (dialogue — the famous detective)
5. "The Rosanna Spoon" — The servant's secret (choice — who to trust)
6. "The Revelation" — The truth about the Moonstone (puzzle — piece together the evidence)
7. "The Return" — The stone goes home (emotional resolution — colonial theme)
**Reward Stone:** Moonstone (associated with intuition, cycles, and new beginnings)
**Characters:** Franklin Blake (player/narrator), Sergeant Cuff (NPC detective), Rachel Verinder, Godfrey Ablewhite, Rosanna Spearman
**Special:** Multiple-narrator structure means scenes replay from different perspectives. The player experiences the same night from different viewpoints, and each perspective reveals new clues. This is the Christie-style "everyone is a suspect" format in public domain.
**Mapping:** Main Floor (Library) with strong connection to the Stone entity — the Moonstone can be cross-referenced in the stone database.

---

### 9. The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes — Arthur Conan Doyle (1892)
**Setting:** London, 1880s-1890s
**Themes:** Observation vs. seeing, the science of deduction, justice outside the law
**Significance:** The defining detective character. Holmes stories through 1927 are public domain in the US.
**Scenes (selected stories):**
1. "A Study in Scarlet" — Meeting Holmes, the first case (introduction)
2. "The Speckled Band" — The locked room mystery (exploration — examine the bedroom)
3. "The Red-Headed League" — The absurd premise that hides a heist (dialogue — Holmes's interrogation style)
4. "The Final Problem" — Holmes and Moriarty at Reichenbach Falls (choice — the apparent end)
**Reward Stone:** Sardonyx (associated with protection and finding truth)
**Characters:** Sherlock Holmes (NPC — brilliant, abrasive, fascinating), Dr. Watson (player/narrator), Inspector Lestrade, Irene Adler, Professor Moriarty
**Special:** Holmes as an NPC is the star — the player is Watson, observing and assisting. The puzzle is understanding Holmes's deductions. In dialogue scenes, Holmes rapid-fires observations and the player must pick which detail is the key clue.
**Mapping:** Main Floor (Library) — the detective genre shelf, next to Poe and Collins.

---

### 10. The Leavenworth Case — Bramble Katharine Green (1878)
**Setting:** New York, 1870s
**Themes:** Family secrets, inheritance, the locked library
**Significance:** One of the first American detective novels, written by a woman — a century before Christie. Green invented many detective fiction conventions: the map of the crime scene, the corridor diagram, the chain of evidence.
**Scenes:**
1. "The Library" — A dead man in his study, a locked door (exploration)
2. "The Daughters" — Two cousins, one beneficiary, one disinherited (dialogue)
3. "The Investigation" — Detective Ebenezer Gryce arrives (dialogue)
4. "The Handwriting" — Forged letters and family documents (puzzle)
5. "The Trial" — The courtroom drama (choice — who to accuse)
6. "The Truth" — The real killer revealed (emotional climax)
**Reward Stone:** Lapis Lazuli (associated with truth, judgment, and intellectual clarity)
**Characters:** Detective Gryce (NPC), Eleanor Leavenworth (suspect), Harriet Leavenworth (suspect), Raymond (lawyer)
**Special:** Green's contribution to detective fiction is historically overlooked — placing her book alongside Poe and Doyle in the Inn is a scholarly reclaiming, consistent with the Inn's philosophy of presenting knowledge on its own terms.

## The Detective Genre Shelf

These four books form a dedicated sub-section of the Main Floor library — the **Detective Shelf**. Together they tell the story of a genre:

1. **Poe (1841)** invented the detective story
2. **Green (1878)** established the American detective novel
3. **Collins (1868)** wrote the first full English detective novel
4. **Doyle (1892)** perfected the form

The shelf is organized chronologically. Each book introduces a mechanic that the next builds on:
- Poe: The locked room puzzle
- Collins: Multiple narrators / unreliable perspectives
- Green: The crime scene investigation and evidence chain
- Doyle: The deduction as performance — watching genius work

**Future slot — 2043:** When Agatha Christie enters public domain, the Detective Shelf extends to include *The Mysterious Affair at Styles* (1920) — the first Poirot novel — as the culmination of the genre's evolution. A placeholder on the shelf, dimmed and locked, reads: "This space awaits 2043. The queen's works return to the public domain." This becomes a living demonstration of how copyright works — the Inn itself teaches the concept.

## New Scene Type: Deduction Puzzle

Added for detective books — a variant of the Puzzle scene type.

**Deduction Puzzle Mechanics:**
- The player collects clues during exploration scenes
- In the deduction puzzle, clues appear as cards on a "case board"
- The player must connect clues in the correct logical chain
- Wrong connections are gently rejected — "That doesn't follow" — without penalty
- Correct connections light up and lock in
- When the full chain is complete, the detective NPC confirms: "You see it now."
- This can be repeated if the player gets stuck — the detective offers hints through dialogue

**Example (The Moonstone — The Revelation):**
```
Case Board:
[Paint on bedroom door] → connects to → [Rosanna's apron]
[Rosanna's apron] → connects to → [The Shivering Sand]
[The Shivering Sand] → connects to → [Hidden evidence]
[Hidden evidence] → connects to → [Franklin Blake's coat]
[Franklin Blake's coat] → connects to → [Opium]
[Opium] → connects to → [Unconscious theft]

Player completes the chain → Dupin/Cuff: "You see it now. He took it without knowing he took it."
```

This system works for all detective books — the clue cards and connections change per story, but the mechanic is consistent.
