# Geode Inn — The Bone Chess Room
## Zone: Undercity (84Hz Shadow territory — but this room is different)

---

## THE ROOM

Deep in the Undercity, past the sleeping giant's ribcage, through a corridor of marrow hexagons that hum at a frequency that isn't quite 84Hz and isn't quite 42Hz — something in between — the corridor opens into a vaulted chamber.

The floor is a chess board. Full-scale. 8x8 squares, each ten feet across. The squares are alternating polished bone and dark marrow stone. The board fills the entire room — there are no sidelines, no gallery, no place to stand and watch. You are ON the board or you are not in the room.

The walls are lined with workbenches. Bone fragments sorted by type — femurs here, ribs there, knuckles in a jar. Tools hang on pegs. This is a workshop that happens to have a chess board for a floor. Or a chess room that happens to have a workshop for walls. The litch doesn't see a difference.

The ceiling is low and vaulted — the underside of the giant's ribcage. The bones curve overhead like a cathedral built inside a skeleton. The room is warm, which is wrong for the Undercity. The litch's craft generates heat. Bone-work is physical. The constructs don't animate themselves — the litch carves the joints, wires the tendons, aligns the marrow-resonance chambers. It's labor. Labor makes heat. The room smells like bone dust and warm oil and something faintly electrical — the frequency that animates the constructs humming through the air.

There is no throne. The litch doesn't sit in authority. The litch sits at a workbench, assembling something, and looks up when you enter. The constructs stand at rest along the walls — not in niches or on pedestals, just standing there, like workers on break. Some are incomplete. A torso without legs. A skull with no jaw, wired to a spine. A hand, fingers articulating slowly, testing its own joints. These are not trophies. They are projects. Works in progress. No part goes to waste.

## THE LITCH

### Concept
A litch — but not the kind that hoards power. This litch pared itself down to the minimum required to stay animated and do its work. The original body is gone. What remains is the head — the mind, the craft, the identity — mounted in a bone construct body that can be swapped, rebuilt, reconfigured as the task requires. Need fine detail work? Small, precise hands. Need to move heavy materials? Reinforced frame, broader shoulders. The body is a tool. The head is the craftsman.

The litch's goal is not world domination. The goal is to save people from death and the afterlife — to preserve what was, to keep what shouldn't be lost. But the method — raising bone constructs, reassembling the dead, building an army of preserved souls in bone frames — looks exactly like what a villain would do. The litch knows this. The litch doesn't care how it looks. The litch cares about the work.

### Ethos: "No Part Goes to Waste"
Everything is material. A fallen adversary's femur becomes a construct's leg. A shattered rib becomes a chess piece's base. The litch doesn't see death as waste — the litch sees death as a failure of craft, and the litch is determined to fix it. Every bone the litch works with was someone's, once. The litch remembers. The litch doesn't forget a single one. The names are carved inside the bones — tiny, where nobody else can read them. The litch knows every name.

### Moral Position
Anti-hero or boss, depending on the player's perspective. The litch is not evil. The litch is doing something that looks evil because the alternative — letting people be lost — is, to the litch, worse. The litch would rather be feared and right than loved and wrong. This is not arrogance. This is the cost of a decision the litch made a long time ago and has never revisited, because revisiting it would mean admitting the dead might deserve to stay dead, and the litch is not willing to consider that possibility.

### The "Oi!"
When sufficiently annoyed — particularly by a certain Inn guard who shall remain nameless — the litch emits a sharp "OI!" that resonates through every construct in the room simultaneously. It's not speech. It's a frequency command. Every bone in the room vibrates at the litch's annoyance. It's the litch's tell — the one crack in the composure. If you hear "OI!" you know you've gotten under the bone.

### MTG Inspiration
The character is built on the Geralf/Stitcher archetype from Magic: The Gathering:
- Stitcher's Supplier: "No part goes to waste" — every piece is material
- Rooftop Storm: the ability to raise and command freely, an "era of unlife"
- Poppet Factory: the constructs gain form and capability — "It may have been a mistake to give them minds of their own"
- Geralf, Visionary Stitcher: the craft itself — giving constructs new capabilities, sacrificing the old to build the new, inspired by (and in conversation with) a sibling

The litch is a stitcher. Not a necromancer in the classic sense — a craftsman. An engineer of bone. The magic isn't in raising the dead; the magic is in the precision of the construction. Anyone can raise a skeleton. The litch builds things that are BETTER than what they were when they were alive.

### Construct Frame System

The Archivist does not have a single body. The head is the only constant — the mind, the craft, the identity. Everything below the neck is a tool, selected and swapped based on the task. Most constructs are NOT humanoid.

Default humanoid frame: the "social body" — worn when meeting visitors, playing chess, or when the Archivist needs to be read as a person rather than a machine. The closest thing to a face the Archivist presents to the world.

Task-specific frames (non-humanoid):

1. Detail-Work Spider: A low, six-limbed frame radiating from a central chamber. Each limb ends in delicate finger-bone clusters for precision carving. Built for close bench work. The head sits recessed in the central chamber, protected.

2. Heavy-Labor Quadruped: A squat, four-legged reinforced frame built from pelvis-bone and femur struts. Wide and low for hauling material. Thick ribcage armor across the back. The head is tucked near the front like a cockpit.

3. Chess-Play Frame: A tall, thin asymmetrical frame — a long articulated spine rising from a tripod base, topped with a fan of small resonance chambers, each tuned to a different construct on the board. Pure architecture. No humanoid silhouette.

4. Idle Constructs: Several purpose-built frames stand empty along the workshop walls — a wheel-construct of curved rib-arcs for rolling loads, a folding easel-construct of finger bones for display, a cube-construct of interlocked vertebrae for storage. Each has an empty head-chamber, waiting to be occupied.

The humanoid frame is the exception, not the default. The Archivist spends most of the time in shapes that follow function, not anatomy. The workshop is full of empty bodies waiting for a head.

## THE CHESS GAME

### The Board
The room's floor IS the board. When a game begins, the player stands on the board alongside the pieces. The litch plays from a workbench at the edge — not moving pieces physically, but commanding them with frequency. Each construct responds to a specific harmonic. The litch plays chess the way a conductor conducts an orchestra.

The pieces are bone constructs — each one a repurposed soul the litch has "saved." They are not mindless. The Poppet Factory principle applies: "It may have been a mistake to give them minds of their own." The pieces have opinions about being moved. A pawn might hesitate. A knight might enjoy the L-shape. The queen might be reluctant to be sacrificed. The litch overrides them. But the hesitation is visible. The player sees it.

### The Rules
Standard chess rules apply — but with Inn modifications:

1. **Construct Pieces:** Each piece is a named construct with its own personality. The litch knows every name. If the player asks, the litch will tell them who each piece was, before. The stories are not happy. They are not sad, either. They are just... preserved. The litch kept them. That's the point.

2. **Frequency Shift:** Mid-game, the litch may shift the board's frequency from 84Hz (Shadow) toward 42Hz (Living). When this happens, the board warms. The constructs move more fluidly. The game becomes faster. The shift is the litch testing something — seeing if the constructs respond better at a frequency closer to life. It's experimental. The player is a variable.

3. **The Sacrifice Rule:** When a piece is captured, it doesn't fall over. It walks off the board. The litch watches it go. The construct returns to the wall and stands at rest. Nothing is destroyed. No part goes to waste. The captured piece becomes material for the next project. The litch never wastes a game — even a loss is productive.

4. **The Wager:** The litch plays for stakes, not for fun. The litch has never lost. If the player wins, the litch gives them something — a construct companion, a bone charm, access to a deeper room. If the player loses, the litch asks for something. Not a soul. Information. The litch wants to know what the Inn is like at 42Hz — what the living feel that the preserved don't. The litch is a researcher. The wager is always research.

5. **The Unbeatable Problem:** The litch has never lost because the litch has been playing since before most of the Inn's visitors were born. The player is not expected to win through skill alone. The player wins by finding a weakness the litch doesn't know it has — and the litch's weakness is that the constructs have minds of their own. If the player can connect with a piece — talk to it, understand it, earn its trust — that piece may choose to move differently than the litch commands. Not defect. Not betray. Just... hesitate at the right moment. Create an opening. The litch would never blame the piece. The litch gave them minds. That was the mistake. That's also the beauty.

### Difficulty
The chess game is HARD. Not because the AI is unbeatable — because the litch is genuinely brilliant. This is the character who played on a giant chess board at age nine and never lost. The game should feel like playing against someone who sees the board differently than you do — who thinks in shapes and patterns and sacrifices three moves ahead. But it should also feel fair. The litch doesn't cheat. The litch is just better. Until the pieces teach the player how to win.

## THE ROOM'S PLACE IN THE INN

### Zone: Undercity, but Different
The Bone Chess Room is in the Undercity, but it doesn't behave like the rest of the Undercity. The Undercity is 84Hz — Shadow territory, cold, the place where things stay. The litch's room is warm. The frequency is unstable — oscillating between 84Hz and 42Hz. The litch is experimenting. The room is the experiment. The litch is trying to build a space where the dead can be closer to alive — not alive, but closer. The chess game is part of the experiment. Movement is life. The constructs that move are closer to living than the ones that stand still. The litch plays chess to keep them moving. The game is kindness disguised as strategy.

### Connection to Other Characters
- **Lozen (River's character):** The relationship is combative but fond. Lozen guards the Inn. The litch preserves the dead inside it. These are not compatible worldviews. The "OI!" is the litch's only break in composure, and Lozen is the only one who triggers it. There is respect underneath the friction. Lozen fights for the living. The litch fights for the dead. Both are fighting against loss. Neither would say so to the other.

- **The Crone:** The Crone has opinions about the litch. The Crone's notebook contains annotations about the bone constructs — observations about their craftsmanship, questions about their autonomy. The Crone is fascinated but careful. The litch is the only person in the Inn who might understand the Crone's obsession with preserving knowledge — both are keepers. Different materials, same instinct.

- **Quire (the Archive scholar):** Quire would be terrified of this room and would never admit it. Quire researches in the margins of restricted texts. The litch works in the margins of life and death. Quire sees a mirror and flinches.

### Storyline Potential
The litch's noble goal (save people from death) that looks like villainy (armies of bone constructs) is a perfect Geode Inn storyline — moral complexity without clear right and wrong. The player discovers the litch's purpose over time, through the chess games and through talking to the constructs. The revelation is not "the litch is actually good" or "the litch is actually evil." The revelation is "the litch is doing something that has a cost, and the litch has decided the cost is worth paying, and you have to decide if you agree."

## AUDIO PROFILE

- **Room Ambient:** Bone percussion — the subtle click and clack of constructs testing their joints. Low hum at the litch's working frequency (between 84Hz and 42Hz, unstable). Warm despite the Undercity context. The sound of craft — carving, filing, the whisper of bone dust.
- **Chess Game:** Each piece moves with a resonant tone — the litch's frequency command made audible. Moving a piece sounds like a low chord. Capturing a piece sounds like a construct exhaling — a release of pressure, then silence as it walks off the board.
- **Frequency Shift:** When the litch shifts the board from 84Hz toward 42Hz, the room's ambient tone rises. The constructs' movements become more fluid-sounding. The warmth increases. The sound is the litch's hope made audible — and it's unstable, because hope in the Undercity always is.
- **The "OI!":** Every construct in the room vibrates simultaneously. A sharp percussive hit across all bone surfaces. Brief. Startling. Then back to work.

## ART STYLE

The Bone Chess Room sits in the Undercity, so the foundational DNA applies: Klimt's Death and Life. But the litch's craft adds a layer — the precision of the stitching, the engineering of the constructs, the WORKSHOP quality. Think Klimt meets biomechanical drawing. The bones are not morbid — they are crafted. The joints are articulated with care. The room is a studio, not a crypt.

The chess pieces themselves should have individual character — each construct is a person who was someone, and their bone frame should hint at who they were. A tall thin piece might have been tall in life. A stocky piece might have been strong. The litch preserves the essence in the form.
