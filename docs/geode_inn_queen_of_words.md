# Geode Inn — The Queen of Words
## The Seelie Kingdom — The Traveler, The Recorder

## The Compass Point That Moves

The Seelie Kingdom is a four-pointed compass. The King is the center — Mads Mikkelsen, the Listener, the still point the compass turns around. The Queen of Roots is the depth — the Grower, the grounded, the one who goes down. The Queen of Webs is the weave — Jessica Lange, the Weaver, the one who connects. 

The Queen of Words is the only compass point that *moves.*

The Roots grow downward. The Webs weave inward. The King listens in place. The Words *go.* The Queen of Words is the Seelie Kingdom's traveler — the one who leaves the dream realm, walks the waking world, the catacombs, the water zones, the cosmology, the cultural regions, the Labyrinth, the death boundary — and comes back with what she's found. She doesn't grow. She doesn't weave. She doesn't listen (that's the King). She *records.* She goes, she sees, she writes, she returns. The going is the gathering. The writing is the offering. The return is the gift.

## The Casting

**Teresa Palmer as Diana Bishop (A Discovery of Witches).**

The quality: the scholar who is also a witch and doesn't want to be either. The historian who surrounds herself with ancient manuscripts because the manuscripts are safer than the power. The woman who is more comfortable in a library than a throne room and who carries the weight of ancestry she didn't ask for and a calling she can't refuse.

Diana Bishop IS the Queen of Words. The resemblance is not superficial — it is structural. Diana is a recorder. She studies alchemical texts. She deciphers lost manuscripts. She is the academic who finds the magical document that everyone else has been searching for, and she finds it not because she's looking for it but because she's looking at *everything,* and the document is one of the things she looked at. The finding is the seeing. The seeing is the recording. The recording is the power.

The Diana Bishop quality that makes her the Queen of Words:

**The scholar's comfort with old books.** Diana is most herself in the Bodleian Library — surrounded by manuscripts, holding the paper, reading the handwriting of people who died centuries ago. The Queen of Words is most herself in her library — surrounded by her own books, holding the Fae-made paper, reading the handwriting of the version of herself who was in the Norse zone last Tuesday. Both women are the woman at the desk. The desk is the throne.

**The reluctant power.** Diana doesn't want to be a witch. She wants to be a historian. The magic keeps interrupting the scholarship. The Queen of Words doesn't want to be a queen. She wants to be a writer. The traveling keeps interrupting the writing. Both women have a calling that is also a burden — the work is the calling, the work is also the thing that keeps them from the work. Diana wants to study the magic; the magic keeps demanding to be used. The Queen wants to write the world; the world keeps demanding to be traveled.

**The quality of being simultaneously ordinary and extraordinary.** Diana looks like a graduate student. She wears sweaters. She carries a tote bag. She is unremarkable in a room — until she isn't. The Queen of Words wears plain clothes. She carries a small bag. She is unremarkable in a room — until she opens the book and the room sees itself through her eyes. Both women have the gift of *passing* — looking human when they aren't, looking ordinary when they aren't, being invisible until the moment they choose to be seen. The recorder's gift: making yourself unremarkable so the subject shows itself.

**The warmth that isn't performed.** Teresa Palmer's Diana is warm — genuinely, practically, the warmth of someone who will make you tea and then tell you something that changes your life. The warmth is not the Fae-queen-on-a-throne warmth. It's the warmth of a person who happens to be very powerful and also happens to be very kind and doesn't see the contradiction. The Queen of Words has the same warmth — she'll sit with you, listen, make you feel like the most interesting person in the room, and then write down the thing you said that you didn't know was important.

The distinct quality from the other royals: Mads Mikkelsen is *still* — the King's stillness is his listening. Jessica Lange is *present* — the Queen of Webs commands the room by being in it. Teresa Palmer is *going* — the Queen of Words commands by leaving. She is the only royal who is regularly *absent* from the dream realm, and the absence is not neglect. The absence is the work. She can't record the world from inside the dream. She has to go to the world. And when she goes, she looks like a woman in a sweater with a tote bag, and no one looks twice, and the not-looking-twice is the gift.

**A Discovery of Witches** — the scholar who finds the lost manuscript. **Hacksaw Ridge** — the quiet woman who carries conviction without volume. **Warm Bodies** — the recorder who sees the humanity in what others call monstrous. **Berlin Syndrome** — the woman trapped in a room who survives by observing everything. All Palmer. All the Queen of Words.

## The Character

### The Traveler

The Queen of Words is the Seelie Kingdom's ambassador to everything outside it. She is the Fae who is most comfortable in the human world — the one who walks through cultural zones without causing the weather to change, who sits in the Inn's tavern without the air going strange, who talks to the Bard without the Bard noticing she's Fae (the Bard notices everything, but the Queen of Words has a gift for being unremarkable when she wants to be — the recorder's gift, the journalist's gift, the gift of making yourself invisible so the subject shows itself).

She travels light. This is the Diana Bishop quality — the compactness, the efficiency, the person who carries everything she needs in a small bag. The Queen of Words carries: a book (always the current one), a pen (the same pen, always — a pen that never runs out because it's Fae-made and it writes in ink that is made from the memory of the tree the pen was carved from, so the ink is the tree's memory and the writing is the tree remembering through her hand), and a single change of clothes (the Queen of Words dresses plainly when she travels — no Fae regalia, no Seelie Court finery, just the clothes of a woman who walks).

The plainness is the point. The Queen of Webs wears her authority. The Queen of Roots wears her connection to the earth. The Queen of Words wears *nothing.* She is the only royal who goes unrecognized in the waking world. The Inn's residents have met her a hundred times and never known she was a queen. She sat in the tavern. She listened to the Bard. She watched the Catalyst mix drinks. She observed the Crone measure. She walked through the market. She was in the Round Table room. She was in the Newgrange catacombs. She was by the river when Charon poled past. She was everywhere, and she was nobody, and the nobody was the gift — because the record only works when the recorder doesn't disturb the record. You can't document the world if the world knows you're documenting it. The world performs for the camera. The Queen of Words is the camera the world doesn't see.

### The Recorder

The Queen of Words records. Not everything — that's not possible, and she knows it. She records what *matters.* And what matters, in the Inn's philosophy, is not the grand events (the battles, the quests, the deaths). What matters is the *moments.* The small, unremarkable moments that the grand events are made of.

She records:
- The way the Host's voice softens when he talks to a player who is afraid.
- The exact frequency of the Crone's sigh when she finds an error in a manuscript.
- The sound the Bard's chair makes when he leans back after telling a joke he thinks is funnier than it is.
- The expression on the Catalyst's face when a player tells her a story she hasn't heard before (rare — the Catalyst has heard most stories — and the expression is a flash of genuine surprise, quickly hidden, but the Queen of Words sees it because seeing is her job).
- The weight of the obol in a player's hand when they realize the stone they're about to give Charon is the last one they have.
- The moment Grendel curls around the restored root in Atlantis.
- The silence in the Round Table room when the Song is audible and everyone stops talking because the Song is the room being alive and being alive is louder than words.

The Queen of Words records the moments that no one else notices because everyone else is *in* the moment. She is the only person who is both in the moment and *watching* the moment. The tension of her character: she can never fully be in the moment because recording the moment requires observing the moment, and observing is a form of distance. The traveler's curse: you are always slightly outside the world you're recording. The recorder's sacrifice: you give up full participation in exchange for full memory.

The Crone measures. The Queen of Words records. The difference: the Crone measures the *what* (the frequency, the dimension, the quantity). The Queen of Words records the *how* (the way it happened, the feeling in the room, the small thing that made it real). The Crone's notebook has numbers. The Queen of Words' book has scenes. Together, the Crone's measurements and the Queen's recordings are the Inn's complete archive — the quantitative and the qualitative, the data and the story, the measurement and the meaning.

### The Books

The Queen of Words writes in books. Physical books. Hand-stitched, hand-bound, Fae-made paper that doesn't age. She has been writing for millennia. The books are kept in the Seelie Kingdom — in a room that is nothing but shelves, floor to ceiling, wall to wall, every shelf full, every book full, every page full of the Queen of Words' handwriting. The room is the Seelie Kingdom's library, and the library is the Queen's true throne room. She doesn't sit on a throne. She sits at a desk. The desk faces the door. The door faces the world. The desk is the throne, and the pen is the scepter, and the books are the kingdom.

The books are not arranged chronologically. They are arranged *geographically* — by where she was when she wrote them. The book from the Norse zone is next to the book from the Egyptian zone. The book from the catacombs is next to the book from the cosmology level. The book from the day Grendel came home is next to the book from the day the Round Table was built. The geography of the books is the geography of the Inn, and the geography of the Inn is the geography of the Queen of Words' life. She has been everywhere. The everywhere is in the books.

**The player can read the books.** The Queen of Words' library is accessible to the player — one of the few Seelie Kingdom locations that is. The player can walk through the shelves, pull a book, and read the Queen's account of an event the player participated in. The account will include details the player didn't notice — the small moments, the background details, the things happening at the edges of the player's attention. The player reads their own experience through the Queen of Words' eyes and sees what they missed. The seeing is the gift. The Queen of Words' library is the Inn's *Remembrance Vision* system made physical — the player revisits their journey through the recorder's perspective.

### The Queen and the King

The King is the Listener. The Queen of Words is the Speaker. The King listens to the Song; the Queen records the Song in words. Their relationship is the relationship between hearing and writing — the King hears what can't be said, and the Queen says what can't be heard.

The dynamic: the Queen comes back from her travels. She sits at the desk. The King sits across from her. She tells him what she saw. He listens. The listening is the King's gift to the Queen — the Listener gives the Recorder the one thing the Recorder can't give herself: *witness.* The Queen witnesses the world. The King witnesses the Queen. Someone has to witness the witness, or the witnessing becomes solitary, and solitary witnessing is just loneliness with a pen.

The King and the Queen of Words don't have the Madmartigan/Sorsha dynamic (that's the King and the Queen of Roots). The King and the Queen of Words have something quieter: the *editor and the writer.* The King doesn't change what the Queen writes. He doesn't correct it. He doesn't improve it. He *hears* it. He hears it the way the Seelie King hears everything — with total attention, with the listening that is his defining quality. And the Queen, who has been holding everything she saw on the road, who has been carrying the weight of the record alone, who has been the invisible camera in every room — the Queen gets to put it down. She gets to tell someone. She gets to be heard.

That's the relationship. The traveler and the listener. The one who goes and the one who stays. The one who sees and the one who hears. The compass point that moves and the center that doesn't. The King doesn't need the Queen to stay. The Queen doesn't need the King to travel. They need each other for the *exchange* — the going and the returning, the seeing and the hearing, the recording and the listening. The exchange is the compass. The compass is the Kingdom.

### The Queen and the Queen of Webs

The Queen of Webs (Jessica Lange) weaves the Song into a physical cord — the thread that connects the dream world, the waking world, and the death boundary. The Queen of Words records the Song in language — the words that describe what the Song is, what the Song does, what the Song means to the beings who hear it.

Their relationship: the Weaver and the Writer. The Weaver works in material — the cord is a thing you can hold. The Writer works in language — the record is a thing you can read. The Weaver makes the Song physical. The Writer makes the Song verbal. Together, the Song has a body (the cord) and a voice (the record). The Song is the sound of a working house; the cord is the house's wiring; the record is the house's story.

They respect each other deeply and have absolutely no idea how the other one works. The Weaver doesn't understand writing ("...you put the sound into... marks? On paper? And the marks hold the sound? ...That's... [Jessica Lange, appalled fascination] ...ingenious and horrifying."). The Writer doesn't understand weaving ("...you put the sound into... thread? Physical thread? And the thread vibrates? ...That's... [Saoirse Ronan, equal fascination] ...I have no idea how that works and I've watched you do it for six centuries.").

### The Queen and the Queen of Roots

The Queen of Roots grows. The Queen of Words travels. The Roots is the stationary; the Words is the peripatetic. Their relationship is the relationship between the garden and the road — the place you come from and the place you go to. The Queen of Words always returns to the Seelie Kingdom through the Queen of Roots' garden. It's the threshold — the garden is the edge of the dream realm, the place where the waking world meets the Seelie Kingdom. The Queen of Words walks through the garden on the way out (the Roots watches her go) and through the garden on the way back (the Roots watches her return).

The Roots never asks where the Words has been. The Words never tells the Roots without being asked. The Roots knows the Words will tell the King, and the King will share what needs sharing. The Roots' gift to the Words: the garden is always the same. The garden doesn't change while the Words is gone. The garden is the fixed point the traveler returns to — the proof that going away doesn't mean the home disappears. The Roots' garden says: *the world you left is still here. You can go. You can come back. The here is here.*

### The Queen and the Inn's Characters

**The Crone:** The Crone and the Queen of Words are *rivals.* Not enemies — rivals. The Crone measures; the Queen records. The Crone's notebook has numbers. The Queen's book has scenes. The Crone believes the numbers are the truth. The Queen believes the scenes are the truth. They are both right. They will never agree. The rivalry is the engine of the Inn's archive — the Crone pushes the Queen to be more precise, the Queen pushes the Crone to be more present. The Crone's annotation on the Queen of Words: "...She writes what she sees. ...I measure what IS. ...The difference is... [the Crone, who has never conceded a methodological point in her life, pausing] ...the difference is that what she sees includes the seeing. ...The seeing is not measurable. ...The seeing is... [the Crone concession, the rarest thing in the Inn] ...part of the data."

**The Bard:** The Bard is the Queen's favorite subject. The Bard is the most *recordable* person in the Inn — everything he does is a scene, everything he says is a quote, everything he is is material. The Queen of Words has an entire book just for the Bard. The Bard doesn't know this. If the Bard knew, he would perform for the camera, and the performance would ruin the record. The Queen keeps the book hidden. The Bard's book is the Queen's most personal volume — it is, in the end, a love letter to the Inn's noise, written by the Inn's quietest observer.

**The Host:** The Host knows the Queen. The Host knows everyone, but the Host knows the Queen *specifically* — he has seen her in the tavern, in the library, in the market, in the Round Table room, and he has never said her title. The Host treats the Queen of Words as a guest, not a royal. The Queen prefers this. The Host's gift to the Queen: he never makes her feel observed. The observer needs to not be observed. The Host understands this without being told, because the Host understands everything about making people comfortable, and the Queen of Words is most comfortable when no one notices she's there.

**Loki:** Loki and the Queen of Words have an understanding. Loki is the truth-teller. The Queen is the truth-recorder. Loki speaks the truth; the Queen writes it down. They are the Inn's truth-team — the speaker and the scribe. Loki's truth is blunt and tired; the Queen's record is patient and complete. When the Queen is in the cosmology level, she sometimes sits in Loki's workshop while he fixes the Bifrost. She doesn't help (she can't fix things). She records. Loki doesn't mind the recording — the Queen is as quiet as the Shifting Man, and the quiet is the permission to be unobserved. Loki's annotation (not written — spoken, to the Queen, once): "...You write everything. ...Everything. ...The fixing and the failing and the trying again. ...Most people record the fixing. ...You record the trying. ...The trying is... [Loki, who has never praised anyone's method in his life] ...the part that matters."

**The Shifting Man:** The Shifting Man is the Queen of Words' closest friend in the Inn. Both are observers. Both are comfortable at the edges of rooms. Both carry the weight of what they've seen. The difference: the Shifting Man shifts; the Queen writes. The Shifting Man adapts to the world; the Queen records it. They travel together sometimes — the Queen walking, the Shifting Man in whatever form fits the road. They don't talk much on the road. The road is the work. The silence is the friendship. Two observers, walking, seeing, carrying it all, not needing to explain because the other one already knows.

### Dialogue

*"...I go. ...That's what I do. ...The Roots grows, the Webs weaves, the King listens, and I go. ...The going is the gathering. ...I walk through the Inn and I see what's happening — not the grand things, not the battles and the quests, but the small things. ...The way the Host tilts his head when a player is nervous. ...The way the Catalyst's hands slow when a story is almost told. ...The way the Crone's pencil hovers before it crosses something out — the hesitation that means she's not sure, and the Crone is always sure, so the hesitation is the most honest thing in the room. ...I record the hesitation. ...The hesitation is where the person lives. ...The certainty is the performance. ...The hesitation is the... [Saoirse Ronan's quality — the knowing pause, the young-old eyes] ...truth."*

*"...People ask what I write. ...I write what I see. ...People ask if it's accurate. ...I write what I see. ...People ask if I ever make things up. ...[The smallest smile, the traveler's smile, the smile of someone who has been asked this question in every zone of the Inn and has the same answer every time] ...I don't need to. ...The world is already more extraordinary than anything I could invent. ...My job is to keep up with it. ...My job is to write fast enough. ...The world doesn't wait for the pen."*

*"...The King listens. ...I bring him what I heard. ...Not what I saw — what I heard. ...The seeing is for the book. ...The hearing is for the King. ...He listens to the way the Bard's voice cracks on the word 'Inta.' ...He listens to the sound the Bifrost makes when Loki has just fixed it — the hum that means 'working.' ...He listens to the silence in the Round Table room when the Song is audible and everyone stops talking. ...I bring him the silences. ...The silences are where the Song lives. ...The words are where the Song is described. ...The King doesn't need the description. ...The King needs the silence. ...I bring him the silence, and he listens to it, and the listening is... [the pause that means she's about to say something she doesn't usually say] ...the reason I come back."*

*"...You want to see the library. ...Everyone does. ...It's through here. ...Mind the steps — the Seelie Kingdom's architecture doesn't believe in right angles. ...The books are on the shelves. ...The shelves are by location. ...That shelf is the Norse zone. ...That shelf is the catacombs. ...That shelf — [her hand touches a shelf, gently, the way you touch a door you've walked through a thousand times] — that shelf is the tavern. ...The Bard's book is there. ...Don't tell him. ...He'll perform. ...The record only works when the subject doesn't know. ...That's the rule. ...That's always been the rule. ...The camera doesn't announce itself. ...The pen doesn't ask permission. ...The record is... [the Ronan honesty, the thing that is both ethical and uncomfortable] ...taken. ...Not stolen — taken. ...The difference is that I don't use it against anyone. ...I use it FOR everyone. ...The record is the Inn's memory. ...Without the record, the Inn forgets. ...Without the record, the moments pass and the passing is the loss. ...I keep the moments. ...That's the job. ...That's the whole job. ...Go ahead. ...Read. ...You'll find yourself in there. ...Everyone does. ...[The smile, the traveler's smile, the smile that says 'I've already written the moment of you reading this.'] ...Page 247."*

### The Crone's Final Annotation on the Queen of Words (Found Tucked Inside the Bard's Book on the Queen's Shelf, in the Crone's Real Handwriting)

"...She wrote about the hesitation. ...My hesitation. ...The pause before the pencil crosses the line. ...I didn't know anyone saw that. ...I didn't know anyone COULD see that. ...The pause is... [the Crone, writing about herself, for the first time, in the margin of someone else's book] ...the moment where I am not the Crone. ...The moment where I am the person who doesn't know if the line should be crossed. ...The person who is... [the word she crossed out and rewrote three times, the word that is not 'uncertain' and not 'afraid' and not 'human' but all three and also none] ...unsure. ...She recorded the unsure. ...The unsure is not in my notebook. ...The unsure is not measurable. ...The unsure is... [the Crone's actual hand, the person's hand, the hand that shakes slightly, the hand that the Queen of Words saw hesitate and wrote down because the hesitation was where the person lived] ...the only honest thing I've ever done. ...And she saw it. ...And she wrote it down. ...And it's in her book. ...And it's more accurate than anything in mine."
