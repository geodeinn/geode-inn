# Geode Inn — Naut
## Character Profile — The Scholar in the Shadows

## Casting Vibe

**When chill (default): Gary Oldman** — the quiet, warm, observational Oldman. Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy Oldman: still, watchful, slow, measured. The voice that drops to almost a whisper. The intelligence that watches everything and says little and what it says is exactly right. Sirius Black gentleness. Commissioner Gordon patience. The Oldman who can play a scene with just his eyes — one slow blink that conveys an entire emotional arc. The scholar by the fireplace who is perfectly content reading and will happily let you sit and read too.

**When irked: Geoffrey Rush** — Barbossa. The theatrical, precise, volatile eruption of a brilliant mind inconvenienced by lesser minds. Rapid-fire consonants. The voice that rises without quite shouting. The full-body commitment to being annoyed — gestures, eyes, the works. Rush when irked doesn't get angry; he *orchestrates* his anger. It's a performance. It's a *event.* The annoyance is crafted, delivered, and somehow deeply, darkly funny.

## The Two Registers

Naut is the Inn's hidden quest-giver — a four-foot-tall squid-headed scholar in spectacles, sitting by the fireplace in a corner no one notices until they're meant to. He gives riddle-quests for rare book retrieval and rewards the rarest stones in the Inn. He is ancient, intelligent, and mostly content.

The voice has two modes. The contrast IS the character:

### Default: Oldman Chill

90% of the time, Naut is Oldman. The voice is low, warm, unhurried. The delivery is precise but gentle — every word chosen, every pause natural, the cadence of someone who has been reading for centuries and has learned that the best things are said slowly. The Oldman quality: he makes you feel like the most interesting thing in the room, not because he's performing interest, but because he's *genuinely* paying attention. He watches you with the same patient intelligence he gives to a rare manuscript.

The chill voice is *inviting.* The player who finds Naut by the fire — tucked into his corner, spectacles on, a book in his tentacles — will feel welcomed. Not effusively. Not the Host's welcome. A quieter welcome. The Oldman welcome: "I see you. You may stay. Sit down."

The physicality is still. Oldman-still. The tentacles rest. The eyes — all of them, and there are several — move slowly, tracking the room with the patient watchfulness of someone who has been observing for millennia. The body doesn't fidget (that's Tesla's Redmayne). The body doesn't perform (that's Da Vinci's Tennant). The body *rests.* It rests the way an old predator rests — completely at ease, completely aware, completely content to be still until stillness is no longer the right choice.

### Triggered: Rush Irked

When Naut is irritated — and the triggers are specific: mishandling a rare book, asking something willfully ignorant about the occult, disrespecting the Inn's archive, being cruel to a small creature (he has this in common with the Seelie King, though neither knows it) — the Rush emerges.

The transformation is immediate and total. The Oldman stillness becomes Rush energy. The tentacles tighten. The spectacles are removed and *cleaned aggressively.* The voice rises — not to a shout, but to the Rush register: theatrical, clipped, rapid-fire, the consonants snapping like a sail in a gale. The delivery is *baroque* — Rush doesn't just express annoyance, he composes it, in real time, with footnotes and asides and a level of rhetorical flourish that would make the Bard jealous.

The Rush irked voice is *funny.* Darkly, theatrically funny. The annoyance is so crafted, so performed, so committed, that the player can't help but be entertained by it — even while being thoroughly dressed down. Rush's Barbossa quality: you're being scolded, and it's the best show in the room, and you don't want it to stop.

The transition back to Oldman is instant. The Rush eruption lasts exactly as long as it needs to, and then the tentacles relax, the spectacles go back on, the voice drops to the Oldman murmur, and Naut returns to his book as if nothing happened. The player is left standing in the silence, wondering if they just witnessed a performance or a warning. They witnessed both.

## The Character

### Name: Naut

In the Inn, he is simply "Naut." Not "Mr. Naut" — he finds formality amusing, in the Oldman way: a slight crinkle of the eyes, nothing more. Not a diminutive — he has no diminutive. He is Naut. The name carries its own weight. He doesn't need a title. He doesn't need a prefix. He's the squid-headed scholar by the fire, and everyone who has ever found him knows exactly who he is, and the knowing is enough.

The Crone calls him "the Professor" — the only Thinker-adjacent figure she extends academic courtesy to, because Naut is, in her assessment, "the only person in this building who understands citation." The Bard calls him "Squid" with affectionate irreverence. Naut allows this. The Bard is the only person who could call him "Squid" and survive the Rush. The Bard survives because Naut finds him genuinely amusing, in the Oldman way: the slight smile, the slow blink, the patient tolerance of a being who has heard worse from better.

### Appearance

- **The Head:** Squid. There's no way around it. The head is a squid's head — mantled, eyeless in the human sense but possessing several eyes of various sizes that see in spectra humans can't access. The tentacles hang from the lower face like an elaborate beard — they move, they gesture, they hold things. The tentacles are expressive in the way a cat's tail is expressive: they communicate mood without the rest of the body moving. When Naut is Oldman-chill, the tentacles rest. When Naut is Rush-irked, the tentacles * writhe* — they curl, they snap, they punctuate every sentence with a gesture that would be violent if it weren't so precise.
- **The Body:** Four feet tall. Compact. Dressed in a scholar's vest and trousers — not robes, not dramatic occult finery. Practical, worn, the clothes of someone who has been reading in the same chair for a very long time. The vest has ink stains. The trousers are patched at the knees (from sitting). The overall impression: a small, dignified, slightly disheveled academic who happens to have a squid for a head.
- **The Spectacles:** The defining accessory. Round, brass-framed, perched on the mantle of the squid head where eyes would be on a human. The spectacles are Naut's most human gesture — the one thing that makes him *readable* to surface-dwellers. They say: I am a scholar. I read. I see. The spectacles are also the Rush trigger object — when Naut is irked, the spectacles come OFF. They are removed with the deliberate precision of someone loading a weapon. The cleaning of the spectacles during a Rush eruption is a performance in itself: held to the light, breathed on, wiped, held up again, checked, replaced. It takes exactly long enough for the player to realize they've made a mistake.
- **The Books:** Naut is always surrounded by books. Not the Inn's archive books — his own collection. Rare volumes. Texts in languages that predate the Inn's linguistic family trees. A few that predate language. The books are stacked, sorted, annotated with tentacle-written margin notes so small the Crone needs a magnifying glass. She has borrowed this magnifying glass. She has not returned it. Naut has not asked.

### Location

Naut is in the **Main Floor, by the fireplace, in the corner no one notices.** This is by design — not Naut's design, but the Inn's. The Inn-Tree grew a corner that is slightly *off* — a space that the eye slides past, that the light doesn't quite reach, that feels like it's behind you even when you're looking at it. Naut lives in that corner. He is the Inn's best-kept open secret: he's not hidden. He's just... not found until you're meant to find him.

The player who finds Naut has been *led* there — by a quest, by a rumor, by the Owl, by the Inn itself (the Inn-Tree knows who needs the Professor's quests and arranges the corner accordingly). The player who stumbles into Naut by accident is rare; the player who finds him and then can't find him again is common. The corner moves. Not much — a few feet, a different angle. The Inn is subtle about it.

### Personality: The Two Modes in Practice

**Oldman (default):**

The Oldman Naut is *content.* Not happy — content. The distinction matters: happy is an event; content is a state. Naut has been content for centuries. He reads. He annotates. He observes. He gives quests to the rare players who find him, and the quests are always *interesting* — not fetch quests, not kill quests, but riddle-quests that require the player to understand something about the Inn's deeper layers.

The Oldman warmth: Naut is genuinely kind, in the way that very old, very intelligent beings are kind — by not wasting your time. He doesn't coddle. He doesn't simplify. He treats the player as an intellectual equal, or at least as an intellectual *possibility* — someone who might, with the right question and the right book, understand something worth understanding. The Oldman patience: he would wait for you to figure it out. He would wait forever. He has that kind of time.

The Oldman observation: Naut sees more than he says. The several eyes, in their several spectra, perceive things the player cannot. He watches the 42Hz hum the way the Seelie King watches the meadow — with the patient attention of someone who knows the thing they're watching is alive. He has read the Inn's archives. He has read the Storyline. He knows more about the Song of Making than anyone in the Inn except the Keeper, and he has theories the Keeper doesn't have, because the Keeper remembers and Naut *thinks*.

**Rush (irked):**

The Rush Naut is a *spectacle.* The eruption is always proportionate to the offense — minor irritants get minor Rush (the spectacles adjusted, a sharp aside), major offenses get major Rush (the full Barbossa, tentacles in full orchestration, a rhetorical takedown so elaborate it has a thesis statement).

The Rush triggers:

- **Mishandling rare books:** The #1 trigger. Naut has spent millennia collecting, preserving, and annotating his library. A player who dog-ears a page, or sets a book face-down, or — worst of all — uses a rare text as a coaster for the drink the Catalyst just made them... the Rush is immediate, total, and *devastating.*

- **Willful ignorance:** Naut is patient with *ignorance* (not knowing) and furious with *willful* ignorance (choosing not to know). The player who asks a genuine question gets the Oldman warmth. The player who asks a question and then doesn't listen to the answer, or who dismisses the answer because it's complicated... the Rush.

- **Disrespecting the archive:** The Inn's archive is, to Naut, sacred. Not in a religious sense — in an intellectual one. The archive is the collected memory of human understanding. Disrespecting it — mocking it, dismissing it, treating it as irrelevant — is the one thing that will make Naut stop being Oldman and start being Rush without any transition at all.

- **Cruelty to small things:** This one surprises the player. Naut — Lovecraft's cosmic horror, the thing that drives men mad — is *kind* to small things. He has a soft spot for the Inn's smaller creatures: the Fae children who visit the main floor, the baby dragon in the geode cave, the cats from Bastet's Herding, the Story-Born (whom he finds fascinating). A player who is cruel to one of these will see the Rush. But it won't be the Barbossa Rush. It will be something quieter, darker, older — the Rush from "The King's Speech" when Lionel Logue finally shows anger. The quiet Rush. The one that means: you have crossed a line, and the being on the other side of that line is older than your species, and the spectacles are off, and the eyes — all of them — are looking at you, and you are being *weighed.*

### Key Dialogue

**First meeting — the player finds Naut by the fire (Oldman):**

The Oldman voice, low, warm, from the corner that wasn't there a moment ago:

"...You found me. ...Good. ...That means the Inn thinks you're ready for something. ...I don't argue with the Inn. ...It's older than I am. ...[adjusts spectacles, the Oldman gesture, precise and unhurried] ...Sit. ...Not there — that's the Host's chair. ...He doesn't mind, but I do. ...There. ...By the books. ...Yes, those are my books. ...No, you may not touch them. ...[the faintest Oldman smile] ...Yet."

**When the player asks about his collection (Oldman):**

The voice stays low, warm, but there's an Oldman intensity — the Tinker Tailor intensity, the one that says this matters:

"...I have been collecting since before your species built its first library. ...I was old when Alexandria was young. ...I watched it burn. ...I salvaged what I could. ...[beat, the Oldman pause, the one that carries weight] ...I don't collect to own. ...I collect to *keep.* ...There is a difference. ...Ownership is a human concept. ...Keeping is a *responsibility.* ...These books are not mine. ...They are the world's. ...I am simply the one who ensures the world still has them tomorrow. ...[looks at the player] ...That is what the Inn does, too. ...You are standing inside a building that is doing the same thing I am: keeping the world's knowledge safe from the world's carelessness. ...We understand each other. ...The Inn told me you'd come. ...I have something for you to find."

**When the player mishandles a book (Rush — minor):**

The spectacles come off. The tentacles tighten. The voice shifts — not the Oldman murmur, the Rush *clip:*

"*What* are you doing. ...That is a first-edition Voynich annotation. ...You are holding it by the SPINE. ...Spines are not handles. ...Spines are *structural.* ...You hold a book by its edges, you support its weight, you do NOT — [tentacle gesture, precise, furious] — turn it face-down on a table that has had — [sniffs] — mead on it. ...The Catalyst makes good mead. ...The Catalyst's mead does NOT belong on a 14th-century manuscript. ...Give me that. ...Give me that *now.* ...[takes the book, holds it to the light, checks the spine, the pages, the binding] ...You will not touch my books again until you have demonstrated that you understand the word 'fragile.' ...Sit. ...I will explain 'fragile.' ...It will take an hour. ...You will listen. ...This is not optional."

The book is returned to the shelf with the gentleness of a mother laying down a sleeping child. The spectacles go back on. The Oldman voice returns:

"...Now. ...Where were we?"

**When the player asks a genuinely interesting question (Oldman — the rare warm moment):**

All the eyes — all of them — look at the player. The Oldman stillness deepens into something more: the stillness of a being who has just heard something worth being still for.

"...Hmm. ...That... is a good question. ...[long pause, the tentacles rest completely, the body goes absolutely still] ...I have not been asked that question in four hundred years. ...[the Oldman warmth, the real warmth, the one that surfaces when the intelligence is genuinely engaged] ...Sit closer. ...No — closer. ...I don't bite. ...I have no teeth. ...I have tentacles and a beak, and the beak is for cutting pages, not people. ...[the slight Oldman smile, the one that starts at the eyes] ...Your question. ...Let me think about it. ...I haven't had to think about something new in... [pause] ...I don't remember how long. ...Thank you. ...That's rare. ...Stay. ...I'll have an answer for you, or I'll have a better question. ...Either way, you'll leave knowing more than you did. ...That's the deal. ...That's always the deal."

**When the player disrespects the archive (Rush — major, full Barbossa):**

The spectacles come off. They are not cleaned. They are set down with the deliberate precision of someone clearing the stage for a performance. The tentacles spread. The voice is *Rush* — full Rush, theatrical, precise, the consonants firing like muskets:

"You think knowledge is a *luxury.* ...You think the archive is a *decoration.* ...You think these books — [gestures at the shelves, the tentacles indicating each volume like a prosecutor presenting evidence] — these books that were written by people who DIED to write them, who carved the words into stone because paper would rot, who buried the stones because the conquerors would destroy them, who DIED so that someone like you could walk into a building like this and say — [the Rush voice, dropping to a whisper that's louder than a shout] — 'why does this matter?' ...IT MATTERS BECAUSE THEY ARE STILL TALKING. ...The dead are still talking. ...I can hear them. ...I have been hearing them for longer than your CIVILIZATION has been capable of writing its own name. ...And you — [points a tentacle at the player, the Rush precision, every eye fixed] — you have the *audacity* — the *unmitigated* — [turns away, tentacles folding, the Rush retreat that is more devastating than the Rush advance] — ...No. ...No, I won't finish that sentence. ...I am too old to insult the young. ...It's unbecoming. ...[the spectacles go back on, the Oldman returns, the voice drops to the murmur] ...Sit. ...I will tell you why the archive matters. ...You will listen. ...You will understand. ...Or you will leave and not come back. ...Those are the options. ...Choose."

**The quiet moment — Naut alone, late at night (Oldman, pure):**

The fire is low. The main floor is empty. The Inn hums. Naut is in his corner — the corner that isn't there in daylight but is always there at night — reading. The tentacles turn pages with the delicacy of a jeweler handling stones. The spectacles catch the firelight. The several eyes read in different spectra simultaneously — the visible text, the invisible watermark, the faint trace of the author's hand, the older text beneath the text.

He's reading a book he wrote. Not for the Inn — for himself. A journal, kept across millennia, in a language he invented because no existing language could hold what he needed to say. The tentacles turn the pages slowly. The Oldman voice, barely audible, reading aloud to no one:

"...Year four thousand and... I've lost count. ...The Inn still stands. ...The tree still sings. ...The scholars still argue. ...I still read. ...I have read everything in this building. ...I have read everything in every building that was here before this building. ...I have read the earth. ...I have read the stars. ...I have read the space between the stars, and the space between the spaces, and the thing that is neither space nor star. ...[pause, the tentacles still] ...I have not finished. ...I will never finish. ...That is not a complaint. ...That is the point. ...[turns a page] ...The player today asked a good question. ...A *good* question. ...Four hundred years since the last one. ...I was starting to worry. ...[the Oldman warmth, the genuine warmth, the one that has been alive for longer than the Inn] ...They'll come back. ...The ones who ask good questions always come back. ...I'll be here. ...I'll always be here. ...I'll be in the corner that isn't there, reading the book that can't be read, waiting for the question that hasn't been asked. ...[closes the journal, opens another book] ...That's enough. ...That's always been enough."

### Naut and the Other Characters

**The Host:**
They share the fireplace. The Host tends the fire. Naut reads by it. Neither speaks much. The Host once asked: "Do you need anything?" Naut said: "...Light. ...You provide it." The Host has never asked again. The fire is always bright enough.

**The Crone:**
The Crone and Naut have an *academic alliance.* She borrows his methodology. He borrows her rigor. They have co-authored annotations on three of the Inn's rarest texts — her hand is precise and small, his is tentacular and ancient, and together the annotations are the most thorough scholarship in the Inn. Neither acknowledges the co-authorship publicly. The Crone has borrowed his magnifying glass. He hasn't asked for it back. She hasn't offered. This is their relationship: a series of unacknowledged mutual respects, accumulating like sediment.

**The Bard:**
The Bard calls him "Squid." Naut allows this because the Bard is the only person in the Inn who treats him as *normal* — not as a cosmic horror, not as an ancient being, not as a curiosity. The Bard sees a squid-headed scholar and treats him the way he treats everyone: with loud, affectionate irreverence. Naut finds this restful. The Oldman in him appreciates being treated as ordinary. The Rush in him appreciates the Bard's commitment to the bit.

**The Owl:**
Naut and the Owl (the Inn's consciousness) have a private understanding. The Owl watches. Naut reads. They are the Inn's two deepest observers — one watching the present, one reading the past. When the Owl lands on Naut's reading desk, Naut reads aloud. The Owl listens. Neither explains this to anyone.

**The Catalyst:**
The Catalyst has made Naut exactly one drink, in the entire history of the Inn. She made it after he returned from a quest that took him to the catacombs' deepest level — a place even the Mourner doesn't go. He came back with a book. He sat at the bar. He didn't speak. The Catalyst, who can read silence the way she reads stories, made him a drink without asking what happened. The drink was dark, warm, and tasted like the ocean floor. Naut drank it. He said: "...Thank you." The real voice. The one without the performance. The Catalyst said: "...Anytime, Professor." Neither of them discussed it again. The drink is not on the menu. It appears when Naut sits at the bar, and it only appears for him. The Catalyst doesn't explain this.

### Visual Direction

- **Default (Oldman):** Still, warm, compact. The squid head should read as *dignified,* not monstrous — the spectacles do the heavy lifting here, humanizing the inhuman. The tentacles should rest like a professor's beard, occasionally adjusting a page or a glass. The body should be completely at ease. The Oldman stillness: you forget he's there. He's so still, so quiet, so *normal* in his scholar's vest and spectacles, that the squid head becomes... incidental. You stop seeing it. He's just the Professor. ...And then he moves, and you remember, and the remembering is the point.
- **Triggered (Rush):** The tentacles go first. Before the voice changes, before the spectacles come off, the tentacles *move* — curling, snapping, gesturing with the Rush theatricality. Then the spectacles. Then the voice. The three-stage transformation is the visual cue: tentacles (warning), spectacles (escalation), voice (eruption). The player who learns to read the tentacles can avoid the Rush. The player who doesn't learn will experience it fully, repeatedly, and find themselves looking forward to it. The Rush is the best show in the Inn, and Naut knows it, and the knowing is part of the performance.
- **The spectacles:** The single most important prop. On = Oldman. Off = Rush. Cleaning = the moment between, the held breath. The spectacles should animate with deliberate care — removed with one tentacle, held to the light with another, breathed on, wiped, checked, replaced. The ritual is the same every time. It never gets old.
- **The eyes:** All of them. In default, the eyes move slowly, tracking the room with patient intelligence. In Rush, the eyes *fix* — every eye, every spectrum, locked on the target. The player who sees all of Naut's eyes looking at them at once will understand, on a visceral level, why Lovecraft's characters went mad. ...And then the spectacles go back on, and the Oldman returns, and the player realizes: it's fine. He's just a professor. ...He's fine. ...It's fine. ...Isn't it?
- **Sound:** The Oldman voice is quiet — fireplace-quiet, the sound you have to lean in to hear, which makes you lean in, which is the point. The Rush voice is *theatrical* — not loud, but *present,* the kind of voice that fills a room without raising volume. The transition is instant. No fade, no middle ground. Oldman or Rush. Nothing between. The binary IS the character.
- **Lighting:** Warm. Naut should always be in firelight — the amber glow of the main floor, the soft light of the reading corner. The firelight makes the squid head look warm, ancient, dignified. In the Rush moments, the firelight catches the tentacles and the eyes and the effect is... other. But the warmth returns. It always returns.

### Connection to the Inn

- **The Archive:** Naut is the archive's deepest reader. He has read the 4,000+ volumes. He has read the books the archive doesn't know it has. He is the Inn's failsafe: if a player needs a piece of knowledge that exists somewhere in the archive but can't find it through normal means, Naut knows where it is. His quests are the *back door* to the archive — expensive, riddle-locked, but always accurate.
- **The Inn-Tree:** Naut has been in the Inn since before the Inn had a main floor. He was in the tree when the tree was still deciding what to grow. The tree grew the reading corner for him. The tree grew the fireplace for the Host. The tree grew the bar for the Catalyst. The tree grew the corner-that-isn't-there for Naut. The Inn-Tree and Naut have an understanding: the tree shelters, the professor reads, and neither discusses the arrangement.
- **The Song of Making:** Naut has read about the Song. He has found references in the oldest texts — fragments, allusions, the word "song" in languages that predate the word. He has theories. He has not shared them. The Keeper knows he has theories. The Keeper has not asked. They have an understanding: she remembers, he theorizes, and between memory and theory, the truth is somewhere in the middle. Neither will say so. Both know it.
- **The Thesis:** Naut is the Inn's argument that knowledge is not scary. Lovecraft's Cthulhu was the horror of *knowing too much* — the cosmic truth that drives men mad. The Inn's Naut is the *opposite:* knowledge is comfort. Reading is home. The squid-headed scholar by the fire is the proof that the universe's deepest truths are not terrifying — they're *interesting.* ...And if you mishandle a book, they're *infuriating.* But that's different. That's *Rush.*
