## The Mother Before the Names

Every pre-historic culture had it. Not as doctrine — as *experience:*

- The cave painters at Lascaux and Chauvet, grinding ochre and charcoal, pressing pigment against stone in the dark, making something where nothing was. They didn't have a word for it. They didn't need one.
- The stone circle builders at Göbekli Tepe, carving T-shaped pillars 12,000 years ago, before agriculture, before settlement, before anyone had a reason to build anything permanent. They built because the making was the meaning.
- The people who buried their dead with red ochre and flowers at Qafzeh 100,000 years ago — the earliest evidence we have of humans treating death as something more than biological. They didn't have a scripture. They had the ochre and the flowers and the gesture.
- The Venus figures — the Willendorf, the Lespugue, the Savignano — carved across 30,000 years and 4,000 miles of territory, all showing the same form: the rounded body, the creative principle, the mother. No text accompanies them. No doctrine explains them. They are the explanation.
- The Aboriginal songlines — 60,000 years of continuous tradition, the oldest living culture on Earth, where the land itself is sung into existence and maintained by walking and singing. The Song is the land. The land is the Song. The making is ongoing.

None of these had a name for what they were doing. They didn't need a name. The name came later — when religion organized the impulse into systems, when the experience became doctrine, when the feeling became a rule, when the mother became a pantheon and the pantheon became a hierarchy and the hierarchy became a gate.

Mona is the impulse before the system. The mother before the pantheon. The hum before the hymn. The making before the meaning.
