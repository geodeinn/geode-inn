## PHASE 0 — THE HAZARD LEDGER (read first, always)

Cocobolo is the most dermatologically treacherous wood in common cabinetmaking. Its dust is a strong sensitizer: reactions escalate with each exposure and never un-learn. Yew (worked extensively, unprotected, in the past) is cardiotoxic — taxine alkaloids — and its dust is also a sensitizer. The record so far is lucky. Luck is not a protocol.

**The Cocobolo Law (this project's version of the Toll Booth):**
1. **Respirator, not dust mask.** A proper half-face respirator with P100/P3 organic-vapor-rated cartridges for ALL cutting and sanding, every time, no exceptions, no "just one pass."
2. **Dust extraction at the tool.** Shop vac with HEPA bag at every sanding/cutting station. Hand-planing and scraping only outdoors or with strong cross-ventilation. (Cocobolo planes beautifully and makes coarse shavings — favor the plane over the sander; shavings don't fly like dust.)
3. **Skin protocol:** long sleeves, gloves for handling rough stock, wash immediately after every session, launder shop clothes separately.
4. **Sweep never.** Dry-sweeping throws settled dust back into the air. HEPA vacuum only.
5. **Finishing note:** cocobolo's natural oils mean it barely needs finish — burnishing with a cloth and its own oil is traditional, which conveniently removes most reactive finishing chemistry from the project. One less hazard lane.
6. **The Bob Doctrine applies:** the dust collector starts as Bob. If it fails you in a cloud of rosewood dust, it gets demoted on the spot.

## PHASE 1 — FOUNDATIONS (months 0–4)

**The workbench.** The first real project is always the bench. A laminated-strip workbench top (maple or beech, cocobolo's hazards argue for a non-sensitizing bench wood), legs and stretchers, a proper face vise and tail vise. Built flat, tuned flat, trusted forever.

**Sharpening as a religion.** Scary-sharp method: granite plate or float glass + sandpaper grits through 800+, then strop. Hand tools only work when they're genuinely sharp; palm-carving taught the touch, this builds the edge. A honing guide until freehand is trustworthy.

**Tool additions (the honest short list, nothing exotic):**
- Low-angle jack plane (the one-workhorse plane: roughing, smoothing, shooting)
- Shoulder plane (rabbets, tenon cheeks, truing)
- Card scrapers + burnisher (cocobolo's finish; scraper = dust-free smoothing)
- Two decent panel saws (rip + crosscut) or a good bandsaw if the budget allows — bandsaw is the safer cocobolo cutter (coarse waste, less airborne dust)
- Mortise chisels (1/4" + 3/8") + firmer chisels
- Marking gauge, cutting gauge, try square, winding sticks

## PHASE 2 — SKILL LADDER (months 3–12, overlapping)

Each rung is a small project that teaches one joint; nothing goes on the clock until it's been proved on scrap:
1. **Flat/square/true:** dimensioning a board entirely by hand. The hardest and most important skill; the whole case depends on it.
2. **Mortise and tenon:** small tables, stools, a tool tote. This is the case's skeleton.
3. **Panel glue-ups:** cocobolo doesn't glue readily (the oils fight adhesive). Protocol: fresh-scrape both surfaces immediately before glue, standard PVA (Titebond III) or epoxy for oily-wood joints, pins to hold while clamped. Practice on offcuts first. Wide case panels get made from narrower rift/quartersawn boards.
4. **Dovetails:** the drawer (one drawer in the trunk, traditional). Hand-cut, through, visible. The badge project.
5. **Scraping/burnishing:** cocobolo's finish. Learn to raise a burr and take gossamer shavings.

## PHASE 3 — THE MOVEMENT DECISION (make by month 6)

| Option | What | When |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Kit movement now** | Hermle or comparable cable-driven mechanical movement (Westminster or triple chime), pendulum + weights included. ~$300–600. Runs the case as soon as it's built. | Recommended first |
| **B. Wooden movement** | Clayton Boyer's wooden-gear clock plans (wooden escapement, weight-driven). The honest Fizgig path; the movement itself is a second apprenticeship. | The chameleon circuit, later |
| **C. Both** | Case + kit movement first; build the wooden movement as a separate quest while the case seasons. Swap when ready. | The TARDIS gets flying quickly, upgrades in a later regeneration |

**Ruling on file: C is the plan.** The case is the two-year project; the movement is a swappable organ. No need to master escapement theory before the case exists.

## PHASE 4 — THE CASE (months 12–24)

**Form:** traditional English longcase proportions, 76–84" tall. Three sections: hood (the head), trunk (the body, with the door), plinth (the base). The movement hangs on a seat board in the hood; the trunk houses weights + pendulum; the door is the face of the whole thing.

**References to study (not copy, study):**
- Klockit (movements, dials, chime rods, hands — the standard supplier kit)
- Traditional longcase plans: Woodsmith/Gary Rogowski's longcase build; John N.APIA-free "how to build" folklore: museum photos of 18th-c. English cases — the proportions are the lesson
- Ron Walters' "The Grandfather Clock" case plans (a common public reference)

**Wood strategy (the budget truth):** solid cocobolo for the whole case would be extremely costly and heavy (cocobolo is dense enough to sink; an all-cocobolo longcase would weigh like a safe). Traditional practice: primary show wood outside, secondary wood inside. Recommended: solid cocobolo for the door frame, hood faces, plinth show surfaces, and trim; stable secondary (poplar or quarter-sawn pine) for the interior carcase; cocobolo veneer or solid for show panels. **CITES note:** cocobolo is CITES Appendix II — buy only from certified/licensed dealers with paperwork. Keep the paperwork; it travels with the clock.

**The dial:** brass or painted dial from Klockit, OR the jeweler's move — a hand-made dial. Achroite/tinted mother-of-pearl numerals are a real dial-inlay tradition. This is where Soulstice joins the project; the clock face can be stone-set. (Parked: a beaded bezel chapter ring.)

## PHASE 5 — THE TUNING (concurrent with Phase 4)

"Beautifully tuned" is a designable property, per the IEM engineering law:

1. **The chime chord.** Chime rods (vs. tubes: rods are mellower, tubes louder/brighter) are selected to a chord. Westminster is standard; other songs and chords are specifiable. Choose the chord first; it sets the clock's voice.
2. **The case is a soundboard.** Cocobolo is a true tonewood (luthiers use it for guitar backs; marimba bars). Case design choices that shape the voice: back-panel thickness (thin = resonant), back-panel mounting (floating vs. screwed — floating hums longer), bracing placement, gaps around the hood. A sealed heavy case thuds; a tuned case rings warm. Listen to prototypes with scrap-panel mock-ups before committing.
3. **The tick.** Pendulum + crutch + escapement character. The tock of a well-tuned longcase in a tonewood case is a struck marimba key, not a knock. Depth of tock is adjustable (weight of pendulum bob, escapement geometry on the kit).
4. **Sealed vs. ported:** the closed-box debate applies here, scaled up. The trunk's door gap acts as a port; door seal strips change the low end. This is a genuinely fun tuning surface, and entirely reversible.

**TARDIS cues (the joke that isn't a joke):**
- **Lit interior:** interior LED strip (warm, Steady-Light sine-clamped — no flicker, candle-class only) that comes on when the door opens. The console room reveal.
- **Bigger on the inside:** a small diorama room in the trunk or a hidden panel — a lit miniature scene behind glass. The Inn's own micro-parlor inside the clock. Open the door: a world.
- The chameleon circuit: swap kit movement → wooden movement in a future regeneration (Option C).
- Door lock or catch shaped like a tiny key (the TARDIS key). Cast from brass or carved bone.
