# Geode Inn — The Poison Garden
## Alnwick, Northumberland, England | Celtic Region Zone

## Overview

The Poison Garden is based on the real Alnwick Garden Poison Garden in Northumberland, England — home to ~100 of the world's most toxic, intoxicating, and narcotic plants. Behind black iron gates bearing a skull and crossbones and the warning "These plants can kill." Visitors require a safety briefing. No touching, tasting, or smelling. People have fainted from the fumes alone.

In the Geode Inn, the Poison Garden is a zone in the Celtic region — a walled garden within the Inn's grounds where every plant can kill, cure, or transform. It is the most beautiful place in the Inn. It is also the most dangerous. Both of these things are the point.

## Design Philosophy: The Dose Makes the Song

The Poison Garden is the Geode Inn's alchemical heart expressed in botany. Every plant in the garden has a dual nature — poison and medicine, death and healing, beauty and danger. The principle is Paracelsus's: *"The dose makes the poison."* In the Inn's language: every note of the Song can heal or harm depending on how it's played.

The garden is not evil. The garden is *honest*. These plants were here before humans. They don't need us. They don't want us. They were growing in the earth, doing what they do, long before anyone called them "poison." The gardener's philosophy (from the real Alnwick): *"I feel like we're in their spaces. A lot of these plants might have been there before we came along."*

This is the same philosophy as the National Parks — the residents were here first. The Poison Garden is where that philosophy meets the alchemy staircase. The plants are the alchemical ingredients. The garden is the alembic. The player is the alchemist.

## The Garden

### Appearance

A walled garden — old stone walls covered in ivy (English ivy, which is itself mildly toxic, because of course it is). The gate is black iron, forged with a skull-and-crossbones motif, but the ironwork is also *beautiful* — vines and leaves worked into the design, because the person who made the gate understood that death and beauty share a forge. The gate is locked. It opens only with permission.

Inside: paradise. The garden is *gorgeous* — foxgloves in purple spires, laburnum trees cascading yellow flowers, belladonna with its glossy black berries, monkshood with its hooded violet blooms, opium poppies in blood-red. The colors are saturated, the growth is lush, the air is thick and sweet and slightly wrong. The amber lighting of the Inn filters through the canopy, making everything glow like a painting.

It's the most beautiful place in the Inn. It will also kill you if you touch the wrong leaf. Both of these things are visible at the same time. That's the design.

### The Rules (Game Mechanic)

When the player enters, they receive a briefing (from the Gardener NPC). The rules are simple:

1. **Do not touch.** Touching certain plants causes damage — a burn, a rash, a debuff. The giant hogweed causes blisters that persist for several in-game days. The monkshood causes a numbing effect that slows movement. The damage is not lethal — the Inn doesn't kill its guests — but it's unpleasant enough that the player learns to be careful.

2. **Do not taste.** Eating any plant in the garden (an option that exists, because the player should be *allowed* to make mistakes) causes escalating effects. A nibble of foxglove causes a heart-racing visual effect. A bite of belladonna causes a hallucination sequence (a brief, beautiful, controlled trip — the screen warps, colors shift, the player sees the garden as the plants see it: alive, aware, watching). A full mouthful of ricin causes the player to "die" — they wake up outside the garden gate, minus some inventory items, with the Gardener standing over them saying: "I told you not to taste."

3. **Do not smell.** Some plants emit fumes. The cherry laurel, when brushed against, releases cyanide gas — a visible greenish haze that causes dizziness (screen blur, audio distortion). The player who walks too close to the laurel hedge without covering their mouth (a game option) will stagger. The Gardener says: "The laurel doesn't want to hurt you. It just doesn't want to be touched. Everything in here has boundaries. Respect them."

The "don't touch / don't taste / don't smell" mechanic is the garden's core gameplay loop — a navigation puzzle where the player must move through a beautiful space *without interacting with it*. The temptation to touch is constant. The plants are gorgeous. The player wants to interact. The gameplay is restraint.

## The Plants

Each plant in the garden is a real species with real properties, adapted to the Inn's mythology. The player who learns the garden learns real botany — names, properties, historical uses, dangers. The garden is an education disguised as a game.

### Selected Plants (Non-Exhaustive)

**Foxglove (Digitalis purpurea)** — Purple spires. Beautiful. Contains digitalis, which is used in heart medication. The right dose saves a failing heart. The wrong dose stops it. In the Inn: foxglove is the plant that teaches the alchemy principle. The player who harvests foxglove (with the Gardener's permission, wearing gloves) can brew a heart tonic that restores health. The same brew, brewed wrong, stops the heart. The Gardener says: "Foxglove is the best teacher in the garden. It doesn't lie. The line between medicine and poison isn't a wall. It's a *dose*. Learn the dose."

**Monkshood / Wolfsbane (Aconitum napellus)** — Hooded violet flowers. Contains aconitine, a neurotoxin. In the Inn: monkshood is connected to the wolf myths of Europe — the plant was historically used to poison wolf baits. The Inn's monkshood hums at a frequency that calms aggressive NPCs. The player who brews monkshood oil can apply it to reduce the aggression of hostile creatures in the Fae Kingdom. The Gardener: "Wolfsbane doesn't kill wolves. It kills the *wolf* in people. The rage. The hunger. Sometimes that's the same thing."

**Belladonna (Atropa belladonna)** — Glossy black berries, purple flowers. Contains atropine. Historically used by women to dilate pupils for beauty ("bella donna" = beautiful lady). In the Inn: belladonna is the plant of the Cailleach (the Celtic Keeper) — the dark beauty, the winter queen. The player who eats belladonna (despite the rules) experiences the Cailleach's vision: they see the world as she sees it — cold, clear, stripped of illusion. It's a truth-serum trip. The Gardener: "Belladonna doesn't lie. She doesn't have to. She shows you what's actually there. Most people can't handle what's actually there."

**Ricin / Castor Bean (Ricinus communis)** — The world's most poisonous plant (Guinness). A single seed can kill. In the Inn: ricin is the garden's deepest lesson — the most dangerous thing in the most beautiful place. The player cannot harvest ricin. The Gardener won't allow it. The ricin plant is there to be *seen* — to exist as a reminder that the most deadly things are sometimes the most unremarkable in appearance. The castor bean is a plain-looking plant. Nothing special. Nothing beautiful. And it will kill you deader than anything else in the garden. The Gardener: "Everything in here has a warning. Color, smell, thorns. Ricin doesn't warn. Ricin just waits. That's the real danger in this garden. Not the things that announce themselves. The things that don't."

**Laburnum (Laburnum anagyroides)** — Cascading yellow flowers. Contains cytisine. The second most poisonous tree in the UK. In the Inn: laburnum is the garden's trap — the most beautiful tree in the garden, and the most dangerous. A branch that falls to the ground is still deadly months later. The player who picks up a laburnum branch (it looks like a perfect walking stick) suffers a slow, creeping paralysis. The Gardener: "The laburnum is the garden's test of greed. It's the most beautiful thing here. You want to take a piece of it home. Don't. The beauty IS the trap. That's not a metaphor. That's botany."

**Cherry Laurel (Prunus laurocerasus)** — Common hedge plant. Contains cyanogenic glycosides. When trimmed or crushed, releases cyanide gas. In the Inn: the cherry laurel hedge surrounds the garden's inner sanctum. The player who pushes through the hedge (instead of finding the gate) is hit with a cloud of cyanide — greenish, sweet-smelling, dizzying. It's not lethal, but it drops the player to their knees. The Gardener: "The laurel is the gatekeeper. Not the iron gate — the laurel. The gate can be opened. The laurel cannot be negotiated with. It doesn't care who you are. It will gas you just the same."

**Giant Hogweed (Heracleum mantegazzianum)** — Enormous umbel flowers. Sap causes severe phytophotodermatitis — blisters that worsen with sunlight exposure. In the Inn: the giant hogweed is the only plant the Gardener approaches in a full hazmat suit. It's the garden's boss — not a fight, but a presence. The hogweed is huge, its flower heads eight feet tall. It's magnificent. It's also the most physically dangerous plant in the garden. The player who brushes against it in sunlight gets blisters that persist for three in-game days. The Gardener: "The hogweed is the only thing in here I'm afraid of. Everything else, I respect. The hogweed, I fear. There's a difference. Respect means I understand it. Fear means it doesn't care if I understand."

**Yew (Taxus baccata)** — Ancient tree, dark green, red berries (the aril is edible, the seed inside is deadly). Yew is used in cancer treatment (taxol). In the Inn: the yew is the garden's oldest resident. The yew tree in the garden is ancient — older than the walls, older than the gate, older than the garden itself. It was here first. The garden was built around it. The yew is the Cailleach's tree — the tree of death and rebirth, the tree that lives for millennia and regrows from its own dead wood. The yew's wood was used for longbows (English history) and for divination (Celtic tradition). The player who sits under the yew and listens hears a heartbeat — 42 Hz. The yew is a node of the Song. The oldest living node. The Gardener: "The yew was here before the garden. Before the wall. Before the idea of 'garden.' I built the garden for the yew, not the other way around. It doesn't need me. I need it."

**Opium Poppy (Papaver somniferum)** — Red flowers. Source of opium, morphine, heroin. In the Inn: the opium poppy is the garden's dream-plant. The player who is permitted to harvest poppy resin can brew a tincture that allows access to the "Remembrance Vision" mechanic — the Inn's system for viewing past lives (connected to the reincarnation narrative spine). The poppy doesn't kill in the Inn. It *opens*. The Gardener: "The poppy doesn't poison the body. It poisons the boundary between sleeping and waking. Sometimes you need to cross that boundary. Sometimes you don't. The poppy doesn't decide for you. It just... opens the door."

**Angel's Trumpet (Brugmansia)** — Pendant flowers, intoxicating scent. Contains scopolamine, hyoscyamine. In the Inn: the angel's trumpet is the garden's voice. The flowers, when the wind moves them, produce a low, resonant tone — 42 Hz. The entire garden hums, but the angel's trumpet is the loudest. The player who stands beneath the trumpet tree at night (in-game night) hears the Song clearly — not as a hum, but as a melody. The angel's trumpet is the garden's instrument. The Gardener: "If the garden sings, and it does, the trumpet is the lead. Everything else is harmony. Don't stand under it too long. The melody is... persuasive. People forget themselves."

## The Gardener

The Poison Garden is tended by **the Gardener** — an NPC who is not quite a Keeper but is more than a ranger. She is somewhere between the Keeper (Fibonacci) and the rangers of the National Parks: an expert, a custodian, and a person who has been changed by the thing she guards.

### Appearance

- Middle-aged, weathered, strong hands. Gardeners' hands — calloused, stained, capable.
- Practical clothing: boots, apron, gloves that she removes and replaces constantly. She wears a different pair of gloves for each section of the garden — the player who notices this realizes the gloves are the key: she knows exactly which plant requires which protection.
- She carries shears, a trowel, and a small glass vial of milk (milk is the historical remedy for several plant poisons — it binds to certain toxins. She knows this. She carries it the way the Keeper carries tea — for visitors, not herself).
- Her face is calm, attentive, and slightly amused — the expression of someone who has been working with things that can kill her for decades and has developed a philosophical relationship with danger.

### Personality

The Gardener is Smith — dry, direct, and quietly hilarious. She has the energy of someone who talks to plants more than to people and prefers it that way. She is not unkind. She is *precise*. Every word she says is measured the way she measures doses — exactly enough, no more.

She has a relationship with each plant. Not anthropomorphic — she doesn't pretend they're people. But she treats them as *entities* with preferences, boundaries, and moods. The monkshood "likes" the shade. The yew "doesn't care" about anything. The hogweed "is in a mood today." The player who spends time with the Gardener realizes she's not projecting personality onto the plants. She's *reading* them. They do have moods. They do have preferences. They are, in the Inn's mythology, conscious. Not human-conscious. Plant-conscious. Slow, chemical, seasonal. The Gardener is the only person who speaks their language.

### The Gardener and the Keeper

The Gardener and the Inn's Keeper (Fibonacci) have a relationship. The Keeper visits the Poison Garden — the only zone outside the Geode Cave where she goes. She comes at night, when the garden is closed. She sits under the yew. She doesn't speak to the Gardener. The Gardener doesn't speak to her. They sit. The yew hums. The angel's trumpet sways.

If the player is in the garden at night (a rare event — the garden is usually locked after dark) and sees the Keeper sitting under the yew with the Gardener, neither speaking, they have witnessed one of the Inn's quietest and most significant moments. The two women who tend the most dangerous things in the Inn — the Keeper tends the Song, the Gardener tends the poisons — sitting together in silence. The Gardener, if asked later: "She comes for the yew. Not for me. The yew remembers her. She remembers the yew. I'm just... here. Making sure nobody touches anything while they have their visit."

### The Gardener and the Alchemy Staircase

The Poison Garden is the physical extension of the alchemy Staircase. The Staircase connects physical matter to the divine; the Poison Garden is where that connection is expressed in *living matter*. Every plant in the garden is an alchemical ingredient. The player who learns the garden — who learns which plant heals, which harms, which transforms — has learned the alchemical principle: *everything is medicine. Everything is poison. The difference is you.*

The Gardener offers a crafting system: the player can harvest plants (with permission and proper tools) and brew tinctures, salves, and oils. Each brew has a dual effect — a small dose heals, a large dose harms. The crafting system is the alchemy Staircase's gameplay loop, expressed in botany.

### Dialogue Samples

- *(Entrance briefing):* "Welcome. Don't touch anything. Don't taste anything. Don't smell anything — yes, that includes the flowers, especially the flowers. The gate says 'these plants can kill.' The gate is not being dramatic. Stay on the path. Follow me. If you feel dizzy, tell me. If you feel wonderful, tell me faster — wonderful is usually worse."
- *(When the player touches the foxglove):* "I said don't touch. ...You're tingling, aren't you? That's the digitalis. It's touching your heart right now. Not metaphorically. Physically. Your heart rate just changed. It'll pass. Don't touch it again. The foxglove is the nicest plant in this garden and it will still teach you a lesson."
- *(When the player eats belladonna and experiences the vision):* The Gardener waits. When the player returns to normal: "You ate the belladonna. I saw. I didn't stop you because some people need to learn by doing. What did you see? ...The Cailleach. Yes. She shows herself to people who are ready. Or people who are foolish. Sometimes those are the same people."
- *(When the player asks about the yew):* "The yew is the oldest thing in the garden. Older than the walls. Older than the gate. Older than the idea of 'garden.' It was here when this was just a hill with a tree on it. I built everything around it. I work for the yew, not the other way around. ...It hums, if you haven't noticed. Low. Steady. The same note, always. I don't know what it's saying. I don't think it's saying anything. I think it's just... being. That's enough for a tree."
- *(When the player asks why she does this):* "Why do I tend a garden of things that can kill me? Because they were here first. Because they don't need me — I need them. Because every one of these plants is also a medicine, and the line between the two is the most interesting line in the world. ...And because someone has to. If I don't tend them, they tend themselves. They tend themselves right over the wall and into the village. I'd rather they stay in here with me. We have an arrangement."
- *(About the giant hogweed):* "Everything in here, I respect. The hogweed, I fear. Fear is not disrespect. Fear is the honest acknowledgment that something doesn't care about your respect. The hogweed will hurt you. Not because it's malicious. Because it doesn't know you're there. And it doesn't care to learn."
- *(At night, if the player catches her with the Keeper):* "She comes for the yew. I come for the rest. We sit. We don't talk. There's nothing to say that the yew hasn't already said. ...You should go. The garden at night is not the garden by day. The plants sleep. Some of them dream. You don't want to be in a dreaming plant's dream."

## Connection to the Inn

- **Alchemy Staircase:** The Poison Garden is the Staircase's living laboratory. Every plant is an ingredient. The crafting system (brewing tinctures) is the Staircase's gameplay.
- **The Cailleach (Celtic Keeper):** The belladonna is the Cailleach's plant. The yew is her tree. The garden is her *spring version* — the Cailleach is winter; the garden is what grows from winter's melt. The poison garden is the proof that winter doesn't just kill — it *grows* things. Dangerous, beautiful, necessary things.
- **The Song of Making:** The yew and the angel's trumpet are 42 Hz nodes. The garden is part of the Inn's resonance network. The plants don't just grow — they *hum*.
- **Modern Legends:** The real Alnwick Poison Garden (established 2005 by the Duchess of Northumberland) is itself a "modern legend" — a place that exists, that you can visit, that contains things that can kill you, that is open to the public with a warning and a briefing. It is the real-world version of the Inn's philosophy: dangerous knowledge, made accessible, with respect.
- **The Silica Thread:** Many of these plants grow in mineral-rich soil. The yew, in particular, roots deep into stone. The garden's connection to the earth's mineral network links it to the Silica Thread — the plants are the biological expression of the geological thread. Quartz in the ground becomes mineral in the soil becomes toxin in the plant becomes medicine in the body. The Silica Thread runs through botany, not just geology.

## Visual Direction

- The garden is the Inn's most saturated, most colorful zone — deep greens, purple foxgloves, yellow laburnum, red poppies, black berries, white hellebores. The Cozy Gothic aesthetic at full bloom.
- The walls are old stone, mossy, warm-toned. The gate is black iron, beautiful and ominous.
- The lighting shifts with the garden's mood — amber in daytime, deeper amber at dusk, blue-green at night (when the plants "dream"). The angel's trumpet glows faintly at night.
- The yew tree is the visual anchor — dark, massive, ancient, occupying the center of the garden like a pillar in a cathedral. The garden's other plants are arranged around it the way the Inn's other zones are arranged around the Geode Cave.
- The Gardener's hazmat suit (for the hogweed) is the one deliberately ugly object in the garden — a visual reminder that the beauty has a boundary, and the boundary is safety.
