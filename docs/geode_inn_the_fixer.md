# Geode Inn — The Fixer
## The Celtic Mason Nobody Notices

---

## The Character

**Name:** Wrought. (Not his real name — a trade name. "Wrought" means worked, shaped, forged. Iron is wrought. Stone is wrought. The word means "shaped by labor." Wrought is what he does, not who he is. Nobody asks who he is. Nobody asks what he does. The Inn stands. That's enough.)

**What he is:** A mason. One of several masons in the Inn — the game has multiple stone-workers (the Stone Guard in the Bedrock Fortress, the builders of the catacombs, the architects of the stone circles). Wrought is one of them. He belongs to the trade. He carries the tools. He works the stone. But unlike the other masons, who are visible, ceremonial, architectural — Wrought is the FIXER. The repair man. The one who keeps things working. The one who tightens the loose stone, re-points the crumbling mortar, re-aligns the shifted lintel, re-seats the cracked step. The maintenance. The labor that keeps the structure standing but that nobody sees because the structure's STANDING is the proof that the work is working.

**What nobody notices:** The symbols. Wrought's clothing is Celtic-patterned — the knotwork, the interlace, the spirals that the Celtic zone wears as its aesthetic. But within the knotwork, hidden in the complexity of the interlace, are MASONIC SYMBOLS. The compass. The square. The plumb line. The level. The gavel. Each one incorporated into the Celtic pattern as "just another knot" — the compass is a circle within a spiral, the square is an angle within a lattice, the plumb line is a vertical thread in a vertical weave. The symbols are there. The symbols are visible. But they're so subtle, so integrated into the pattern, that the eye reads "Celtic knotwork" and moves on. The eye doesn't see the mason. The eye sees the pattern.

The tool handles are the same. Wrought's tools — a hammer, a chisel, a trowel, a plumb bob — have handles carved with the same Celtic-masonic patterns. The symbols are on the handles, where the hand grips, where the wood is worn smooth by centuries of use, where the stain and the sweat and the stone dust have made the carving almost — but not quite — invisible. You'd have to pick up the tool. You'd have to hold it. You'd have to look closely at the worn wood in the right light. Then you'd see: the compass in the chisel handle. The square in the hammer grip. The plumb line in the trowel shaft. The gavel in the mallet head. The tools are marked. The tools are SYMBOLS. But the tools are tools — they're used, not examined. Nobody examines a worn chisel handle. Nobody looks for meaning in a hammer grip. The symbols are hidden in the function.

This is the masonic tradition itself: meaning hidden in plain sight, visible to those who know how to look, invisible to those who don't. The masons built the cathedrals and carved their symbols into the stone, and the symbols were there for anyone to see, but only the masons knew what they were. Wrought is the Inn's mason. The Inn is his cathedral. The symbols are in his clothing and his tools. The player who looks closely — who EXAMINES the Celtic pattern, who PICKS UP the tool, who NOTICES the fixer — finds the symbols.

## What He Does

Wrought is always working. He's the Inn's constant maintenance — the labor that keeps the structure sound while everyone else is doing the dramatic things (composing the Song, fighting dissonance, mapping the cosmos, stealing sparkly things). The Inn is an enormous, ancient, living structure made of stone and root and crystal, and stone and root and crystal SHIFT. The Inn moves — the Canopy City sways at 50 BPM, the catacombs settle, the Bedrock Fortress expands, the redwood roots grow. The moving creates stress. The stress creates cracks. The cracks need repair. Wrought repairs them.

Wrought's daily work (never announced, never requested, never noticed):
- Re-pointing the mortar in the catacomb corridors (the underground river's humidity erodes the joints)
- Re-seating the crystal supports in the Prism's chamber (the Portal's vibration loosens the settings)
- Re-aligning the stone circle portals on the world map (the earth shifts, the circles drift, the portals need to be true)
- Tightening the Canopy City's platform fastenings (the 50 BPM sway loosens the joints over time)
- Repairing the alchemical staircase's stone treads (the Retort's experiments crack the steps)
- Re-seating the gargoyle sentinels (the gargoyles vibrate at their frequencies and slowly shift in their mountings)
- Filling the root fissures in the Redwood Cathedral's trail (the redwood roots grow and crack the path stones)

The work is invisible because the work SUCCEEDS — the Inn stands, the stones are true, the mortar holds, the crystals are seated, the portals are aligned. The proof of Wrought's work is the ABSENCE of problems. Nobody notices the fixer because the fixer's work is the reason there's nothing to notice.

## The Celtic-Masonic Symbols

The Celtic patterns on Wrought's clothing and tools are the Inn's Celtic zone aesthetic — the knotwork, the spirals, the interlace that Niamh's territory wears as its visual identity. But within the patterns, the masonic symbols are embedded:

### The Compass (in the Celtic spiral)

The compass is the mason's tool for drawing circles — the symbol of boundary, of the circle within which the mason works. In Wrought's Celtic pattern, the compass appears as a SPIRAL within a larger spiral — the center point of the spiral is the compass's pivot, the outer arc is the compass's reach. The spiral is Celtic. The spiral is also the compass. The two symbols share a geometry: the circle. The Celtic artist and the mason both work from a center, outward.

In the Inn's symbolic vocabulary: the compass is the CIRCLE — the reincarnation cycle, the 42Hz Song's circularity, the seasonal zones' return. The compass says: everything comes around. The fixer's work is circular — repair, erode, repair, erode, repair. The work comes around. The fixer doesn't fight the cycle. The fixer works WITHIN the cycle.

### The Square (in the Celtic lattice)

The square is the mason's tool for measuring right angles — the symbol of correctness, of things being TRUE. In Wrought's pattern, the square appears as a lattice within the knotwork — the right angles where the cords cross, the 90-degree turns in the interlace. The lattice is Celtic. The lattice is also the square. The two symbols share a geometry: the right angle. The Celtic artist and the mason both work from straight lines that meet correctly.

In the Inn's symbolic vocabulary: the square is the STANDARD — the 42Hz frequency, the correct resonance, the measure of harmony. The square says: things should be true. The fixer's work is measured against the square — the stone is true or it isn't, the mortar holds or it doesn't, the portal is aligned or it isn't. The fixer doesn't approximate. The fixer SQUARES.

### The Plumb Line (in the Celtic vertical)

The plumb line is the mason's tool for measuring verticality — the symbol of uprightness, of things being LEVEL with the earth's pull. In Wrought's pattern, the plumb line appears as the vertical threads in the knotwork — the straight-up-and-down cords that anchor the pattern to the ground. The vertical is Celtic. The vertical is also the plumb line. The two symbols share a geometry: the true vertical.

In the Inn's symbolic vocabulary: the plumb line is the RESONANCE PULSE — the 50 BPM heartbeat that runs from the underground altar to the lighthouse tower. The plumb line is the Inn's vertical axis — the Inn-Tree's trunk, the world's gravity, the frequency that keeps everything aligned. The fixer uses the plumb line to check the Inn's alignment — is the Inn still true to its heartbeat? Is the structure still plumb with the crystal's pulse?

### The Level (in the Celtic horizontal)

The level is the mason's tool for measuring horizontality — the symbol of equality, of things being EVEN. In Wrought's pattern, the level appears as the horizontal threads — the straight-across cords that connect the verticals. The horizontal is Celtic. The horizontal is also the level. The two symbols share a geometry: the true horizontal.

In the Inn's symbolic vocabulary: the level is the EQUILIBRIUM — the balance between dissonance and consonance, the harmony that the player maintains through the resonance combat. The level says: things should be even. The fixer's work keeps the Inn LEVEL — the floors even, the stairs balanced, the platforms horizontal. The Inn's physical levelness is the Inn's harmonic levelness made structural.

### The Gavel (in the mallet head)

The gavel is the mason's tool for shaping stone — the symbol of labor, of the WORK that transforms the rough ashlar into the perfect ashlar. In Wrought's tool, the gavel IS the mallet — the hammer he uses to tap stone into place. The gavel is not hidden in a pattern. The gavel is the tool itself. The mason's most visible symbol is Wrought's most used instrument. The hiding is in the FUNCTION — the gavel is so obviously a tool that no one looks for the symbol. The symbol is the tool. The tool is the symbol.

In the Inn's symbolic vocabulary: the gavel is the RESONANCE PULSE's impact — the moment the frequency meets the stone, the moment the tuning happens. The gavel is the combat — the DEPLOY phase, when the player's frequency hits the enemy's dissonance. The gavel shapes. The gavel doesn't destroy. The gavel TAPS the stone into its true shape. Wrought's gavel-tapping is the combat's thesis in miniature: gentle, repeated, patient taps that shape the stone, not blows that break it.

### The Rough Ashlar and the Perfect Ashlar

The masonic tradition centers on two stones: the rough ashlar (the uncut, raw stone — the natural state) and the perfect ashlar (the cut, squared, finished stone — the worked state). The mason's journey is the transformation of the rough into the perfect — through labor, through tools, through time.

In the Inn's vocabulary: the rough ashlar is the UNHARMONIZED enemy — dissonant, raw, unshaped. The perfect ashlar is the HARMONIZED companion — consonant, shaped, refined. The resonance combat is the masonic work: the player uses frequencies (tools) to transform the rough (dissonance) into the perfect (harmony). The fixer does this literally — he takes rough stone and works it into true stone, the same way the player takes rough dissonance and works it into perfect harmony.

Wrought carries a rough ashlar and a perfect ashlar in his tool bag — two small stones, one rough, one smooth. He uses them as reference: the rough stone is what the work starts with. The perfect stone is what the work aims for. The two stones are the fixer's PHILOSOPHY in his pocket. He doesn't explain this. He doesn't display them. They're in his bag, with the mortar and the chisels. But the player who looks in the bag (the player who NOTICES the fixer) sees the two stones and understands: the fixer is doing the same work the player is doing. The fixer is shaping stone. The player is shaping frequency. The work is the same.

## Wrought's Presence in the Inn

Wrought is everywhere and nowhere. He's the Inn's most ubiquitous NPC and its most invisible:

- He's in the catacombs, re-pointing a corridor, when the player walks past. The player sees a figure in the peripheral vision, hears the tap of a hammer, and the figure is gone — moved to the next repair. The player thinks: "Was someone there?" The corridor is sound. The mortar is fresh. Someone was there. The question fades.
- He's on the Canopy City's lower platform, tightening a fastening, when the player crosses the bridge. The player sees a figure crouched on a beam, Celtic-patterned clothing, a tool in hand. The player looks again and the figure has moved to the next beam. The platform is steady. The fastening is tight. The figure is already elsewhere.
- He's at the Prism, re-seating a crystal support, when the player enters. The player sees a pair of legs sticking out from behind the Portal's mounting. The player walks around to look and the legs are gone — the support is seated, the crystal is stable, the only evidence of the repair is the fresh mortar (still slightly wet — the fixer was JUST here).
- He's in the Redwood Cathedral, filling a root fissure, when the player passes on the trail. The player sees a crouched figure, hears the tap of a mallet, sees the Celtic pattern on a sleeve. The player blinks and the figure is gone — the fissure is filled, the trail is smooth, the redwood's root is stabilized.

Wrought's presence is always the same: a glimpse, a sound, a repair completed, a figure gone. The player never catches Wrought working. The player only catches Wrought's AFTER — the fresh mortar, the tightened joint, the re-seated crystal, the filled fissure. The work is the evidence. The worker is the ghost.

The player who WANTS to find Wrought (who is curious about the glimpses, who follows the sound of the mallet, who tracks the fresh mortar) can find him — but only if the player is PATIENT. Wrought doesn't hide. Wrought is simply working, and working is not interesting to watch (the way a repair is not interesting to watch — the work is slow, repetitive, unglamorous). The player who sits and watches Wrought work (the Nook Effect applies — the watching is a nook) sees: the care, the precision, the centuries of expertise in every tap. The watching is boring. The watching is the point. The watching is the Nook Effect: the deep work that happens when you sit and pay attention to something unglamorous.

## Wrought and the Inn's Characters

**The Host/Librarian (Patrick Stewart):** The Host knows Wrought. The Host knows everyone. But the Host never calls attention to Wrought — never introduces him, never praises him, never mentions him in conversation. The Host lets Wrought be invisible because the Host understands: the fixer's work requires invisibility. If the Inn noticed the fixer, the Inn would have to acknowledge that the Inn NEEDS fixing. The Inn doesn't want to acknowledge that it needs fixing. The Inn wants to be eternal, unchanging, perfect. The fixer is the reminder that the Inn is MAINTAINED, not eternal. The Host protects the fixer by not pointing at him. The Host says, once, to a player who asks about the maintenance: "...The Inn tends itself. ...[The pause — the Host knows this is not true, and the Host knows the player knows it's not true, and the Host says it anyway because the fixer deserves his privacy.] ...The Inn... [the correction, the Host being honest, the Host being the Host] ...is tended. ...By whom is not the question. ...That it is tended is... [the word] ...enough."

**The Crone (Anthony Hopkins):** The Crone sees the masonic symbols. The Crone sees everything. The Crone looks at Wrought's Celtic pattern and sees the compass in the spiral, the square in the lattice. The Crone says nothing. The Crone does not make a column for Wrought. This is different from the Duck (who has no column because the Duck doesn't fit). Wrought doesn't have a column because the Crone CHOOSES not to write him down. The Crone understands the masonic principle: the hidden stays hidden until the seeker is ready. The Crone, who measures everything, measures Wrought by NOT measuring him. The Crone's silence about Wrought is the Crone's highest respect — the respect of one measurer for another's privacy. The Crone, if pressed about Wrought, says: "...Some things are not for the column. ...Some things are for the stone. ...[The pen, tapping once.] ...He knows I know. ...I know he knows. ...The knowing is... [the word] ...enough. ...Leave him to his work."

**Leonardo (The Wanderer):** Leonardo's unfinished work is Wrought's TO-DO LIST. Wrought has been quietly completing Leonardo's projects for centuries — tightening the flying machine's frame (in the Canopy City), stabilizing the water organ's pipes (in the tidal caves), rebinding the anatomical atlas's loose pages (in the nooks). Leonardo doesn't know. Wrought doesn't tell him. The fixer fixes. The starter starts. The two never discuss the handoff because the two never acknowledge the connection. But the Inn works — the structures stand, the machines function, the pages hold — because Wrought finishes what Leonardo begins. The fixer is the Wanderer's invisible partner. The Wanderer starts; the fixer finishes. Neither knows the other's name for what they do. Leonardo calls it "curiosity." Wrought calls it "maintenance." The Inn calls it "standing."

**Gesso (The Ceiling Gnome):** Gesso and Wrought are the Inn's two invisible craftsmen — both always working, both never done. Gesso works UP (the ceiling, the visible, the decorative). Wrought works DOWN (the foundations, the invisible, the structural). Gesso adjusts details that no one sees from the floor. Wrought repairs structures that no one sees from ANYWHERE. Gesso grumbles (loudly, constantly, entertainingly). Wrought is SILENT. The two have never spoken. The two don't need to speak. The ceiling gnome and the stone fixer are the Inn's two halves of craft: the surface and the structure, the beautiful and the true, the paint and the mortar. Gesso makes the Inn look right. Wrought makes the Inn BE right. The Inn needs both. The Inn has both. Neither knows the other exists.

**The Duck:** The Duck doesn't steal from Wrought. This is notable — the Duck steals from everyone (the baby dragon's gems, the Cook's ladle, Gesso's gold leaf, the Crone's pen). The Duck doesn't steal from the fixer. The Duck doesn't steal from the fixer because the fixer's tools are WORN — the handles are smooth with use, the metal is dark with patina, the leather is soft with age. The Duck steals SPARKLY things. Wrought's tools are not sparkly. Wrought's tools are WORKED. The Duck, who perceives frequencies through electroreception, can sense that Wrought's tools carry a different kind of frequency — not the bright, high frequency of a crystal, but the DEEP, low frequency of a tool that has been used for centuries. The tools don't sparkle. The tools RESONATE. The Duck respects the resonance. The Duck leaves the tools alone. The Duck, sitting on Wrought's tool bag (the Duck sits on everything — it's a platypus), looks at the fixer with those round dark eyes and the fixer looks at the Duck and neither of them says anything and the Duck waddles away and the fixer goes back to work. This is the Inn's quietest relationship: the thief and the mason, connected by the understanding that some things are not for stealing and some things are not for fixing.

## What Wrought Is, Really

Wrought is the Inn's LABOR. Not the dramatic labor (the Song, the combat, the cosmology) — the MAINTENANCE labor. The labor that keeps things working. The labor that nobody sees because the labor's success is the absence of problems.

Wrought is the Inn's argument that MAINTENANCE IS CREATIVE. The fixer doesn't build — he REPAIRS. The fixer doesn't compose — he RE-TUNES. The fixer doesn't start — he FINISHES. The culture that values only the NEW, the ORIGINAL, the STARTED — that culture forgets the fixer. The Inn doesn't forget the fixer. The Inn HIRES the fixer. The Inn says: the world is full of starters. The world is full of geniuses who begin. The world is full of Leonardos who sketch and leave. The world needs the person who FINISHES. The person who TIGHTENS. The person who RE-POINTS. The person who keeps the structure standing while the starters are starting the next thing.

The masonic symbols in Wrought's clothing and tools are the Inn's masonic thesis: the rough ashlar becomes the perfect ashlar through LABOR. The labor is not glamorous. The labor is not visible. The labor is the tap of the mallet, the scrape of the trowel, the check of the plumb line. The labor is Wrought, crouched in a corridor, re-pointing mortar, while the player walks past on the way to something more interesting. The labor is the Inn's foundation — the work that makes everything else possible, that holds the structure up, that keeps the stones true, that maintains the alignment between the crystal's heartbeat and the lighthouse's beam.

The player who finds Wrought (who follows the fresh mortar, who tracks the mallet's tap, who sits and watches the fixer work) finds the Inn's secret: the Inn is not eternal. The Inn is MAINTAINED. The Inn stands because someone is always tightening, always re-pointing, always re-seating, always checking the plumb line. The Inn's permanence is not geological. The Inn's permanence is CRAFTSMANSHIP — the daily, invisible, unglamorous labor of a mason who carries the symbols in his patterns and the tools in his bag and the philosophy in his pocket (the rough ashlar and the perfect ashlar — the two stones that say: the work is the transformation, and the transformation never ends).

The fixer is the Inn's truest mason — not because he builds, but because he MAINTAINS. The Inn doesn't need another builder. The Inn needs someone to keep it standing. Wrought keeps it standing. Nobody notices. That's the point.
