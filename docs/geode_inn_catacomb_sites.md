## Overview

The US has a network of underground catacomb sites that rival European examples in architecture and historical significance. These map to the **Downstairs (Catacombs)** level of the Geode Inn as explorable world map pins.

All sites predate or sit at the Inn's pre-industrial cutoff (pre-1850s).

## Catacomb Sites

### 1. Old St. Patrick's Cathedral Catacombs — New York City, NY
- **Established:** Early 1800s (cathedral completed 1815)
- **Location:** Mulberry Street, Little Italy, Manhattan
- **Description:** 37 hermetically sealed family and group vaults arrayed along three 120-foot corridors beneath the cathedral. Marble facades, arched stone passages. The only Catholic catacombs in New York City.
- **Notable:** Sealed for over 200 years, rarely seen by anyone beyond clergy and descendants. Notable New Yorkers entombed including the Havey family and other prominent Catholic families of early NYC.
- **World Map Pin:** New York, NY
- **Inn Connection:** Rivals Paris catacombs in construction quality. Connects to the Inn's European architectural tradition brought to American shores.

### 2. Westminster Hall Catacombs — Baltimore, MD
- **Established:** 1786 (burying ground), 1852 (church built above)
- **Location:** 519 W Fayette Street, Baltimore
- **Description:** Church constructed on brick piers above existing graves, creating a network of catacomb passages beneath. Brick-arched corridors with sealed vaults.
- **Notable:** Final resting place of Edgar Allan Poe. Site of historical grave robbing. The catacombs became notorious as a gathering place for Baltimore's underground scene — illegal parties, occult rituals reported in the 19th century.
- **World Map Pin:** Baltimore, MD
- **Inn Connection:** Poe connection ties to the Inn's literary/archival nature. The occult gathering history connects to the modern legends nook.

### 3. Indianapolis Catacombs (Tomlinson Hall) — Indianapolis, IN
- **Established:** 1886 (Tomlinson Hall built)
- **Location:** Beneath City Market, Indianapolis
- **Description:** Brick-arched underground passages, the surviving ruins of Tomlinson Hall. The hall seated 3,500 people and burned in 1958, but the catacombs survived.
- **Notable:** After the fire, the underground structure was used as a shooting range by police and as shelter by homeless populations. Romanesque-style imported Italian stone construction.
- **World Map Pin:** Indianapolis, IN
- **Inn Connection:** Right at the pre-industrial cutoff. The layered history (public hall → fire → catacombs → repurposed use) mirrors the Inn's theme of spaces transforming over time.

### 4. St. Louis Cemetery No. 1 — New Orleans, LA
- **Established:** 1789 (Spanish royal decree)
- **Location:** Basin Street, New Orleans
- **Description:** Above-ground tomb city — not underground catacombs, but vaulted mausoleum streets built above ground due to the high water table. Row after row of elaborate stone tombs, family vaults, and society crypts.
- **Notable:** Oldest extant cemetery in New Orleans. Tomb of Marie Laveau (Voodoo queen). The above-ground design was mandated after bodies in ground graves were found to be the cause of an 1830 epidemic. Spanish colonial architectural influence.
- **World Map Pin:** New Orleans, LA
- **Inn Connection:** Unique American variant of catacomb architecture. Marie Laveau's tomb connects to the modern legends nook and the Inn's folklore framework. The water-table-forced design is a natural-world constraint shaping human architecture.

### 5. Unitarian Church Cemetery — Charleston, SC
- **Established:** 1772 (construction began), 1787 (first used)
- **Location:** Archdale Street, Charleston
- **Description:** Gothic churchyard cemetery with overgrown, disheveled decay aesthetic. Sealed vaults and above-ground tombs. The church was built by the Society of Dissenters (religious outcasts).
- **Notable:** Haunted by the subject of Edgar Allan Poe's "Annabel Lee" — a local legend connects the graveyard to Poe's time in Charleston. The Gothic decay is natural and uncultivated, unlike manicured historic sites.
- **World Map Pin:** Charleston, SC
- **Inn Connection:** The "Dissenters" origin connects to the Inn's theme of reclaiming power from persecutors. The Poe connection deepens the literary thread. The deliberate wildness of the decay fits the Inn's aesthetic.

### 6. Philadelphia Quaker Burial Grounds — Philadelphia, PA
- **Established:** 1700s
- **Location:** Arch Street Friends Burial Ground
- **Description:** Simple, unadorned Quaker burial tradition — a deliberate contrast to the ornate Catholic catacombs. Underground vaults used for simple interment.
- **Notable:** The Quaker approach to death — plain, equal, unornamented — represents a completely different American relationship to burial and the sacred.
- **World Map Pin:** Philadelphia, PA
- **Inn Connection:** The contrast with ornate catacombs is itself meaningful — the Inn presents multiple traditions on their own terms, not just the most dramatic ones.

## Ancient American Sites (Catacomb-Adjacent)

These are not catacombs but are ancient American sites that connect to the Smithsonian suppression narrative and should be mapped to the catacombs level as explorable pins.

### 7. Cahokia Mounds — Collinsville, IL
- **Established:** c. 600–1400 CE
- **Description:** Largest pre-Columbian settlement north of Mexico. Monks Mound is the largest earthwork in the Americas. Population estimated at 10,000–20,000 at its peak — larger than London was at the time.
- **Inn Connection:** Proof of complex, populous American civilizations long before European contact. The "mound builder" mystery — early Americans couldn't believe indigenous peoples built these, leading to the suppression narrative.

### 8. Watson Brake — Monroe, LA
- **Established:** c. 3500 BCE (5,400+ years old)
- **Description:** 11 earthen mounds — the oldest mound complex in North America. Older than the Egyptian pyramids. Built by hunter-gatherers, not agricultural people, which challenges assumptions about what kinds of societies build monumental earthworks.
- **Inn Connection:** Predates everything. Forces a rethink of what "primitive" means — exactly the kind of assumption-challenging content the Inn exists to preserve.

### 9. Poverty Point — West Carroll Parish, LA
- **Established:** c. 1700–1100 BCE
- **Description:** UNESCO World Heritage Site. Massive earthwork complex with concentric ridges. Built by a society without agriculture, pottery, or known writing — yet constructed geometrically precise earthworks spanning 345 acres.
- **Inn Connection:** Sacred geometry without known texts. The geometric precision connects to the staircase (alchemy/sacred geometry) level.

## The Smithsonian Suppression Narrative

### Context
In the decades after the Smithsonian's founding (1846), a narrative emerged that the institution suppressed evidence of giant skeletons and advanced ancient civilizations found in American burial mounds. The controversy centers on:

1. **The Mound Builder Myth** — Early European settlers refused to believe indigenous peoples built the massive earthworks found across the Midwest and South. They invented theories of a lost "ancient white race" or "giants" who built them, then vanished.
2. **Giant Skeleton Reports** — Newspaper accounts from the 1800s reported 7-8 foot skeletons found in mounds. Some were hoaxes, some were misidentified, some were real but not "giants" — just tall individuals.
3. **The Smithsonian's Role** — The Smithsonian actively debunked the "ancient white race" theory, arguing (correctly) that indigenous peoples built the mounds. However, in doing so, they also dismissed or failed to properly catalog some genuine finds, feeding the suppression narrative.
4. **The Cultural War** — The tension between "amateur" mound-diggers who found things and "professional" institutions who controlled the narrative is itself a modern legend about who gets to define history.

### Geode Inn Treatment
- The **actual mound sites** (Cahokia, Watson Brake, Poverty Point) go in the **Catacombs** as real ancient civilizations.
- The **suppression narrative** goes in the **Modern Day Legends** nook as folklore about how institutions shape (and sometimes distort) historical truth.
- The **giant skeleton claims** go in **Modern Day Legends** as a specific example of myth-making around American archaeology.
- The Inn presents both the real archaeology AND the legends around it, letting visitors draw their own conclusions.
