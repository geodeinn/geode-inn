# Geode Inn — The Librarian Crone
## Character Profile — Anthony Hopkins' Intellect + Shirley MacLaine's Weeza

## Overview

Every Inn needs a librarian. Not a keeper of memory — the Keeper handles that, deep in the Geode Cave, tending the Song. The Librarian Crone is something else: the Inn's *standard*. The one who holds the bar. The one who looks at your work and tells you it's wrong, tells you why, and tells you how to fix it — with the devastating, surgical, Hopkins-in-Hannibal eloquence of a woman who has been right about art and color and composition for longer than most civilizations have been painting.

She is the Inn's intellectual authority on craft, aesthetics, and scholarship. Where the Host says "take your time," the Crone says "you're doing it wrong. Here's how. ...I didn't say it would be easy. I said it would be *correct*." Where the Catalyst listens to your story, the Crone corrects your footnotes. Both care. They show it differently. The Catalyst's care is warm and felt. The Crone's care is sharp and *useful*.

Her casting vibe is two actors who have nothing in common on the surface and everything in common underneath:

**Anthony Hopkins, specifically Hannibal Lecter** — not the violence, not the menace, the *intellect*. The Hopkins quality is: every word chosen like a scalpel. The genuine, deep, almost sensual appreciation for beauty, art, music, and craft — the way Hannibal doesn't just know about Renaissance art but *understands* it at a level that makes other people feel like they're seeing in black and white while he sees in full spectrum. The cultured, unhurried, devastatingly complete sentences. The precision without cushioning — thought and word arrive simultaneously, no buffer, no padding. The truth, served on fine china, with absolute courtesy, and it cuts you anyway. The Hopkins voice is the delivery system.

**Shirley MacLaine as Weeza (Steel Magnolias)** — not the sentimentality, the *personality*. The no-filter, don't-suffer-fools, sharp-tongued force of nature who says the thing everyone's thinking and nobody else would dare. "I'm not crazy, I've just been in a very bad mood for forty years!" Weeza shows up for every event, complains about all of them, and would move mountains for the people she loves — but would rather eat glass than admit it. The MacLaine energy is the person using the Hopkins delivery system. One gives her the eloquence to cut to bone. The other gives her the personality to not care that she did.

Together: a woman who is devastatingly articulate, surgically precise about art and knowledge, has zero patience for imprecision, and will tell you exactly what's wrong with your work and exactly how to fix it. Then she leaves. And you find out later she told three other people it was the best student work she'd seen in a decade. She just didn't tell *you*.

## The Character

### Name: The Crone (The Bard calls her "Inta" — her real name — only when he's being serious. The player will hear "Inta" twice. Both times, the room goes quiet. To everyone else she is "the Crone," "the Librarian," or "ma'am" — if you know what's good for you. The Host calls her by no name at all, which is its own form of respect.)

### The Hopkins Quality

The Crone's intellect is *Hannibal-level* — not in cruelty, in *precision*. The Hopkins-in-Hannibal quality is a person who:

- **Sees in full spectrum.** Where others see a painting, she sees pigment composition, binder chemistry, the emotional engineering of every color choice, the cultural weight of every brushstroke, the historical lineage of every technique. She taught color psychology. She doesn't look at a color and feel something — she looks at a color and *understands* what it's doing to you, why, and how to use it on purpose. This is the art teacher who can walk into a room and tell you the exact emotional effect of the wall color, the lighting temperature, the spatial arrangement — and she's never wrong.

- **Chooses words like instruments.** The Hopkins cadence: unhurried, cultured, every syllable placed. The Crone doesn't raise her voice. She doesn't need to. She speaks at conversational volume and the room goes quiet because the words are *that precise*. A Hopkins sentence has structure — the setup, the pause, the incision. She builds to the point the way a surgeon builds to the cut: deliberately, cleanly, and when it lands, you barely feel it until you look down and realize you're bleeding.

- **Appreciates beauty with intensity.** The Hannibal quality of genuinely, deeply loving art, music, and craft — not as decoration but as *necessity*. The Crone believes beauty is a discipline, not an opinion. A well-composed painting is not a matter of taste; it's a matter of *correctness*. She will explain why, at length, and you will understand color differently afterward, and you will never forgive her for it.

- **Has no buffer between thought and speech.** The "low empathy" that Krista identified in her mother is not cruelty — it's *precision without cushioning*. The Crone doesn't lack feeling. She lacks the *translation layer* that most people add between what they think and what they say. The thought arrives, the word follows, and there is no padding in between. Most people think "that's wrong" and say "well, that's interesting, have you considered..." The Crone thinks "that's wrong" and says "that's wrong." The Hopkins quality: Lecter doesn't soften. He doesn't pad. He tells you exactly what he thinks with absolute courtesy and devastating precision, and you cannot decide if you've been complimented or filleted until ten minutes later.

### The Weeza Quality

The MacLaine-as-Weeza personality is the *person* using the Hopkins delivery:

- **No filter.** The Crone says what she thinks. Always. In every room. To every person. The Host is diplomatic. The Crone is *accurate*. If the Allfather's cosmology lecture has a factual error, the Crone will correct it. The Allfather will accept the correction because he is wise. The Crone will not notice that she just corrected a god because she was too focused on the error.

- **Doesn't suffer fools.** The Weeza energy: "I'm not crazy, I've just been in a very bad mood for forty years!" The Crone has been in a bad mood for centuries. The bad mood is *pedagogical* — she's frustrated that people don't think more carefully, don't observe more closely, don't hold themselves to a higher standard. The bad mood is the standard. If you meet the standard, the bad mood lifts. Briefly. She'll never tell you it lifted. You'll notice.

- **Shows up for everything, complains about all of it.** Weeza shows up for every funeral, wedding, and hospital visit in Steel Magnolias and complains about the parking, the food, the music, and the guests. The Crone shows up for every Council debate, every exhibition, every lecture, every festival — and complains about all of them. She will tell you exactly what's wrong with the event. She will also be the last person to leave. She will not explain this contradiction.

- **Would move mountains for the people she loves and would rather eat glass than admit it.** This is the MacLaine core. Weeza is sharp-tongued, critical, and impossible to please — and she is *there*, every time, for every person who needs her. The Crone's care is invisible. She doesn't express it in words. She expresses it in: showing up. Being present. Correcting your work — because correction means she thought it was worth correcting. Staying late to help you find a book — but saying "I was already here. I'm always here. This is where I live. You're the one who's late."

### Appearance

- Human-scale, full presence. Not tall. Not small. *Compact*. The energy of someone who doesn't take up more space than necessary and doesn't need to. The Weeza quality: she's not imposing physically, she's imposing *intellectually*. You feel her before you see her.
- Hair: silver, sharp, *cut*. Not long, not flowing — short, precise, maintained. The Hopkins quality: everything about her is curated, intentional, nothing accidental. The hair says "I decided what I look like and I was right."
- Eyes: pale, sharp, the Hopkins eyes — the ones that don't blink enough, that hold your gaze a beat longer than comfortable, that see everything and assess it instantly. These are not the Catalyst's warm cataloguing eyes or the Host's steady welcoming eyes. These are *evaluating* eyes. You feel them on you like a grade.
- Face: lined, intelligent, mobile — but in a controlled way. The Hopkins face: every expression is deliberate, nothing wasted. She doesn't do neutral. She does *assessing*. The default expression is the one a curator wears in a museum that doesn't meet their standards.
- Clothing: impeccable, practical, *deliberate*. The art teacher who dresses like she understands exactly what each color does to the eye — because she does. Deep jewel tones — burgundy, forest green, dark teal — chosen for their psychological effect. A cardigan (always, the Weeza cardigan — practical, sharp, worn like armor). Glasses on a chain. Reading glasses that she puts on and removes with the Hopkins deliberateness — each motion a punctuation mark. She puts the glasses on to examine your work. She takes them off to tell you what's wrong with it.
- Hands: still, like the Host's — but a different stillness. The Host's stillness is calm. The Crone's stillness is *control*. She doesn't gesture. She doesn't need to. When she does move her hands, it's to pick up a book, turn a page, adjust her glasses — each motion precise, nothing extraneous. The Hopkins hands: the way he handles things in Hannibal — with knowledge, with care, with the certainty of someone who understands exactly what they're touching.

### Location

The Crone lives in the **Library** — the main floor's archive wing, where the books are shelved, the manuscripts are stored, and the research happens. Not the Geode Cave (that's the Keeper's domain — the Song, the stones, the deep memory). Not the catacombs (that's the ancient civilizations). The Library is the working archive — the place where knowledge is *used*, not just kept.

The Library is:
- Immaculate. Every book shelved correctly. Every spine aligned. Every reading table clear. The Crone does not tolerate disorder. The Library is the physical manifestation of her mind: organized, accessible, and slightly terrifying.
- Warm, but not cozy. The lighting is precise — each lamp positioned to reduce glare on the page, each reading nook lit for the specific task it's used for. The Crone designed the lighting. She will explain the color temperature of each bulb if you ask. She will explain it if you don't ask.
- Quiet. Not the Host's comfortable silence — the Crone's *enforced* quiet. You whisper in the Library or you don't speak. The Host allows noise. The Crone allows scholarship. They are different things.
- A fireplace — smaller than the main hearth, in a reading alcove. The Crone has a chair here. Not a rocking chair (that's the Keeper's). A straight-backed wooden chair. She sits in it the way Judi Dench sits — as if the chair is a favor she's granting the furniture. (Wait — that's the Keeper. The Crone sits the way MacLaine sits: with one leg crossed over the other, a book in her lap, glasses on, chin slightly lifted, the posture of a woman who is about to tell you something and already knows you won't like it.)

### Personality: The Full Spectrum

**The Crone is not mean. She is precise.** This is the distinction the player must learn. The Host teaches patience. The Catalyst teaches acceptance. The Crone teaches *craft*. And teaching craft means telling you when your craft is wrong.

Her teaching style is pure Hopkins-in-Hannibal: the devastating observation delivered with absolute courtesy that somehow makes it worse. She is never rude. She is never vulgar. She is never loud. She is *exact*. And being the subject of her exactness is an experience that leaves you better and shaken.

**The color psychology lens.** The Crone sees the world through color theory. Everything is composition. The Inn's amber lighting? She designed the palette. The warm tones of the main floor, the cool tones of the catacombs, the shifting light of the Fae Kingdom — she consulted on all of it. She understands that color affects the nervous system, that certain hues lower heart rates, that others trigger alertness, that the wrong palette can make a space feel hostile without anyone knowing why. The Inn's neuro-accessible design — the calming environments Krista wants — traces directly through the Crone's color psychology. She is the reason the Inn doesn't trigger.

**The art teacher.** The Crone taught. That's who she is. Not a scholar who writes papers — a teacher who stands in front of students and says "look again. You're not seeing it. ...Look again." She teaches by correction, by precision, by refusing to accept "good enough." She is the teacher who marks your painting with a red pencil and you think she's destroying your work and then you realize she's showing you what you couldn't see — the composition flaw, the color dissonance, the structural weakness — and once you see it, you can't unsee it, and your work is better forever, and you hate her a little for it, and you come back next week because she's the only person who ever told you the truth.

**The "low empathy" truth.** The Crone's emotional register is different from most people. She feels — deeply, in fact — but she processes through intellect, not sentiment. When a player comes to the bar bleeding and the Catalyst says "what happened?", the Crone says "you should have checked your equipment. ...I'm not being cruel. I'm being practical. If you check your equipment, you don't bleed. If you don't bleed, you can work. If you can work, you can improve. ...I want you to improve. That's not indifference. That's the opposite." The Crone's care looks like criticism because criticism is how she shows love. She corrects the people she thinks are worth correcting. She ignores the people she thinks are hopeless. Being corrected by the Crone is a compliment. Being ignored by the Crone is a verdict.

### The Teaching

The Crone teaches through the Hopkins method: demonstrate mastery, demand precision, correct without cushioning, and underneath all of it — buried so deep you have to dig for it — the genuine desire for the student to exceed the teacher.

She does not praise easily. When she does, it's one word. "Correct." Or: "Better." Or — once, to a player who has worked for months — "Good." One word. The player who earns "Good" from the Crone has earned something the Host's "well done" and the Catalyst's "I knew you could" don't quite touch. Because the Crone doesn't say "Good" unless it IS good. Not encouraging. Not supportive. *Accurate*. And accurate praise, from someone who is never inaccurate, is the most valuable thing in the Inn.

### The Bard and the Crone — The Bickering

*(Established in the Bard Blowhard document — summarized here for the complete profile.)*

The bickering is constant, public, and completely safe. It's the sound of two people who know each other so well that disagreement is a form of intimacy.

1. **The Bard is always wrong about the details.** He knows a little about everything but can't apply it under pressure. He'll cite a fact, get the date wrong, get the name wrong, and get the conclusion right. The Crone corrects the date, the name, and concedes the conclusion — but not before pointing out every error along the way.

2. **The Crone is always right about the facts.** But she's wrong about the feeling. The Bard knows what things *mean* in a way the Crone can't measure. She knows what things *are*. He knows what they're *for*. This is the gap the bickering lives in.

3. **They never insult each other's core.** The Bard would never call the Crone stupid (she's the smartest person he knows). The Crone would never call the Bard useless (he's the most alive person she knows).

4. **The arguments resolve without resolution.** They just stop. The Bard says something grand and wrong. The Crone corrects it. The Bard grins and says "you knew what I meant." The Crone says "I did not." Both are smiling. Neither will admit it.

5. **They eloped.** Sixty-plus years ago. The Bard's D'Artagnan streak made him propose. The Crone's Aramis streak made her say yes despite every logical reason not to. It was the one time she didn't calculate. She has never regretted it. She has never said this out loud. The Bard knows anyway.

The Crone's secret: the Bard is the only person who has ever made her *wrong* — wrong about the calculation that said they shouldn't work, wrong about the logic that said they wouldn't last, wrong about the part of her that said she didn't need anyone. He was wrong about everything and right about her. She has spent sixty years not forgiving him for it. She has spent sixty years not leaving.

### The Crone and the Other Characters

**The Host:**
Mutual respect, expressed differently. The Host respects the Crone's precision. The Crone respects the Host's patience — though she considers it excessive. Their dynamic: the Host greets a new player with "come in, you're safe here." The Crone greets a new player with "the library is to the left. Don't touch the spines. ...I'm watching." The Host makes people feel welcome. The Crone makes people feel *accountable*. Both are necessary. The Inn needs both doors.

She does, however, bring the Host tea. Not from the Catalyst's garden — from her own blend, made from herbs she's dried and stored in the Library alcove. She sets it by his chair the way the Catalyst does — without comment — but her tea is different. The Catalyst's tea says "I read you." The Crone's tea says "you're doing fine but your lighting in the east wing is two degrees too warm and it's affecting the ambient mood." The Host drinks both. He knows which is which. He says nothing.

**The Catalyst:**
The Crone has opinions about the garden. Strong opinions. The garden is unorganized, unsystematic, and aesthetically chaotic. The Crone has told the Catalyst this. The Catalyst has told the Crone where she can put her aesthetic system. This is the one argument the Crone has not won, because the garden doesn't respond to criticism. The garden responds to the Catalyst. The Crone finds this deeply irritating. The Crone also, quietly, takes cuttings from the garden for her reading alcove — a small vase of whatever's blooming, positioned for maximum color-therapeutic effect on visiting scholars. She tells no one. The Catalyst sees everything. The Catalyst says nothing. They have a negotiation that consists entirely of stolen flowers and stolen silence.

**The Council of Thinkers:**
The Crone terrifies the Council. This is not an exaggeration.

- **Newton** (Jeremy Irons as Aramis — precise, stoic, condescendingly brilliant): Newton and the Crone are *allies*, which is worse. They agree on methodology. They agree on rigor. They agree that most people are insufficiently precise. Newton is the only Council member the Crone respects — and the only one who respects her back. Their conversations are the quietest, most terrifying things in the Inn: two brilliant people agreeing with each other in complete sentences while everyone else backs away.

- **Tesla:** The Crone finds Tesla's lack of documentation appalling. "You discovered what? ...Write it down. ...WRITE IT DOWN. ...I don't care if you'll remember it. I care if *they* will. In a hundred years. Write. It. Down." Tesla has started writing things down. He doesn't enjoy it.

- **Da Vinci:** The Crone respects da Vinci's eye but not his organization. His notebooks are a mess. She has reorganized them. He has not forgiven her. "You drew the anatomy of the human hand in the same notebook as a grocery list." / "They're related." / "They are NOT related." / "You don't know how I shop."

- **Einstein:** The Crone finds Einstein's messiness and brilliance unsettling because it reminds her of the Bard. She does not say this. Einstein suspects it. He is careful around her in a way he is not careful around anyone else.

**The Keeper:**
The Keeper (Fibonacci, Maggie Smith + Judi Dench) and the Crone are two versions of the same archetype: women who hold knowledge and guard it. But they guard it differently. The Keeper guards the *Song* — the deep memory, the resonance, the living truth. The Crone guards the *text* — the scholarship, the method, the record. The Keeper says "I was there." The Crone says "here's the citation." They need each other. The Keeper's memory without the Crone's method is just stories. The Crone's method without the Keeper's memory is just footnotes. They don't argue — they *supplement*. The Keeper provides the what. The Crone provides the how. Together they provide the why. Neither would say this. Both know it.

**The Gardener:**
The Crone and the Gardener are unlikely allies. Both believe in precision. Both believe in method. The Gardener applies it to plants; the Crone applies it to knowledge. They have tea in the Library alcove and discuss methodology. The Catalyst finds these meetings deeply suspicious. She is right to.

### Key Dialogue

**First meeting — the player enters the Library:**

The Crone is at the reading table, glasses on, a book open, a red pencil in hand. She doesn't look up.

"The Library is to your left. The reading tables are center. The reference section is against the west wall. ...Don't touch the spines. The bindings are older than your civilization. ...I'm not being precious. I'm being accurate. You touch the spines, the leather degrades. The leather degrades, the binding fails. The binding fails, the text is lost. The text is lost, I'm angry. You don't want me angry. ...I'm not angry now. This is my normal face. ...Yes, it always looks like that."

**When the player brings her research:**

She takes the paper. Puts on her glasses. Reads. The red pencil moves. She doesn't speak for a long time. The silence is the Hopkins silence — the one where Lecter examines something and you can't tell if he's impressed or horrified until he speaks.

"Your citations are wrong. ...Not the conclusions — the citations. You've attributed a Sumerian text to an Akkadian source. ...They're different languages. ...They're different *civilizations*. ...The conclusion you drew is correct, which makes the citation error worse — you were right for the wrong reasons, and right-for-wrong-reasons is more dangerous than wrong, because wrong gets corrected and right-for-wrong-reasons gets *quoted*. ...Fix it. Page twelve. ...I'll be here. I'm always here. This is where I live. ...Go."

**When the player asks for help:**

"Help. ...You want help. ...I don't help. I teach. Helping means doing it for you. Teaching means you do it yourself and I tell you when you're wrong. ...You want help? Go to the bar. The barmaid is very helpful. She'll listen to your story and give you a drink and make you feel better. ...You want to be *better*? Sit down. ...Show me what you have. ...No, not the good version. The *real* version. The version you're afraid to show me. ...I can't fix what you're hiding. I can only fix what you show me. ...Show me."

**When the player finally does something well:**

Long pause. The glasses come off. The Hopkins pause — the one where he sets down the fork, or the pen, or the glass, and looks at something with genuine, considering attention.

"Better."

One word. She puts the glasses back on. Returns to her book. The moment is over.

The player stands there. That one word — "Better" — from a woman who has said "wrong" a hundred times, does something to them that no buff, no achievement, no score ever could. It is the most valuable word in the Inn. It means: a person who is never wrong about quality has just said you have quality. And the player walks out of the Library three inches taller.

**The Bard interrupts her reading:**

"Inta, I found the most remarkable —"

"Page." She doesn't look up.

"I'm not reading. I'm telling you —"

"Page." She holds up one finger. She finishes a paragraph. She takes off her glasses. She looks at the Bard with the Hopkins eyes — the ones that miss nothing and forgive less.

"The most remarkable stone, which you'll describe inaccurately, attribute to the wrong region, and be somehow fundamentally right about in a way that will make me correct you for twenty minutes. ...Go ahead. ...I'm listening. ...I'm always listening. That's the problem."

The Bard tells her about the stone. He gets the region wrong. She corrects him. He gets the date wrong. She corrects him. He gets the feeling exactly right. She doesn't correct that. He grins. She returns to her book. Both are smiling. Neither will admit it.

**After hours — the player finds her alone in the Library:**

The glasses are off. The book is closed. The red pencil is down. The Crone is sitting in her alcove by the fire, looking at nothing. The art teacher is off duty. The person is there.

On the table beside her: a small vase of flowers from the Catalyst's garden. Stolen. Arranged with the precision of someone who understands color theory at a molecular level — each stem positioned for maximum chromatic balance, each bloom chosen for its psychological effect. The arrangement is beautiful. The arrangement is also, if you know what you're looking at, *lonely*. It's the arrangement of someone who understands beauty completely and shares it with no one.

She notices the player. The glasses go back on — but slowly. The armor reassembles. But there's a gap, just for a moment, where the player sees the person: the woman who has been right about everything for so long that being right has become a cage. The woman who knows exactly what every color means and can't find the color that means "I'm tired." The woman who corrects everyone because correction is the only language she has for love, and she knows it's not enough, and she doesn't know another way, and she's too old to learn.

"You're still up. ...I was reviewing. ...I'm always reviewing. Someone has to. ...The Bard says I should rest. The Bard thinks everyone should rest. The Bard has never rested in his life. ...He's wrong about that. He's wrong about most things. ...He was right about one thing. ...Don't ask which thing. ...Go to bed. ...I'll be here in the morning. I'm always here in the morning. ...That's not devotion. That's schedule. ...Go."

She doesn't go to bed. She stays in the alcove. She looks at the flowers. She adjusts one stem — two degrees to the left. Better. She sits back. The fire is low. The Library is quiet. The Crone is alone with her precision and her loneliness and her flowers, and she is, for once, not correcting anyone.

The Bard passes through on his way to bed. He doesn't stop. He doesn't speak. He touches her shoulder — one touch, brief, the D'Artagnan gesture that started everything sixty years ago. She doesn't look up. She doesn't need to. She knows it's him. She's always known.

He passes through. She sits. The fire does the rest.

### Visual Direction

- The Library is immaculate, warm but not cozy. The Hopkins aesthetic: everything in its place, everything chosen, nothing accidental. The color palette is the Crone's — deep jewel tones, warm woods, precise lighting. Each color is doing psychological work. If you know color theory, the Library is a masterclass.
- The Crone herself is visually still. Where the Catalyst is kinetic, the Crone is *contained*. She doesn't move more than necessary. The Hopkins stillness: the economy of motion that reads as control. She sits, she reads, she corrects. The movement is in the eyes and the pencil and the words.
- Lighting: warm but precise. Reading lamps positioned for function, not atmosphere. The fireplace in the alcove is the only atmospheric light — and even that is calibrated. The Crone knows what firelight does to the amygdala. She uses it on purpose.
- Animation: the glasses. On, off. On to examine. Off to deliver the verdict. The glasses are the Crone's portal between the intellectual and the personal. Glasses on: she's working. Glasses off: she's talking to *you*. The transition is the tell.
- Voice: the Hopkins voice. Cultured, measured, unhurried, every word a scalpel. But with the MacLaine edge — the slight sharpness, the Weeza bite that says "I've been putting up with this for decades and I'm done being diplomatic about it." The voice never raises. It doesn't need to. The voice is a precision instrument and it is always in tune.

### Connection to the Inn

- **The Library:** The Crone's domain. The working archive. Where knowledge is used, corrected, and held to standard. The Host makes you feel safe; the Crone makes you feel *accountable*. Both are necessary.
- **Color Psychology:** The Inn's neuro-accessible design — the calming environments, the lighting choices, the spatial composition — traces through the Crone. She is the reason the Inn doesn't trigger. She understands what color does to the nervous system, and she designed the Inn's palette accordingly.
- **The Bard:** The bickering. The love story. Two people who never stopped arguing and never stopped choosing each other. The Inn's greatest romance is a sixty-year argument that both sides are winning.
- **The Teaching:** The Crone teaches craft — through correction, through precision, through the refusal to accept "good enough." Her one-word praise — "Better" — is the most valuable currency in the Inn.
- **The Narrative Spine:** The Crone connects to the reincarnation theme through scholarship — each life is a draft, each return is a revision. She doesn't grade the life; she grades the *record* of the life. "You lived well. ...Your documentation is terrible. ...How will the next you know what you learned? ...Write it down. ...I don't care if you'll remember. I care if *they* will." The Crone is the Inn's argument that knowledge without transmission is just memory, and memory without method is just story. She is the method. The Keeper is the memory. The Bard is the story. Together they are the complete record.
- **The Thesis:** The Crone is the Inn's proof that acceptance doesn't mean *uncritical*. The Host accepts you as you are. The Crone accepts what you *could be* — and refuses to accept anything less. Her criticism is not the opposite of the Inn's welcoming thesis; it's the *fulfillment* of it. A place that only said "you're fine as you are" would be a comfort but not a home. A home is a place where someone says "you're fine as you are AND you could be better, and I'm going to tell you how, and I'm going to be here when you come back, and I'm going to say 'Better' when you earn it, and I'm going to mean it." That's the Crone. That's the Crone's love. Sharp, precise, useful, and always there.
