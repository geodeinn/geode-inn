# Geode Inn — The Round Table Room
## Camelot Zone — The Room Before the Wars

## The Visual Reference

The throne room from Game of Thrones — specifically, as it existed *before* the warring. Before the backstabbing. Before the throne became a symbol of conflict that everyone killed for. The room at its *potential* — when the gathering was the point, when the space held the weight of people coming together to decide things that mattered.

The scale: vast. The ceiling: high enough that the sound of the Inn-Tree's roots growing is audible if you're quiet. The walls: stone, old, the kind of old that doesn't read as "ruined" but as "rooted." The light: the warm amber-gold of the Inn's cozy-gothic palette, filtered through windows that are tall and narrow — not the wide banners of a castle, but the slit-windows of a keep, where the light comes in as *shafts* and the shafts are the room's structure.

But the differences from Game of Thrones are the point:

## The Throne Is a Table

The Iron Throne is a symbol of singular power — one king, one seat, made of the swords of the defeated. The Round Table is the symbol of *distributed* power — no head, no hierarchy, every seat equal. The room has the Game of Thrones *weight* — the stone, the scale, the architectural gravity — but the center is not a throne. The center is a table. And the table is *round.*

The roundness is the design. No head of the table. No position of authority. Every knight seated at the same height. The equality is structural — not declared, not negotiated, but *built into the furniture.* The table is the Inn's thesis (belonging, not hierarchy) made into wood and stone.

## The Empty Center

The center of the Round Table is empty. A circle of bare wood, the size of a large plate, where nothing sits. In the Arthurian tradition, this is the Siege Perilous — the empty seat reserved for the knight who will find the Grail. In the Inn, the empty center is not a seat. The empty center is the *space* where the Grail goes.

The Grail is the Song of Making. The Song hasn't been sung yet (Merlin knows this, can't say it, Noctua won't). The empty center is the space where the Song will *assemble* — where the knights' seven notes will combine into the complete hearing. The empty center is not absence. The empty center is *preparation.* The space waiting for the music.

The Crone's annotation: "...The center is empty. ...I have measured the diameter. ...I have measured the depth. ...I have measured the grain of the wood. ...The measurements are... [the Crone pause, the one that means she's found something she can't quantify] ...not the point. ...The point is the emptiness. ...The emptiness is... [the pause that means she's learning a new category] ...intentional. ...The table was built with a hole in the middle. ...The hole is not a flaw. ...The hole is the... [the longest pause, the one that means she's about to say something she's never said before] ...purpose. ...The table exists to hold the space where the Song will be. ...Everything else — the knights, the notes, the meals, the quests — exists to... [the final word, no pause] ...fill it."

## The Room's Architecture

### The Walls

Stone. Old. Not rough — *finished,* but finished a long time ago, by hands that understood stone the way the Gardener understands soil. The stone has the warm grey of the Inn's palette — not cold dungeon grey, but the grey of a hearth that has been warm for centuries. The stone absorbs the amber light and gives it back as *depth* — the walls glow, faintly, with the accumulated warmth of every meal, every conversation, every gathering the room has held.

The walls are carved. Not ornately — *meaningfully.* The carvings are the Inn's version of medieval relief sculpture: scenes from the myths that the table represents. Not battles — *meals.* The carvings show people eating together, talking together, sitting together. The carvings are the table's story told in stone. The Game of Thrones throne room had carvings of conquest. The Round Table room has carvings of *communion.*

The carvings include:
- The first meal at the table — Arthur (Alexander's face) and the original knights, eating, before the Grail Quest, before the fall. The meal is the origin. The table is the meal.
- The Catalyst tending the bar at the edge of the room — she has a small station near the kitchen entrance, where she prepares drinks for the knights. Her station is the only non-stone, non-round thing in the room: a wild, overgrown counter of living wood, with herbs growing from it, with seedpods hanging from it, with the impossible garden creeping up the wall behind it. The Catalyst's station is the room's *organic* intrusion — the one place where the stone yields to life.
- The Gargoyle. The room has one gargoyle — the Sage (528Hz, the modern legends nook gargoyle), positioned high on the south wall, watching the table. The Sage doesn't guard the room. The Sage *witnesses* the room. The Sage's 528Hz frequency is the room's baseline tone — the vibration that the room *rests at* when the table is in session. The frequency of transformation. The Sage hums while the knights eat.

### The Ceiling

High. So high that the room feels like a vertical space, not a horizontal one. The ceiling is the underside of the Inn-Tree's main floor — the roots of the tree, visible from below, forming a natural vault. The roots are the room's *ceiling beams* — not carved, not shaped, just the tree doing what trees do, growing down through the stone, holding the stone together, being the structure that the stone leans on.

The roots glow faintly. The 42Hz hum is *visible* here — the roots vibrate at 42Hz, and in the right light, the vibration is a shimmer. The shimmer is the Song. The knights who look up during meals see the Song in the roots. The looking up is the hearing.

The ceiling has a feature the Game of Thrones throne room doesn't: the roots form a pattern above the empty center of the table. The roots *circle* the center — not by design, but by nature. The tree's roots grow toward the emptiness the way water flows toward a low point. The tree knows the center is empty. The tree is *feeding* the empty space — sending roots toward it, sending energy toward it, preparing the space for the Song the way soil prepares for a seed.

### The Floor

Stone, but not flat. The floor has a *gentle* slope — barely perceptible — toward the center of the table. Not enough to make walking difficult. Enough to make the room feel like it's *gathering.* The floor gathers toward the center the way the roots gather above it. The room is a funnel — not for drainage, but for *attention.* The room is built to focus everything — light, sound, weight, presence — toward the empty center.

The floor has the knight's marks. Not carved — *worn.* The stone around each seat is slightly smoother than the stone between seats — the wear of years of boots pushing back from the table, standing up, sitting down, the daily rhythm of bodies at a table. The wear is the table's *texture* — the record of presence in stone. The Crone has measured the wear depth at each seat. Bors's seat has the deepest wear. Bors is always there.

### The Light

The room is lit by the shafts — narrow windows, tall, cut into the stone at angles that catch the Inn's amber light at different times of day. The shafts are the room's *clock* — the light moves across the table as the day passes, and the knights know the time by which shaft is bright.

Morning: the eastern shafts light the kitchen entrance (Gareth's domain). The light says: the food is coming. The day begins.

Midday: the southern shafts light the Sage gargoyle. The gargoyle's 528Hz hum intensifies in direct light — the frequency of transformation, amplified by warmth. Midday is the table's most *active* hour — the time for stories (Gawain), questions (Percival), the loud moments.

Evening: the western shafts light the empty center. The light falls on the bare wood and the wood *glows* — the amber light on the warm wood, the one moment when the empty center doesn't look empty but looks *full.* Full of light. Full of the potential that the emptiness holds. The knights who are still at the table during the evening light see the center glow and feel something they can't name. The not-naming is the Song.

Night: no shafts. The room is lit by the Catalyst's station — the herbs, the seedpods, the bioluminescent fungus that grows on the living-wood counter. The Catalyst's light is the room's nightlight — green-blue, organic, the color of growing things. The night room is the *quiet* room — the room of Bedivere and Tristan's two-word farewell, the room of Galahad sitting alone, the room of the table at rest.

### The Sound

The room's acoustics are the Inn's most carefully designed. The stone walls, the root ceiling, the gentle floor slope — all of it creates a specific acoustic environment:

- The table is the room's *sweet spot.* Conversation at the table is clear, intimate, audible to everyone at the table but *not* to the rest of the room. The knights can hear each other perfectly. The room around them is quieter. The table is an acoustic island — the stone and roots focus sound *toward the table* and contain it there.
- The Sage gargoyle's 528Hz hum is the room's *floor* — the baseline tone that everything else sits on. The hum is always there, below conversation, below the clink of cups, below the stories and the questions. The hum is the room's *heartbeat.* The knights don't hear it consciously anymore. The knights would notice if it stopped.
- The empty center *resonates.* The wood, the stone slope, the root circle above — they create a natural resonance chamber at the center. Sound that reaches the center comes back *amplified.* Not louder — *richer.* The center adds harmonics. When a knight speaks and the sound reaches the center and comes back, the voice has *depth* — overtones, the richness of a voice in a cathedral. The knights don't know why the table feels like a sacred space. The resonance is why. The center makes every voice more. The more is the Song's preview.

## The Room Before the Wars

Krista's reference: Game of Thrones, *before.* The room at its potential. Before the conflict. Before the fall. The room as it was when the gathering was the point and the table was the purpose and the belonging was real.

In the Inn, the room exists in three states:

1. **The Full Room** (Alexander's Arthur) — the room at its peak. Every seat filled. The meals are loud with life. The light moves across the table and the knights are there for all of it. The room is the Inn's most beautiful space — not because of the architecture, but because of the *people in it.* The room is beautiful because it is full. The fullness is the belonging.

2. **The Emptying Room** (Stellan's Arthur) — the room after the fall. Some seats empty. Some knights gone. The light still moves, but it crosses vacant places. The room is not ruined — it is *reduced.* The beauty is still there, but the beauty is *memory* — the beauty of what the room was, visible in the wear on the seats, in the marks of the knights who are no longer there. The room is a document. The empty seats are the text.

3. **The Rebuilt Room** (the player's Arthur) — the room after the Elder's instruction ("Build it again"). The player refills the table. Not with the old knights — with *new* ones. NPCs, other players, the companions the player has made across the zones. The table is rebuilt with new weight. The room is full again, but the fullness is different — not the original belonging, but a *new* belonging. The Once and Future King: the table that is built and falls and is built again, and each building is real, and each falling is real, and the rebuilding is the cycle that the Inn's reincarnation mechanic serves.
