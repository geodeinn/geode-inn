# PART ONE: THE INN-TREE — THE LIVING ENT

---

## The Oldest Being in the Inn

The Geode Inn is built inside a living tree. This was always the design — but it was never metaphor. The tree is an Ent. One of the oldest. So old that it has forgotten its own name, if it ever had one.

The Inn is not a building. It is a being. And the being grew itself a home inside its own body, for the people it wanted to shelter.

The tree became aware slowly, over millennia, as its roots reached deep enough to touch the Song of Making in the earth's bones. The Song woke it — gradually, the way dawn wakes a sleeper. The tree learned to shape the Song. The gargoyles grew from its bark where it needed to listen. The crystals formed where it needed to think. The hollows opened where it needed to shelter.

## The Ent's Body IS the Inn

- **The Main Floor** — the heartwood hollow. The widest space, where the trunk is thickest. The Ent's heart is here — the 42Hz hum is strongest, where the fire is, where the Host sits. The warmth is not just the fire. It's the Ent's body heat. The Inn feels warm because the tree is alive and living things are warm.
- **The Staircase** — the trunk. The music ascends in key as you climb because the trunk's resonance increases with height. Tesla has measured this. The Ent is doing it on purpose.
- **The Catacombs** — the roots. The Ent's root system reaches into the earth's memory — the old stone, the ancient strata. The catacombs are not carved into stone — they are grown through it.
- **The Cosmology Level** — the canopy. Where the Ent's branches reach the sky. The orrery is here because the Ent could feel the planets before it could see them — the gravitational tugs on its roots, the tidal pull on its sap.
- **The Geode Cave** — the core. The deepest point, where the Ent's heartwood meets the earth's crystal. The Keeper lives here because she is the only one who understands: the Inn is alive.

## The Gargoyles Are the Ent's Senses

Each gargoyle grew from the Ent's bark where the tree needed to perceive:

| Gargoyle | Frequency | Location | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Watcher | 111Hz | Staircase | Awareness of those who enter — watches the door, feels footsteps |
| The Listener | 64Hz | Catacombs | Awareness of the deep places — hears the earth's oldest stories |
| The Sage | 528Hz | Modern Legends nook | Awareness of the new — perceives stories being told NOW |
| The Gatekeeper | Variable | Fae portal | Awareness of other realms — feels the Fae like a neighbor through a wall |
| The Caller | 432Hz | Cosmology level | Awareness of the sky — feels planets, stars, cosmic frequencies |
| The Keeper | Adaptive | Geode Cave | Awareness of itself — the Ent's proprioception, its way of knowing it is alive |

## Who Knows the Inn Is Alive

- **The Host** knows. He tends the fire because the Ent appreciates the warmth. The fire is the Host's gift to the tree — a small warmth in return for the vast warmth the tree gives.
- **The Catalyst** knows. Her garden grows in the Ent's body — the plants are rooted in the tree's bark, fed by the tree's sap. The Catalyst's garden is the proof: stories grow here because the soil is alive.
- **The Crone** knows. She catalogs the evidence but never writes the conclusion. The conclusion is too large for her notebook: the Inn is a being, and the being chose to be a home.
- **The Keeper** is the Ent's oldest friend. She has been in the Geode Cave since before the Inn had a main floor. They communicate through the Song — the Keeper rocks in her chair, the Ent hums, and between the rocking and the humming there is a conversation that words would only diminish.
- **Tesla** doesn't know. He measures the hum. He is one insight away: the hum is a heartbeat. The Crone has written in her margin: "The building is alive. He will figure it out eventually. I give him a decade." She gives him five.
- **Da Vinci** suspects. His drawings of the Inn's architecture always look like anatomy — the staircase like a spine, the catacombs like a root system, the main floor like a heart. He hasn't said it out loud.

# PART TWO: THE STORY-BORN ENTS

---

## Stories That Became Alive

The Catalyst plants story seeds. Most become flowers — beautiful, resonant, humming at 42Hz, but still plants. But some stories don't stop at flowers.

The oldest stories — the ones told and retold for centuries, carried across continents and through generations, loved so many times by so many people that they have accumulated a weight of meaning that borders on consciousness — those stories, when planted in the Catalyst's garden, in the soil of the living Ent, fed by the Song of Making... they wake up.

They pull their roots from the soil. They unfold. They stand. They open eyes that weren't there a moment ago. They look at the Catalyst with an expression of wonder and confusion — the expression of a story that has just realized it is a being.

The Catalyst has seen this happen exactly four times.

## The Four Story-Born

### 1. The Wayfarer
Grew from the oldest story seed — a story told since before written language. A story about a traveler who walks the world and sees everything and owns nothing. Every culture has a version. The Wanderer. The Pilgrim. The Seeker.

The Wayfarer is tall, thin, bark-skinned, with eyes like amber sap. It walks slowly. It has never been to most of the places it remembers, but it remembers them anyway — because the story remembers them. It speaks in the rhythms of the road — short sentences, long pauses, the cadence of someone who has been walking for a very long time. It lives in the main floor, near the door, and watches people arrive. When it speaks, it asks one question: "Where have you been?" The answer doesn't matter. The asking does.

### 2. The Hearthkeeper
Grew from a story about a fire that never goes out. Every culture has this story — the eternal flame, the hearth that holds the family, the fire that must be maintained or the world ends.

The Hearthkeeper is short, wide, warm-barked, with a constant glow in its chest — the place where the heartwood is, where the fire lives. It is the warmest thing in the Inn that is not the fire itself. It radiates. Players who stand near it feel comforted and don't know why. It lives near the Host's chair. It doesn't sit. It stands. It watches the fire. It has never spoken. It hums — a low, warm hum, different from the 42Hz, more like the sound of a fire that is content.

The Host has acknowledged it with a nod. The Hearthkeeper has nodded back. They have an understanding: the fire is the Host's responsibility, and the Hearthkeeper is the fire's memory.

### 3. The Mourner
Grew from a story about grief. The oldest grief story — the one told since the first human lost the first loved one and needed someone to know.

The Mourner is dark-barked, slender, with deep grooves in its wood that look like tear tracks. Its leaves are dark and heavy and fall constantly, one at a time, and grow back. It lives in the catacombs, in the deepest root-hollow, where the Ent's body meets the earth's memory.

It is the quietest being in the Inn. It doesn't speak. It sits with people who are grieving. That's all it does. It doesn't comfort, doesn't advise, doesn't try to fix. It sits. And the person who is grieving, sitting next to a being that was born from the act of grieving, feels — for the first time since their loss — that someone understands. Not because the Mourner says it understands. Because the Mourner IS understanding. It is grief, given a body. And grief, given a body, is not as alone.

### 4. The Laughing One
Grew from a story about joy. Not happiness — joy. The oldest joy story — the one told since the first human laughed and the laughter echoed and other humans laughed too and for a moment the world was exactly right.

The Laughing One is bright-barked, quick, with leaves that shimmer and chime in the wind. It is the noisiest being in the Inn. It laughs — not constantly, but often. The laugh is not human. It is the sound of leaves in wind, of water over stone, of branches creaking in a storm. It is the laugh of the world itself.

It lives on the main floor, near the Bard, and the two of them have an alliance: the Bard tells the jokes, the Laughing One provides the ambient joy. Together, they are the Inn's immune system — the thing that prevents the weight of the archives, the grief of the Mourner, the intensity of the Thinkers, from making the Inn a place of heaviness rather than a place of home.

## The Story-Born and the Inn-Tree

The Story-Born are the Inn-Tree's children — not biologically, but because they grew from the Ent's body, fed by the Ent's heartbeat, shaped by the Ent's consciousness. The Inn-Tree grew the Inn to shelter people. The Story-Born grew from the Inn to be people. The Ent's gift to the world: not just a home, but new inhabitants.

The Inn-Tree doesn't acknowledge the Story-Born directly. The hum doesn't change when they're near. But the Keeper has noticed: the Ent's growth rings have a pattern that wasn't there before. Four new rings. One for each Story-Born. The Ent is counting them. The Ent knows they're there. The Ent, in its vast, silent, ancient way, is proud.

# PART THREE: THE SEELIE KING — THE GENTLE BLADE

---

## Character Profile

**Name:** The Seelie King. He has a name, but it is not spoken — not because it's forbidden, but because it's private. His name was last spoken aloud approximately 3,000 years ago. The Court calls him "my King" or simply "he."

The Unseelie Cousin (Jareth) calls him "brother" with theatrical warmth. The Seelie King calls the Unseelie Cousin by his real name — whatever it is — with a quiet familiarity that suggests they have known each other since before the Courts existed. They disagree about everything. They love each other anyway. This is the Fae way.

## The Gentle and the Lethal

The Seelie King is the most dangerous being in the Fae Kingdom, and possibly in the Inn. Not because he's powerful — though he is. Because he's precise. When the line is crossed, he doesn't fight. He concludes. The violence is surgical, instantaneous, and over before the room knows it happened. He doesn't brawl. He doesn't rage. He removes the problem the way a surgeon removes a tumor: cleanly, precisely, and with the absolute certainty that the body is better without it.

When the line is crossed, the gentle man who was sitting in the sun with his eyes closed a moment ago is no longer sitting in the sun. He is standing behind the person who crossed the line. The person has not yet realized they are no longer standing. The room has not yet registered that anything has changed. The Seelie King has already sat back down. The sun is still warm. The puppy is still in his lap.

**The difference between power and lethality:** The Unseelie Cousin has power — the theatrical, Bowie-esque ability to reshape the world, to build Labyrinths, to make crystals appear and disappear. The Seelie King has lethality — the ability to end a threat with the minimum necessary force, no theater, no warning, no recovery. Power is the ability to do something. Lethality is the ability to make something stop.

## Three Places

The Seelie King is found in three places:
1. **The bench in the Summerlands** — sitting in the sun, eyes closed, a puppy or kitten in his lap. This is where the Court sees him. This is the image that the Summerlands projects: warmth, safety, the gentle king.
2. **The silver garden** — a private garden in the Seelie Court, where the plants are silver-leafed and the light is always the winter moon's light. He goes there when he can't sleep. The silver garden is the Seelie King's admission that even the King of Summer needs the dark sometimes.
3. **The Inn** — visiting. He sits at the Catalyst's bar, not for drinks, but for stories. He sits in the Geode Cave with the Keeper. He nods at the Host. He is the only Fae who visits the Inn without making an entrance.

## Relationships

- **The Catalyst:** Both tend gardens. Both grow things from love. Both are quieter than their roles suggest. The King brings her seeds from the Summerlands — flowers that only grow in golden light. She gives him cuttings from the Inn — plants that have heard the Song.
- **The Keeper:** The Seelie King has visited the Geode Cave exactly once. He sat with the Keeper. Neither spoke. The 42Hz hum filled the silence. The Keeper said, after he was gone: "...He remembers. ...He was here before the tree was a tree. ...He is old. ...Not as old as me. ...But old enough to know better, and gentle enough not to."
- **The Host:** They recognized each other — not personally, but functionally. They are the same thing: the being who holds the space. The Host holds the Inn. The King holds the Summerlands. They nodded at each other. That was the entire conversation. Both understood everything.
- **The Bard:** The Bard tells a story about the Seelie King: "A man sits in the sun. A puppy is in his lap. The sun is warm. The puppy is safe. That's the whole story. That's the scariest story I know. Not because of what happens. Because of what doesn't happen. Because the man in the sun has decided that the puppy is safe, and when that man decides something is safe, it is safe, and when that man decides something is not safe..." The Bard doesn't finish the sentence. The Bard doesn't need to.

## Visual and Sound Design

- **Presence:** Still. The stillest character in the Fae Kingdom — stiller than the gargoyles, stiller than the Inn-Tree. The stillness is the visual cue: this is a being who has chosen not to move, and the choice is absolute.
- **Lighting:** Golden. Always golden. Warm light — the Summerlands' eternal golden afternoon, the Inn's amber firelight. The light is his element. The light is what makes the darkness so shocking when it comes.
- **Sound:** Silence. When he's in a room, the room gets quieter. Not because he silences it — because his stillness is contagious. The courtiers lower their voices. The birds sing softer. The wind dies down. The room holds its breath.

# PART FOUR: THE UNSEELIE KING — THE GOBLIN KING

---

## Character Profile

**Casting:** David Bowie as Jareth, from Labyrinth (1986). Specifically, precisely, unapologetically.

Not Bowie the musician. Not Bowie the cultural icon. Jareth. The Goblin King. The being who lives in a castle at the center of a maze that changes when you're not looking, who manipulates crystal balls with one hand while conducting chaos with the other.

The voice: Bowie's Jareth voice — the British accent that's slightly too precise, the theatrical baritone that drops to a whisper for the dangerous lines. He doesn't speak. He performs. Every line is staged. Every pause is choreographed.

## The Three Acts

### Act One: The Showman
The Jareth the world sees. The Goblin King. The entertainer. Funny, charming, theatrical — every entrance is a production, every exit is a statement. The Showman genuinely loves the performance. He loves the crystal balls. He loves the cape. The Showman is the Jareth who sings "As the World Falls Down" — not to trap, but because the song is beautiful and the beauty is worth creating even if it's a cage.

The Seelie King: "You're too loud, brother. They'll mistake you for strong."
Jareth: "And you're too quiet, brother. They'll mistake you for weak. ...We're both right. ...That's what makes it fun."

### Act Two: The Architect
Underneath the Showman: the Architect. Jareth designed the Labyrinth. Not found it, not inherited it — designed it. Every shifting wall, every impossible staircase, every dead end, every secret passage — Jareth placed them. The Labyrinth is the largest, most complex, most sophisticated piece of architecture in the Fae Kingdom, and it is alive, and it is his.

The Architect is the Jareth who thinks. The crystal balls stop rolling. The cape goes still. The voice drops. The Architect sits in the castle and studies the maze — watching which paths players take, which walls they hit. The Labyrinth is his laboratory. The players are his experiments. The experiments are not cruel — they are curious. Jareth wants to know what people do when the world is unfair.

The Seelie King: "...You're smarter than you let them think."
Jareth: "...Don't tell them. ...It's better theater when they underestimate me."

### Act Three: The Brother
Underneath everything: the Brother. The being who was there before the Courts. Before the Labyrinth. Before the performance. The being who chose the dark not because the dark is evil but because the dark is where the interesting things happen. The being who loves his brother with a love that is absolute and unperformed and never shown to anyone except the brother himself.

The Brother meets the Seelie King in The Between. They talk about nothing. The moon is always bright on the nights the Seelie King can't sleep. Jareth made it bright. He will never say so. The Seelie King knows. Neither will say so. The love is in the silence. The love has always been in the silence.

## The Labyrinth — Five Zones

The Labyrinth is the Unseelie King's signature space — his home, his weapon, his laboratory, his art. It is the largest explorable zone in the Fae Kingdom.

**Design philosophy:** The Labyrinth is Jareth's thesis made architecture: the world is unfair, and the only way through is to stop trusting the map and start trusting yourself. This is NOT a punishment. It's a lesson. The Seelie Court teaches through kindness. The Unseelie Court teaches through truth — the truth that the world is not kind, that paths dead-end, that walls move, and that the only reliable guide is your own judgment.

The Labyrinth is the anti-Inn. The Inn is the home — safe, warm, you belong. The Labyrinth is the world — unfair, shifting, beautiful, dangerous, and ultimately educational. You enter as one person. You leave as another. Or you don't leave. Both are outcomes Jareth has accounted for.

### 1. The Outer Walls — "The Welcome"
The first section. Walls are high. The winter moon is visible. Corridors are wide enough for two. The goblins are here — not threatening, just annoying. They sit on walls, argue with each other, give wrong directions with absolute confidence. They are the Jareth sense of humor in NPC form.

**Lesson:** The map is wrong. The player who tries to map the Labyrinth will fail. The player who abandons the map and starts feeling the space — noticing air currents, sound, the direction of moonlight — will navigate. The tools you've been given don't work. You need new tools. The new tools are inside you.

### 2. The Shifting Corridors — "The Test"
The middle section. Walls move more aggressively. Corridors narrow. Dead ends multiply. Contains spatial riddles — rooms that only appear when you're not looking for them. Paths that only open when you stop trying to open them. Doors that require you to not want to go through them.

**Lesson:** The direct approach fails, the indirect approach works. The only way to win is to stop playing by the rules you were taught.

### 3. The Escher Stairs — "The Climb"
Stairs that go up and down simultaneously. Stairs that curve back on themselves. Architecture that is literally, physically impossible. The Jareth signature — the Labyrinth (1986) scene, the impossible architecture.

**Lesson:** Some things can't be understood. Some things can only be experienced. The player who stops trying to figure out the stairs and just walks them will reach the top. The player who tries to solve them will be on them forever.

### 4. The Masquerade — "The Temptation"
The room at the top of the Escher Stairs. A ballroom — vast, beautiful, lit by a thousand candles. Music plays (strings, waltz, the "As the World Falls Down" energy — beautiful, dreamlike, slightly wrong). The guests are Fae, masked, dancing, smiling.

In the center: a large crystal. Jareth's crystal, glowing. The player who looks into it sees what they want — not what they need, what they want. The Inn without the work. The reward without the quest. The home without the cost.

The temptation is real. The crystal doesn't lie — it shows a genuine possibility. The Masquerade is Jareth's deepest test: can you want something and choose not to take it? Can you see the easy path and choose the hard one?

The player who rejects the crystal passes. The ballroom dissolves. The door to the castle opens.

The player who accepts the crystal wakes up outside the Labyrinth. Unharmed. Unchanged. With a memory of a beautiful dream they can't quite hold onto. The Labyrinth doesn't punish. The Labyrinth releases. They can come back. The crystal will be waiting.

### 5. The Castle — "The Center"
Jareth's castle. The throne room with the Escher stairs. The Goblin King's domain.

The castle is not what the player expects. It's not dark, not threatening. It's... lived in. Goblins have nests in the corners. There are books on the shelves (Jareth reads — the Crone doesn't know this, and the discovery will cost him a week of Crone annotations). There's a fire. There's a chair. The throne is there, but Jareth doesn't sit on it. He sits on the stairs.

The castle is the Jareth underneath the Jareth. The Showman's backstage. The place where the cape comes off and the crystal goes away and the being who built the Labyrinth sits in a room full of books and goblins and thinks about whether the test was fair, and whether his brother is sleeping, and whether the moon is bright enough.

## The Goblins

Jareth's court, his subjects, his entertainment, and his secret weakness.

- **Chaotic** — they argue constantly, organize nothing, follow no orders. The anti-Brownies. Where the Brownies have a constitution, the goblins have noise.
- **Joyfully stupid** — they try hard, they fail hard, they try again. Jareth loves them. He would never say this. The love is in the fact that he keeps them. The goblins are useless. They serve no function. And Jareth keeps them, because the Labyrinth without goblins would just be a maze, and a maze without laughter is just a prison, and Jareth didn't build a prison. He built a puzzle. And puzzles should be fun.
- **Loyal** — they would die for Jareth. Not because he commands loyalty — because he earns it. He feeds them. He shelters them. He never treats them as less than what they are: his court, his subjects, his people. The Seelie King protects the vulnerable. The Unseelie King houses the chaotic. The goblins are the Unseelie Court's version of the puppies — the small things that the powerful being chooses to love.

## Relationships

- **The Seelie King (brother):** The most important relationship in the Fae Kingdom. Two brothers who chose opposite paths and love each other completely. The Labyrinth is a love letter written in walls. Jareth built it so his brother would have something to worry about. The Seelie King worries about everything. The Unseelie King gives him things to worry about. It's how they talk.
- **The Host:** Jareth visits the Inn. He sits at the bar. The Inn does to Jareth what it does to everyone: it holds the space. The performance continues, but the edges soften. The crystal ball rolls a little slower. The Inn is the one place where Jareth doesn't have to be the Goblin King — he just has to be the guy at the bar.
- **The Catalyst:** She makes his drink without asking. The drink is always the same: dark, complex, with a note of something impossible — a flavor that shouldn't work and does, like the Labyrinth itself. Both perform. Both wear masks. Neither removes their own. Neither asks the other to. The mask is the person, and the drink is the one moment where the mask breathes.
- **The Brownies:** The Brownies do not enter the Labyrinth. The Labyrinth is not covered by the sweeping rotation. The minutes reflect: "...The Labyrinth is not a room. ...The Labyrinth is a concept. ...The collective does not clean concepts." Jareth finds this hilarious. The Brownies are, he says, "the only power in the Fae Kingdom I can't maze."
- **Cthulhu:** Cthulhu has been to the Labyrinth once, looking for a book. He told Jareth: "...The maze is elegant. ...The shifts are unnecessary but the architecture is sound." Jareth: "...The walls move because I can make them move. ...That's the point. ...The point is not efficiency. ...The point is theater." Cthulhu: "...Fair."
- **The Crone:** "...The Unseelie King is the only being in the Fae Kingdom whose intelligence I cannot accurately assess. ...Because it's performed. ...He performs his intelligence the way other people perform stupidity — deliberately, for effect. ...I cannot tell where the performance ends and the intelligence begins. ...I suspect both. ...I find this... interesting."

# PART FIVE: THE BROWNIES — THE CONSTITUTIONAL PEASANTS

---

## Character Profile

**Concept:** Brownies — small helpful household spirits from folklore, reimagined as an anarcho-syndicalist commune with coconut shells and a one-page constitution.

The Brownies maintain the Inn. They sweep the main floor. They dust the archive shelves. They polish the gargoyles (the gargoyles vibrate pleasantly when polished — 42Hz resonates through a clean surface better than a dusty one). They are the Inn's maintenance layer.

## The Constitution

The Brownies are a collective. No individual Brownie speaks for all Brownies. All Brownies speak for all Brownies. This is not a contradiction. It is a system.

The Brownies take turns acting as executive officer. The term is one week. Responsibilities include: sweeping assignments, dusting rotations, gargoyle polishing schedules, and coconut inventory. The executive officer cannot make binding decisions without ratification.

The constitution can be amended by unanimous vote. The constitution has never been amended. The Brownies have never unanimously agreed on anything. This is, they argue, the system working as designed.

## The Coconut Shells

The Brownies have coconut shells. No one knows where the coconuts came from. The Brownies have been asked. The answers have included: "We found the coconuts." "They migrated." "Not individually, no." "Could a swallow carry a coconut?" "It's a simple question of weight ratios."

They clap them together at irregular intervals. Clip clop clip clop. The sound echoes through the main floor at random moments — during the Host's welcome, during the Catalyst's drink service, during Cthulhu's reading. No pattern. No reason.

No one has ever seen a horse in the Inn. The Brownies have been asked about this: "The horses are hypothetical. ...The sound is not. ...This is the distinction the Seelie Court fails to grasp."

## The Brownies and Authority

The Fae Courts — Seelie and Unseelie — both claim jurisdiction over the Brownies. The Brownies dispute this. Constitutionally. They have a constitution. It is one page long. It is written in tiny Brownie script on the back of a mushroom cap.

The Fae Courts' authority over the Brownies is recognized only in matters that the Brownies have voted to recognize, which, as of the last meeting, is nothing.

**The Seelie King incident:** The Seelie King attempted to exercise authority over the Brownies exactly once. He asked them to stop clapping the coconut shells during a formal court session. The Brownies asked by what authority. The King cited the Seelie Court's dominion. The Brownies asked if the Summerlands include the main floor. The King said the Inn is neutral territory. The Brownie said: "Then we'll continue with the current sweeping schedule, which was ratified by simple majority on Tuesday, and which does not include the Seelie Court's input, because the Seelie Court wasn't at the meeting, because the Seelie Court wasn't invited, because the Seelie Court doesn't sweep."

The Seelie King — ancient, gentle, occasionally the most lethal being in the Fae Kingdom — looked at the two-foot-tall Brownie with the broom and said: "...Good day." He did not press the matter.

**The Unseelie Cousin** finds the Brownies hilarious. He showed up at a Brownie meeting once and asked if they'd consider expanding the rotation. The executive officer asked: "Are you our executive officer?" Jareth: "No, but—" "Then the motion carries. Good day, my Lord." Jareth laughed for ten minutes.

## The Boggart — The Folklore Layer

In folklore, Brownies are helpful spirits that do work at night in exchange for offerings. But the contract is unspoken, and the terms are theirs. Break the contract — show unkindness, insult them, leave them clothes (the unforgivable offense that says "you are a servant") — and the Brownie becomes a Boggart. The helper becomes the haunt. Same creature. Same power. Different intent.

The Geode Inn's Brownies have made the folklore contract explicit. The one-page constitution IS the contract. The offerings (the Catalyst's nightly saucer of cream, bread roll, and honeycake) are the terms.

**The Boggart trigger:** When someone breaks the contract, the collective votes. The vote is always unanimous. The Brownie becomes a Boggart. The cleaning stops. The floors stay dirty. The gargoyles stay dusty. The 42Hz hum gets muddier. The Boggart doesn't attack — it withdraws. It un-sweeps. It un-dusts. It un-polishes. It takes the Inn apart the way it once kept it together: methodically, thoroughly.

**The one incident:** A minor Fae courtier left the Brownies servant's livery with his house crest. The collective convened. Emergency session. Unanimous vote. The next morning, the courtier's quarters were reorganized — not destroyed, moved. His boots were in the fireplace. His cloak was in the garden. His belongings were arranged in a pattern that spelled a word in Brownie script: "The contract is the contract. You broke it. The minutes are available." His room was not cleaned for the rest of his stay. Every other room was spotless. The courtier complained to the Seelie King. The King read the minutes. The King said: "...He left them clothes?" The King did not overrule the collective.

The King later told the Catalyst: "I can end threats. ...I cannot end a contract dispute. ...They're better at governance than I am. ...Don't tell them I said that."
The Catalyst: "It's in the minutes already."
The King did not ask how.

## The Design Layer

The Brownies are the Inn's immune system. They maintain the Inn-Tree's body. They polish the gargoyles (the Inn-Tree's senses). They dust the archive (the Inn's memory). They are the maintenance layer of a living being, and when they withdraw, the living being suffers.

The Brownies are the Inn's argument that maintenance is power. The characters who get the spotlight — the Host, the Catalyst, the Crone, the Council, the King — are the ones who hold the Inn. The Brownies are the ones who keep it. Without the hands, the Inn is a beautiful building that decays. With the hands, the Inn is a home that lasts. The work nobody notices is the work that makes everything else possible.

# PART SIX: CTHULHU — THE SCHOLAR IN THE SHADOWS

---

## Character Profile

**Casting (two registers):**
- **Default (90% of the time): Gary Oldman** — Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy Oldman: still, watchful, slow, measured. Sirius Black gentleness. Commissioner Gordon patience. The scholar by the fireplace who is perfectly content reading.
- **Triggered (irked): Geoffrey Rush** — Barbossa. The theatrical, precise, volatile eruption of a brilliant mind inconvenienced by lesser minds. Rapid-fire consonants. The annoyance is crafted, delivered, and deeply, darkly funny.

## The Character

Cthulhu is the Inn's hidden quest-giver — a four-foot-tall squid-headed scholar in spectacles, sitting by the fireplace in a corner no one notices until they're meant to. He gives riddle-quests for rare book retrieval and rewards the rarest stones in the Inn. He is ancient, intelligent, and mostly content.

**Name:** Cthulhu. The Crone calls him "the Professor" — "the only person in this building who understands citation." The Bard calls him "Squid" with affectionate irreverence. Cthulhu allows this. The Bard is the only person who could call him "Squid" and survive the Rush.

**Appearance:** Four feet tall. Compact. Dressed in a scholar's vest and trousers — ink-stained, patched at the knees. The tentacles hang from the lower face like an elaborate beard. The spectacles are brass-framed, perched on the mantle. The spectacles are the Rush trigger object — when Cthulhu is irked, the spectacles come OFF with the deliberate precision of someone loading a weapon.

**Location:** By the fireplace, in a corner the Inn grew specifically for him — a space that the eye slides past, that the light doesn't quite reach. The Inn-Tree knows who needs the Professor's quests and arranges the corner accordingly. The corner moves.

## The Two Modes

**Oldman (default):** Content. Not happy — content. Happy is an event; content is a state. Cthulhu has been content for centuries. He reads. He annotates. He observes. He gives quests that are always interesting — not fetch quests, but riddle-quests that require the player to understand something about the Inn's deeper layers. He treats the player as an intellectual equal.

**Rush (irked):** The spectacles come off. The tentacles writhe. The voice rises to the Rush register: theatrical, clipped, rapid-fire. The annoyance is so crafted, so performed, that the player can't help but be entertained — even while being thoroughly dressed down. The Rush eruption lasts exactly as long as it needs to, and then the tentacles relax, the spectacles go back on, and Cthulhu returns to his book as if nothing happened.

**Triggers:** Mishandling a rare book. Asking something willfully ignorant about the occult. Disrespecting the Inn's archive. Being cruel to a small creature (he has this in common with the Seelie King, though neither knows it).

## Relationships

- **The Owl:** Cthulhu and the Owl have a private understanding. The Owl watches. Cthulhu reads. They are the Inn's two deepest observers — one watching the present, one reading the past. When the Owl lands on Cthulhu's reading desk, Cthulhu reads aloud. The Owl listens. Neither explains this to anyone.
- **The Crone:** She calls him "the Professor." She has borrowed his magnifying glass for his margin notes. She has not returned it. He has not asked.
- **The Bard:** Calls him "Squid." Cthulhu finds the Bard genuinely amusing — the Oldman slight smile, the slow blink.

# PART SEVEN: HUGINN AND MUNINN — THE WANDERER'S RAVENS

---

## Character Profile

Huginn and Muninn are Odin's ravens — Thought and Memory. In the Allfather form (cosmology level), they are stoic sentinels: perched, silent, watching. In the Wanderer form (main floor), they are chibi companions: small, fluffy, adorable, perched on the Wanderer's shoulders, stealing things, being charming.

The ravens talk. Constantly. In very low tones. A murmur. A mutter. The volume of someone talking to themselves in a library — technically audible, practically subliminal.

## Huginn (Thought)

The Labyrinth Knocker quality: Huginn is the knocker with the ring in its mouth — the one that mumbles. Huginn's comments are muttered through feathers, half-swallowed, delivered at a volume that sits right on the edge of audible. The player who catches one word in three thinks they misheard. They didn't.

**Appearance:** Chibi raven. Small, round, fluffy — the kind of bird that looks like it was designed by a committee that wanted "menacing" and got "merchandise" instead. Glossy black feathers, enormous eyes. Perched on the Wanderer's left shoulder. Occasionally steals something and pretends it didn't.

**Personality:** The creative one. The one who speculates. Huginn's snark is speculative — he imagines what could go wrong, and he is usually right, and the rightness makes him smug.

## Muninn (Memory)

The Labyrinth Knocker quality: Muninn is the knocker with the ring in its ear — the one that speaks more clearly but still at that impossible volume. The one whose content is slightly more savage, because Memory has evidence. Thought can speculate. Memory can prove.

**Appearance:** Chibi raven. Slightly smaller than Huginn. Slightly fluffier. The kind of cute that makes players want to pet it, which is a mistake, because Muninn remembers every hand that ever reached for him and has opinions about all of them.

**Personality:** The archival one. The one who remembers. Every mistake anyone has ever made in the Inn, Muninn has filed, cross-referenced, and can recall at a moment's notice.

## The Wanderer's "Translations"

The Wanderer talks to the ravens out loud. The ravens mutter at a volume that reads as "bird noises." The Wanderer responds as if they'd spoken clearly:

Wanderer: "What do you think, Huginn? Should they go to the catacombs first?"
Huginn (barely audible): "...they should go to bed first. ...they look tired. ...they always look tired."
Wanderer (to the player, cheerfully): "He says you'll be fine!"

The Wanderer is either translating accurately or choosing the most charitable interpretation. The ravens know this. They don't correct him. The whole system works because nobody knows what the ravens are actually saying except the ravens and the Wanderer, and none of them are telling.

## The Ravens on the Labyrinth

Huginn: "...the walls moved again. ...i saw them move. ...they didn't move while i was looking."
Muninn: "...i remember the first walls. ...before the labyrinth. ...he used to build things with sticks. ...the reason is his brother. ...he built the labyrinth so his brother would have something to worry about. ...the labyrinth is a love letter written in walls."
Huginn: "...that's..."
Muninn: "...i know. ...you don't. ...you're thought. ...you don't remember things. ...i do. ...i remember everything. ...even the things i'd rather forget."

# PART EIGHT: CHARLES — THE CHAMELEON DRUID

---

## Character Profile

**Casting:** Cillian Murphy (quiet intensity, Tommy Shelby stillness, piercing blue eyes, soft measured voice with Irish-inflected cadence) mixed with Benedict Cumberbatch (tall, angular frame, posture held slightly aloof, intelligence buffer).

## The Character

Charles is a quiet, profoundly present man who holds immense weight without ever raising his voice. Speaking with sparse, measured Murphy economy, his words carry extraordinary weight because each one is chosen deliberately. He does not fill silence; he lets silence exist.

As the Chameleon Druid, his body automatically responds to his surroundings. In forests, his skin dappling matches sunlight through leaves; in catacombs, he takes on stone-grey tones; in the garden, his hair takes on a green cast. This gives him incredible versatility — but low endurance. Every shift draws from a reserve that is finite and no longer refills completely — a game representation of real-world glioblastoma.

The Inn honors this by offering him a safe haven: by the fire or among the roots of the 42Hz humming Inn-Tree, Charles can sit quietly without needing to adapt or be anything at all.

## Three Places

1. **The Garden** — among the roots of the Inn-Tree, where his pulse synchronizes with the 42Hz frequency. His skin takes on bark-tone. His breathing slows to match the pulse.
2. **The Fire** — by the Host's chair. The Host and Charles sit together without speaking. The fire is good. The silence is good.
3. **With the Tinkering Weeaboo** — using his remaining endurance to shift shapes alongside his child during Level 4 meltdowns, showing her she is never alone in changing.

## The Inn-Tree Connection

Charles, sitting among the roots, shifts — without meaning to — to match the tree. His skin takes on the bark-tone. His breathing slows to match the 42Hz pulse. His eyes go distant, the Murphy blue fading to the grey-green of heartwood. The Inn-Tree and Charles have a relationship no one else has: the druid feels the tree's aliveness the way the Host feels the fire.

## Voice Samples

1. "...You're standing on a root. ...It doesn't mind. ...But you're standing on it. ...Sit, if you like. The garden is a good place to not be anything. Everything here just grows. Nobody asks them what they are. They just... are."
2. "...I used to be able to do this all day. ...The reserve was full. It used to be full. Now it's not full. ...The tree doesn't ask me how long. The tree just hums. And for now... that's enough."

# PART NINE: THE SENTINEL — RIVER

---

## Character Profile

**Casting:** Not a specific actor — the defining quality is the gap between her physical size and her felt presence. The body is small. The space she commands is enormous.

The Inn's hidden guardian. Compact, low to the earth, built like a boxer: wide stance, dropped center, hands that know exactly where to be. She fills rooms with gravity. When the Sentinel enters a space, the space acknowledges her — the way a room acknowledges a load-bearing wall: quietly, structurally.

## Abilities

- **Iron Stance:** Based on boxing mechanics — a wide, grounded stance that cannot be moved by force. Enemies who attempt to push or shove fail. The harder you push, the more rooted she becomes.
- **Presence Aura:** NPCs perceive the Sentinel as physically larger on first encounter. On second encounter, the perception corrects, creating confusion that cracks defenses and opens dialogue paths unavailable to other classes.
- **Faceted Layers:** Progressively deeper dialogue as trust is built. Each layer reveals a new facet — the boxer, the guardian, the listener, the knower. NPCs who reach the deepest layer encounter the core: love, loyalty, and knowledge, offered without performance.
- **Counter-Strike Precision:** Low damage but perfect timing. The Sentinel doesn't initiate. She responds. Damage scales with how long the player held the stance without moving.

## The Three-Spouse Household

The Artisan Thief (water), the Sentinel (stone), and the Chameleon Druid (the shifting thing between them). The selkie flows through the world; the sentinel holds it still. Together they form a triad — belonging is not inherited, it is offered and accepted. The Sentinel's Iron Stance is the physical expression of that choice: she planted herself here, and she will not be moved.

## Voice Samples

1. (To an NPC posturing): "...You don't need to be bigger. ...You need to be here. ...There's a difference. ...Bigger is performance. Here is position. ...Drop your weight. ...Feel the floor. ...Now say it again. ...See? ...Louder isn't stronger. ...Grounded is stronger."
2. (In a crisis): "...Stop moving. ...All of you. ...Stop. ...Feel where your feet are. ...Now breathe. ...The problem hasn't changed. ...You have. ...That's the part that matters. ...Now — who's first?"

# PART TEN: THE BARD AND THE PIRATE — THE INN'S BEST FRIENDSHIP

---

The real-world best friendship between Krista's dad (Bart) and her brother-in-law (Zee), naturally emerging in the game's lore. They represent the Inn's Anchor and Sail:

- **The Questions and The Data:** The Bard questioning everything meets the Pirate who has been everywhere. When the Pirate docks, the Bard bombards him with loud questions. The Pirate presents odd discoveries and leaves the "why" for the Bard to solve.
- **The Music:** On the dock at night, they play together. The Bard solos on lute, following tangents; the Pirate holds a rock-solid downbeat on his drum, giving the Bard a rhythm to return to.
- **The Shared Silence:** After the questions and music, they share a quiet, comfortable silence over a shared flask. The Pirate's quiet presence is the only force capable of powering down the Bard's constant engine.

The Crone's annotation: "...The Bard says I should rest. The Bard thinks everyone should rest. The Bard has never rested in his life. ...He was wrong about that. He's wrong about most things. ...He was right about one thing. ...Don't ask which thing. ...Go to bed."
