# THE CITIES OF THE DEAD — The Walkthrough Level (French Quarter / St. Louis No. 1)

**Ruled in (Krista, 2026-09-19):** a walkthrough level of fluid movement through the above-ground tombs of New Orleans' cemeteries. Built the same day as the funerary arts chapter (the Mask Chapter + the Carving Wing) — the trilogy's living street.

## WHAT THE LEVEL IS

The tomb city played as a CITY. The cemetery is not a dungeon and not a gallery — it is streets, rows, addresses, neighbors. The level's whole traversal design answers one question: how does the player move FLUIDLY through a city where every building is a tomb? Answer: the way New Orleans itself answers it — you walk the streets, you greet the neighbors, and the ground does the rest of the work.

**THE SCIENCE IS THE LESSON (honest-label, dated):** the above-ground burial is not gothic affectation. The water table REFUSES (the 1789 Spanish colonial mandate, engineering answering the flood: buried coffins came back up). The ground says no; the city of the living built a city for the dead above it. The player learns the water table the way the Catacombs teach the strata: the ground is the level's first character. The Bayou's water and the cemetery's water are the SAME water — the level sits on the Bayou zone's aquifer (the map tie).

## THE FLUID TRAVERSAL (the movement design)

The fluidity is three flows stacked:

1. **THE FOOT FLOW (the movement system):** narrow aisles, sunken brick, the broken-glass paths of the real cemetery rendered as the movement system's terrain register — the walk is unhurried, sidesteps and turns matter more than sprints. No combat, no chase mechanics anywhere in the level (the Sanctuary law at its most literal: the only city in the Inn where nothing can hurt you). The player's movement tier walks.
2. **THE TEMPO FLOW (the Two-Directions Law, already canon):** the music to the tomb is a slow dirge, the music from the tomb is a joyful parade — the player who follows a funeral band both ways learns the level's rhythm: the walk out and the walk home are one ceremony. The second-line beat at 50 BPM is the level's ground rhythm (the heartbeat's own answer to grief).
3. **THE CYCLE FLOW (the level's signature mechanic — THE YEAR-AND-A-DAY LAW):** the family vaults are not final addresses; they are REVOLVING ones. The real custom: after a year and a day, the vault's previous tenant has rejoined the ancestors and the space welcomes the next. THE MECHANIC: the player experiences the tomb city as a place in mid-cycle — vaults opened for tending, vaults closed for the year, one vault mid-ceremony. The oven tombs (the real name) teach the science: the Louisiana heat is the slow kiln — the same sun that fires the bricks finishes the work inside them (the TERRACOTTA KILN TIE: the mausoleum as kiln, the Carving Wing's cousin — the year-and-a-day is the fire that never goes out). The dead take turns. That is why the city has no full houses: the address never expires (the mausoleum register's law, the Ajivaka shelf's family), it just keeps a calendar.

## THE WALKTHROUGH (the level's rooms, in traversal order)

1. **THE GATE.** The wall and the narrow entry (the real St. Louis No. 1 gate — one body wide, deliberately: you enter the city of the dead single-file, you leave the same way). The dirge begins here.
2. **THE WALL VAULTS.** The perimeter: the stacked vaults in the wall itself — the city's most modest addresses, row on row. The honest-label plaque: the wall holds everyone (the register taught warm, never as poverty — the wall is still a street, the wall is still the city).
3. **THE STREETS (the family rows).** The main traversal: the aisles between family tombs, each tomb a house — names, dates, the carved lintels (the Carving Wing's street level: the letter carvers' work read row by row; the maker's marks on the bricks, the wu-le-gong-ming cousin). The player walks slowly here; the level reads like the Stacks read — accretion, not alphabet: families that were neighbors in life are neighbors here.
4. **THE OVEN TOMBS (the kiln row).** The cycle flow's home: the vaults mid-turn, the year-and-a-day taught by a tomb-tender NPC (the family member with the whitewash — the real practice: the family tends the tomb, paints it, keeps it; the tomb-tender is the level's warmest character: the address never expires BECAUSE someone keeps it). Quest candidate: the player helps with a tending (the whitewash, the flowers, the name re-cut) — the No-Fail chore whose reward is the story the tender tells.
5. **THE SOCIETY TOMBS.** The big houses: the firemen's tomb, the mutual aid societies (the real register: immigrants and workingmen's clubs who bought their members' addresses together). The community law made architecture: nobody buried alone because nobody could afford alone. Ties the Knitting Circle's register and the Coffee Girls' community table.
6. **MARIE'S TOMB.** The X-mark legend, already canon (one small true thing per player, once, never explained; the chalk gone when they turn back). The real tomb's custom honored straight; the voodoo craft layer stays under River's standing review gates. The busiest address in the city — the tomb that gets visitors the way the living city gets tourists.
7. **THE PYRAMID.** The modern tomb (the real one: the nine-foot pyramid in St. Louis No. 1, purchased by a living actor for a future address). The Modern Day Legends entry candidate: the city's newest neighbor, and the level's one wink — the funerary arts are not over; the last commission is still being commissioned.

## THE EXIT

The second line. The parade tempo takes the player back out the single-file gate, and the level's final honest label is the Two-Directions Law made visible: the same street, walked twice, two different musics. The walk out and the walk home are one ceremony — and the player exits the city of the dead at 50 BPM, which is the heartbeat, which is the point.
