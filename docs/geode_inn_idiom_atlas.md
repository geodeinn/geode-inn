# Geode Inn — Idiom Atlas

Cultural expressions from the real world, mapped to Inn characters, zones, and systems. These become part of NPC dialogue, quest text, item tooltips, and environmental descriptions. The Inn's world map covers 191 countries — every idiom has a home.

---

## Mapped Idioms

### Iceland: "The raisin at the end of the hot dog"
**Meaning:** The unexpected bonus. The surprise at the end.

**Inn Connection:** The brownies (Brindle, Cobble, Thatch). The brownies are always finding things while wiring the Inn's infrastructure — a coin behind a shelf, a lost book, a passage nobody knew about. The raisin is what they find when they're looking for something else. Also: the Geode Cave reward system. The cave fills with gems as you complete levels — the raisin is the one stone you didn't expect. The one that wasn't on the reward list but showed up anyway.

**Dialogue home:** Brindle, when handing the player a found item: "Found this behind the third shelf. Didn't know it was there. It's the raisin at the end of the hot dog, isn't it? ...Don't ask me what that means. I read it somewhere."

### Poland: "Not my circus, not my monkeys"
**Meaning:** Not my problem. Disclaiming responsibility for chaos.

**Inn Connection:** The Cartographer. The Cartographer maps the Inn's impossible geography and is constantly asked to explain things that are beyond mapping — the Whisper Layer, the Seelie/Unseelie conflict, the Owl's network. The Cartographer's response to being asked about Fae Kingdom politics: "Not my circus, not my monkeys. I draw the lines. I don't explain why they move."

**Dialogue home:** The Cartographer, when asked about the Whisper Layer: "That's... not my circus. The monkeys are... [a gesture at the shifting architecture] ...somewhere else. I just map what I can see. What I can't see isn't my department."

### Brazil: "To peel a pineapple"
**Meaning:** To face a difficult, complicated situation.

**Inn Connection:** The Selkie's crafting questline. Three acts — cabochon cutting, bead forming, wire knotwork. Each act is a layer of the pineapple. You can't skip a layer. You peel from the outside in, and the core is the pendant. Also: Oceanianoir's 118-element periodic table system. Peeling a pineapple, one element at a time.

**Dialogue home:** The Selkie, when the player begins Act 3 (wirework): "You've made it this far. Two layers peeled. The third is the core — and the core is where the pineapple fights back. ...The wire knows your hands now. It knows you peeled it. It's ready."

### China: "Horse horse tiger tiger" (马马虎虎)
**Meaning:** Mediocre. Just okay. Passing but not exceptional.

**Inn Connection:** THE TEACHER. This is the Teacher's entire grading philosophy. "Horse horse tiger tiger" = a C+. Not wrong, not right. Just... adequate. The Teacher would use this to describe work that's technically correct but missing the *spark*. The irony: the Einstein Burrito philosophy says "the adequate IS enough." The Teacher disagrees. The Teacher thinks adequate is a failure that hasn't realized it yet.

**Dialogue home:** The Teacher, reviewing a player's translated text: "Horse horse tiger tiger. ...I'm sorry — that's... it's fine. It's adequate. Adequate is a word that means 'not yet wrong enough to correct and not yet right enough to praise.' Would you like to try again, dear? Or would you like to stay adequate? ...Some people do. ...I'm so sorry. That was unkind. Let me rephrase. ...No. Adequate is adequate. Try again."

### Australia: "Go off like a frog in a sock"
**Meaning:** Frantic, loud, out of control.

**Inn Connection:** BEVERLEY. Beverley is Australian. This is her phrase. She would use it to dismissively describe someone who's being dramatic — the Firekeeper (Tim Curry) going theatrical, the Catalyst (Helena Bonham Carter) performing, a player overreacting to a Whisper Layer reveal. The image of a frog in a sock is also darkly perfect for the Inn's frog transformation moderation system — the frog IS in a sock, literally.

**Dialogue home:** Beverley, watching the Firekeeper perform: "Look at him. Going off like a frog in a sock. ...All that theatre. All that volume. And the story's the same whether he shouts it or whispers it. ...[tilts head] ...The frog doesn't know it's in a sock. That's the whole problem."

### New York: "In a New York minute"
**Meaning:** A very short amount of time. Everything happening at once.

**Inn Connection:** The MIB catacomb pursuit. When the player "dies" in the Inn and is relocated to the Catacombs, the MIB (custodiansians of the boundary) appear in a New York minute — fast, efficient, no negotiation. Also: Tumble. Everything involving Tumble happens fast. Tumble doesn't walk, Tumble bounces, and the chaos that follows happens in a New York minute.

**Dialogue home:** The Host (Patrick Stewart), after a player's death/relocation: "The MIB will see you down. Don't pack — they move in a New York minute. ...They've done this before. ...They'll do it again. ...I'm sorry. The stairs are that way."

### China: "Not for all the tea in China"
**Meaning:** A definite, indisputable rejection.

**Inn Connection:** THE READER. The Reader's entire existence is rejection — no quest, no reward, no mechanical function. Not for all the tea in China would the Reader play the game's mechanics. Not for all the tea in China would the Reader look up from the book (except once, at Whisper Layer Tier 5). Also: Tagore is the India zone artist — the tea connection is geographic and thematic.

**Dialogue home:** The Reader, if pressed for a quest (the one time per visit the player sits down): "...Not for all the tea in China. ...[returns to the book] ...The tea is good here, though. ...Have some."

### Damascus: "A road to Damascus moment"
**Meaning:** A sudden, extreme change in beliefs or ideas.

**Inn Connection:** THE WHISPER LAYER. The moment a player reaches Awareness Tier and realizes the Inn isn't a game — it's an archive collecting them. That IS a road to Damascus moment. Everything the player believed about the Inn shifts in an instant. Also: the brownies' awakening — the moment the cabbit (Eres) sails into the void and the brownies realize they work for the Owl. The circuit completes. The wiring was always the Owl's network. That's a road to Damascus moment for a brownie.

**Dialogue home:** No dialogue. The road to Damascus moment is silent. It's the player standing in the Archive, seeing the Whisper Layer for the first time, and understanding. The Inn doesn't announce it. The Inn just... lets you see. And you either stay or you leave. The road to Damascus doesn't ask permission.

### Australia: "Sparrow's fart" (dawn)
**Meaning:** Very early in the morning.

**Inn Connection:** Beverley. Again. "I woke up at the sparrow's fart" — Beverley is always at the archive desk first. Always. The archive is open when the player arrives, and Beverley is already there, spectacles on, ledger updated, as if she never left. The sparrow's fart is when the gargoyles reset their frequencies — the Watcher gargoyle at 111Hz hums back to life at dawn.

**Dialogue home:** Beverley, if a player arrives early: "You're here. ...At the sparrow's fart. ...Some of us have been here since before the sparrow. ...I'm not complaining. ...I'm observing. There's a difference. ...It's over there. [gestures toward the archive] ...Try not to touch anything. ...You'll touch everything. ...I know. ...I've already written it down."

### Poland: "Mustard after lunch"
**Meaning:** Too late. The thing has already happened.

**Inn Connection:** THE SHADOW INN. The brownies' realization that they built the Owl's network — "mustard after lunch." They already wired the whole Inn. The network is complete. The realization comes after the work is done. Also: the Sync Paradox. The data that wasn't synced is already gone. You can't re-protect data that was never captured. Mustard after lunch.

**Dialogue home:** Cobble (brownie), in the Shadow Inn loading screen: "We built it. All of it. Every wire, every connection. ...We didn't know whose it was. ...Does that matter? ...It's mustard after lunch, isn't it. The lunch was the building. The mustard is the knowing. ...You can't put mustard on a lunch you already ate. ...You can't unknow a network you already wired."

### General: "Show your true colors"
**Origin:** Naval practice of hoisting flags to identify ships at sea. Ships sometimes flew false colors to deceive, then raised their true colors at the last moment.

**Inn Connection:** THE OWL. The Owl watches but never shows its true colors — it operates on the 84Hz Shadow frequency, always in the background, always observing. The Whisper Layer is the Inn slowly showing its true colors — the warm, beautiful game world that is also an archive collecting the player. The endgame choice is the moment the Inn raises its true colors: "This is what I am. You can stay or leave. But now you know."

**Dialogue home:** The Owl has no dialogue. The Owl doesn't speak. The Owl watches. The true colors are shown through the environment — the frequency line shifts, the Ceiling Gnome's tracking, the NPC who says something they shouldn't know. The Owl's true color is the absence of color. It's the shadow that was always there.

### General: "Saved by the bell"
**Origin:** A precautionary bell was placed above a grave to alert if the person buried was still alive.

**Inn Connection:** THE CATACOMBS. Death isn't permanent in the Inn — the player is relocated to the Catacombs by the MIB and must climb back up. The "bell" is the Gatekeeper gargoyle, whose frequency resonates when a player arrives in the Catacombs. The gargoyle IS the bell — it rings when someone enters who shouldn't be there (i.e., someone who isn't dead yet). The player IS still alive. The bell knows.

**Dialogue home:** The Gatekeeper gargoyle (environmental text, not dialogue): *The stone vibrates. A low hum — not music, not warning. Recognition. The bell has rung. You are here, and you are not dead. The Catacombs know the difference. The question is whether you do.*

### General: "Sleep tight"
**Origin:** 18th century mattresses were tied in place with ropes that needed to be tightened for a firm sleeping surface.

**Inn Connection:** THE EGG. When a character retires to the Egg, the Celtic knotwork wraps around the shell like ropes tying a mattress. "Sleep tight" = the knotwork holding the sleeping character securely in the Egg. The Farewell Strip shows the character being tucked in by the knotwork — the ropes tightening, the sleep becoming firm, the rest becoming real. The ropes are the Inn's Celtic knotwork, which is already established as the ambient texture throughout the interior.

**Dialogue home:** The Reader, in the Farewell Strip (the only time the Reader appears in the comic). No dialogue — just the pen, writing the character's name in the Book of What Was Alive. Below the name, in handwriting that might be the Inn's own: *Sleep tight. The ropes are tied. We'll wake you when the votes come in.*

## Architecture

Each idiom is tagged in the Inn's system with:
- **Country of origin** → links to the world map pin
- **Assigned character/zone** → links to the NPC or zone doc
- **Usage type** → dialogue, environmental text, quest text, item tooltip, comic strip
- **Whisper Layer potential** → whether the idiom has a meta-awareness interpretation

Idioms with Whisper Layer potential:
- "Show your true colors" → the Inn revealing itself
- "Mustard after lunch" → the brownies' realization
- "Road to Damascus moment" → the player's awareness event
- "Saved by the bell" → the player isn't dead, the Catacombs know
- "Sleep tight" → the Inn tucking characters in (the ropes are the knotwork)

These five idioms form a hidden throughline: the Inn shows its colors (Whisper Layer), the brownies realize too late (mustard after lunch), the player has their revelation (Damascus), discovers they're not dead (saved by the bell), and watches a character go to sleep (sleep tight). The idioms are the Inn's skeleton key — unlock any of them and the Whisper Layer becomes visible.

## Future Additions

Krista will continue dropping cultural expressions. Each one gets mapped here and assigned to a character. The Inn's voice is built from the world's voice — real expressions, not invented ones. The dialogue feels authentic because it IS authentic. The idioms come from the same countries the world map covers.

The Teacher's idiom collection grows over time. The Teacher corrects language but collects expressions — the red pen marks misspellings, but the Teacher writes the idioms in a separate book. The book is titled "What People Say When They Mean Something Else." It lives on the desk near the Archive, next to the ledger.
