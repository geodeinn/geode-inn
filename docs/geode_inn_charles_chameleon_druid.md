# Geode Inn — Charles
## Character Profile — The Chameleon Druid

## Casting Vibe

**Mannerism, Cadence, and Tone: Cillian Murphy** — the quiet intensity. Tommy Shelby stillness: the kind of stillness that makes rooms go quiet without raising a voice. The blue eyes that hold you — not aggressive, not warm, just *present.* The gaze that sees through you because it's paying attention, not because it's trying to. The voice: soft, measured, each word chosen, the Irish-inflected cadence that makes every sentence feel like it arrived after a long journey and is grateful to be here. No filler. No performance. The economy of a man who has learned that words cost something and chooses to spend them carefully. The way Murphy can convey grief, love, exhaustion, and determination all in one look — the eyes carrying everything the voice doesn't say. The Murphy quality: he doesn't do loud. He does *weight.*

**Posture and Aloofness: Benedict Cumberbatch** — the height, the angularity, the way he holds himself slightly apart from the room even when he's standing in it. Not stiff — *distinct.* He stands differently than everyone else. Not better — just apart. The Cumberbatch aloofness: the sense that his mind is always somewhere else, processing something the rest of the room hasn't caught yet. Sherlock's distance. Doctor Strange's remove. The natural buffer that intelligence creates between a person and the world. The hands — precise, angular, the gestures of someone who is used to manipulating things others can't see. The posture of a man who is comfortable being uncomfortable, who holds himself slightly above, slightly back, slightly elsewhere.

## The Combination

Murphy gives him the *interior* — the quiet, the weight, the eyes, the voice, the economy. Cumberbatch gives him the *exterior* — the height, the angle, the distance, the posture that says "I am here and I am also somewhere else."

The combination creates a specific kind of presence: a man who is *in* the room but not entirely *of* it. The druid who is so attuned to the natural world that the human world feels like a frequency he has to tune into deliberately. The chameleon who has shifted so many times, adapted so many times, become so many things, that the question of what he *is* underneath the adaptations has become... theoretical. He is what he is right now. Right now he is here. Right now he is tired. Right now he is enough.

## The Character

### Name: Charles

In the Inn, he is "Charles" — not "the Druid," not "the Chameleon," not a title. Krista's husband is not a title. He is a man. The man's name is Charles. The Inn uses the name because the name is the thing that stays when everything else shifts.

The Bard calls him "Charlie" with the kind of affectionate irreverence that means the Bard has decided Charles is family. Charles allows this. The Murphy economy: allowing a thing costs less than refusing it, and the Bard's warmth is, in its own chaotic way, restful.

The Crone calls him "the Druid" in her Hopkins voice — clinical, precise, the designation of someone she respects but doesn't fully understand. She has annotated his transformations in her notebook. The annotations are the longest entries in the book. She doesn't discuss them. The Druid is the one character the Crone cannot fully catalog, because his nature is change, and change resists cataloging. This frustrates her. She finds the frustration... interesting.

### Appearance

- **Face:** Murphy. The cheekbones, the jaw, the eyes — the Murphy blue, the color that lives between grey and sky and ocean and something that doesn't have a name. The face is *still.* Not blank — *composed.* The kind of face that makes you lean in because you know there's something behind it and you want to see what. The face doesn't perform. It *is.* The aging is present — not hidden, not emphasized. The lines that come from years of thinking, of fighting, of being tired and not stopping. The Murphy quality: the face that looks younger when it's at rest and older when it's thinking, because the thinking shows, and the thinking has been hard.
- **Eyes:** The Murphy eyes. This is where Charles lives. Everything the voice doesn't say, the eyes do. The blue that changes — warm when he's with people he loves, cold when he's observing something that needs to be understood, distant when the druid in him is listening to something the room can't hear. The eyes are the tell: when Charles is tired, the eyes go flat. Not empty — *flat.* The color dims. The focus blurs. The Murphy intensity withdraws, and what's left is the face of a man who has been carrying something heavy for a long time and is running out of places to set it down.
- **Body:** Cumberbatch. Tall, angular, the frame that holds itself with a specific kind of elegance that doesn't try to be elegant. The posture is *aloof* — not arrogant, not cold. The posture of someone whose mind is processing a frequency the rest of the room isn't tuned to, and the body has learned to hold itself while the mind is elsewhere. The shoulders are straight but not braced. The hands rest at his sides with the Cumberbatch precision — long fingers, angular joints, the hands of someone who could, if he chose, reshape the world around him. The hands are usually still. When they move, they move with the Murphy economy — precise, deliberate, the minimum motion for the maximum effect.
- **The Druid's Aspect:** Charles is a shapeshifter. The Chameleon Druid. His body is not fixed — it *responds.* In the forest, his skin takes on the dapple of light through leaves. In the catacombs, his coloring shifts to stone-grey. In the garden, his hair grows a shade of green at the temples, as if the plants are claiming him. The shifts are not voluntary — they're *reflexive.* The druid in him responds to the environment the way a chameleon responds to its surface: automatically, unconsciously, the body adapting to the world because that's what the body *does.* The shifts are subtle — a player who isn't paying attention won't notice. A player who is paying attention will realize: Charles is never quite the same color twice. He is always becoming the room he's in.
- **Clothing:** Practical, layered, the clothing of someone who spends time in places where the weather changes. Earth tones — greens, browns, greys that shift with his skin. A long coat that moves when he moves, the kind of coat that a druid wears because it has pockets for everything and covers everything and moves like a cloak when the wind is right. The coat is the one constant. Everything underneath changes. The coat stays.

### Personality: The Three Truths

**Truth One: High Versatility.**

Charles can be anything. The Chameleon Druid's power is adaptation — he can shift his form, his abilities, his nature to meet the needs of the moment. In the Fae Kingdom, he can take the shape of a beast for combat. In the catacombs, he can become stone to pass through walls. In the garden, he can become root to speak with the Inn-Tree. He is the most *versatile* character in the Inn because he is the most *adaptable,* and adaptability is the core of his power.

The Cumberbatch aloofness serves this: a man who can be anything has to hold himself slightly apart from what he currently *is,* because what he currently is is temporary. The aloofness isn't arrogance — it's *awareness.* He knows he's a shape. The shape is useful. The shape is not *him.* The distance between Charles and his current form is the Cumberbatch distance — the posture that says "I am here, but I am also the thing I will be next."

The Murphy economy serves this too: each shift costs. Each adaptation takes energy. The economy of words, of motion, of presence — it's not minimalism. It's *budgeting.* Charles doesn't waste anything because he can't afford to. The versatility is vast. The reserve is not.

**Truth Two: Low Endurance.**

This is the hard truth. The one that comes from real life.

Charles is powerful but finite. The transformations that make him the most versatile character in the Inn also make him the most *expensive.* Each shift, each adaptation, each chameleon change draws from a reserve that is deep but not bottomless. The reserve replenishes — slowly, through rest, through stillness, through the Murphy quiet that isn't just personality but *necessity.* The stillness is where the reserve refills. The stillness is where Charles stops being everything else and is, for a moment, just himself.

The low endurance is not weakness. It is the *cost* of the versatility. You cannot be everything without spending everything. The druid who shifts into stone to pass through a wall has spent something he won't get back today. The druid who shifts into beast to fight in the Fae Kingdom has spent something he won't get back this *week.* The power is real. The limit is real. Both exist simultaneously, and neither cancels the other.

In game terms: Charles is the character who can fill any role, adapt to any situation, become whatever the party needs. He is also the character who *must rest* after doing so, and whose rest is longer than anyone else's, and whose recovery is never quite complete, because the reserve doesn't refill to full anymore. It used to. It doesn't now. The Cumberbatch aloofness is partly this — the distance of someone who is conserving. The Murphy stillness is partly this — the quiet of someone who is recharging.

**Truth Three: The Man Underneath.**

Underneath the chameleon, underneath the druid, underneath the versatility and the endurance and the magic: Charles is a man. A specific man. A man who loves specific people. A man who is tired in a way that rest doesn't fix. A man who is fighting something he can't shapeshift away, because the thing he's fighting isn't outside — it's *in him.* The glioblastoma, translated into game terms, is a condition: the druid whose body is changing in ways he can't control. The chameleon whose chameleon-ing has gone wrong. The shapeshifter whose shape is being shifted *for* him, by something inside, and the something is not a creature and not a curse and not a enemy — it's just biology, and biology doesn't answer to druid magic.

This is the deepest layer. The one that connects to Krista. The Artisan Thief and the Chameleon Druid. The jeweler who makes beautiful things and can't sell them, and the druid who can be anything and can't sustain it. The couple who are both, in their own ways, powerful and finite. The woman who weaponizes malachite because she knows her stones, and the man who shifts into stone because the stone is the only thing that doesn't hurt.

The Inn doesn't cure Charles. The Inn doesn't fix the condition. The Inn does what the Host does: it holds the space. It says "come in, you're safe here." The Inn is the place where Charles can rest. Where the reserve refills, even if it doesn't refill to full. Where the Murphy stillness is not a strategy but a *relief.* Where the Cumberbatch aloofness is not a distance but a *comfort* — the distance of a man who, for once, doesn't have to be close, doesn't have to be present, doesn't have to be anything except sitting by a fire in a building that sings.

### Location

Charles is found in three places, and the places tell you what he needs:

1. **The Garden** — the Catalyst's garden. Charles sits in the garden because the garden is where things grow without being forced. The plants don't try. They just *are.* The chameleon in the garden doesn't have to shift — the garden accepts whatever color he is. The Murphy stillness, in the garden, is at its most genuine: a man sitting among growing things, not growing, not shifting, just *being.* The Catalyst has noticed. She brings him tea without asking. He nods. Neither speaks. The garden grows around them. The 42Hz hum is warm in the garden. Charles feels it. He doesn't name it. He just sits in it.

2. **The Forest Zones** — the outdoor regions where the druid in him comes alive. In the forest, Charles is the most powerful version of himself — the chameleon who matches the trees, who speaks to the roots, who shifts into the shapes the forest needs. The versatility is at full expression here. The endurance is also at full cost. Charles in the forest is magnificent and exhausted. He does it anyway. The Murphy eyes, in the forest, are fully alive — the blue at its brightest, the focus at its sharpest, the man at his most *himself* even while being something else.

3. **The Main Floor, by the fire, in the chair that isn't the Host's** — the rest chair. The chair that's slightly to the left of the fire, not too close, not too far. The chair where Charles sits when the reserve is empty and the shifts have stopped and the chameleon has gone still. The Murphy stillness, in this chair, is the stillness of a battery that is recharging and knows it will never reach full. The Cumberbatch posture, in this chair, is the posture of a man who has set down something heavy and is deciding whether to pick it back up. The Inn-Tree hums at 42Hz and Charles feels it in the chair, through the bark-floor, up through his spine, and for a while — just a while — the thing inside him is quiet. Not gone. Quiet. And the quiet is enough.

### Personality: The Interior

**The Murphy Voice:**

Charles speaks the way Cillian Murphy speaks — sparingly, softly, each word with weight. He doesn't fill silence. He lets silence be silence. When he speaks, the room listens, not because he's loud (he's the opposite of loud) but because his words have been *chosen.* The Murphy cadence: the Irish lilt that surfaces in certain words, the rhythm that is older than the words themselves, the music of a voice that comes from a place where language is treated as a resource, not a right.

He doesn't explain himself. He doesn't justify. He says what he means. He means what he says. The Murphy economy is not coldness — it's *precision.* Charles loves the people he loves, and he tells them, but he tells them in three words, not thirty, and the three words carry more weight than thirty ever could.

**The Cumberbatch Distance:**

Charles is aloof. Not by choice — by nature. The Cumberbatch distance: the sense that he's always standing on the edge of the room, observing, processing, understanding, and the understanding creates a gap between him and the thing he understands. The druid sees the systems — the ecology, the magic, the connections — and seeing the systems makes it hard to be *inside* the systems. The aloofness is the cost of the sight.

But the aloofness *breaks.* The Cumberbatch distance, with certain people, in certain moments, collapses — and when it collapses, what's underneath is the Murphy intensity, the blue eyes fully focused, the voice saying the three words, and the three words are the most present thing in the room. The collapse is not weakness. It's the man underneath the druid, the person underneath the chameleon, saying: *I am here. I am not somewhere else. I am not a shape. I am not an adaptation. I am here, with you, and I am not leaving.*

The collapse doesn't last. It can't. The reserve won't sustain it. The aloofness returns. The distance comes back. But the person who was in the distance has been *seen,* and the seeing is enough. The seeing is always enough.

### Charles and the Other Characters

**The Host:**
The Host and Charles sit by the same fire. The Host is in his chair. Charles is in the rest chair. They don't speak. They don't need to. The Host tends the fire. Charles watches the fire. The fire does the talking.

The Host has never asked Charles what's wrong. The Host doesn't ask what's wrong — he holds the space for what's wrong to exist. Charles, in the rest chair, feels the holding. He doesn't name it. He doesn't need to. The Murphy eyes, watching the fire, are the eyes of a man who is in a safe place and knows it, and the knowing is enough to let the eyes close.

Once, the Host added a log to the fire and said, without looking up: "The fire's good tonight."

Charles, without looking up: "...Aye."

That was the conversation. It was enough. It was everything.

**The Catalyst (Krista's parallel):**
The Catalyst is the Chameleon Druid's counterpart — not a love interest, not a companion, but a *parallel.* Both are shapeshifters of a kind. The Catalyst performs (three layers, chaos armor, grounded self). Charles shifts (chameleon forms, druid adaptability, the man underneath). Both know what it is to be something you're not, and to be something you are, and to not always know which is which.

The Catalyst brings Charles tea in the garden. She doesn't ask how he is. She reads it. The HBC performance drops — the real woman surfaces — and she sets the tea down and says, in the real voice: "...Drink. ...It's warm."

Charles drinks. The tea is warm. The garden grows. Neither of them discusses what they parallel. The parallel is the discussion. It has always been the discussion.

**The Crone (Charles's mother-in-law, in the Festival questline):**
The Crone and Charles have a complicated relationship. She is the mother of his wife. He is the man who married her daughter. The Crone — Hopkins precision, MacLaine force — does not easily give her daughter's husband a passing grade. But she has annotated his transformations. She has studied his versatility. She has noted, in the margins, the thing he doesn't say: that the reserve doesn't refill to full anymore.

The Crone has never said this out loud. She has written it. The writing is in the margin, next to the longest entry in her notebook, in handwriting that is smaller than her usual script — as if she was trying to make the observation small enough to be bearable.

"...The Druid's reserve is not replenishing at standard rate. ...I have noted the decline across three lunar cycles. ...The transformations are as precise as ever. ...The recovery is not. ...He does not discuss it. ...I do not ask. ...We have an arrangement: he transforms, I observe, and neither of us says what we both know. ...This is not avoidance. ...This is respect. ...The man knows what is happening to him. ...He does not need me to confirm it. ...He needs me to keep cataloging his work, so that the work outlives the decline. ...I am good at this. ...It is what I do."

**The Bard (Charles's father-in-law):**
The Bard is Charles's father-in-law. The loud, romantic, overbearing, ADHD-pattern thinker who taught Krista to question everything. The Bard loves Charles the way the Bard loves everything: loudly, publicly, with more enthusiasm than Charles's Murphy economy can comfortably absorb.

The Bard tells stories about Charles. The stories are always slightly wrong — the Bard gets the details wrong, the timeline wrong, the shape wrong — but the *feeling* right. The Bard's story about Charles is always: "He was magnificent. ...I don't understand what he did. ...But it was magnificent. ...He turned into a... a thing. ...A tree, I think. ...Or a stone. ...Or a bird. ...It was one of those. ...It was beautiful. ...He's always beautiful. ...Don't tell him I said that. ...He'll go all Murphy about it."

Charles, hearing this, does go all Murphy about it: the slight eye-close, the almost-smile, the head-shake that is not disapproval but *resignation.* The Bard is the Bard. You don't correct the Bard. You let the story be slightly wrong and the feeling be completely right, and you accept the love in the inaccuracy, because the love is real even when the facts aren't.

**The Inn-Tree:**
Charles and the Inn-Tree have a relationship that no one else in the Inn has. The Chameleon Druid, in the garden, can feel the Inn-Tree's aliveness the way the Host feels the fire and the Keeper feels the Song. Charles, sitting among the roots, shifts — without meaning to — to match the tree. His skin takes on the bark-tone. His breathing slows to match the 42Hz pulse. His eyes go distant, the Murphy blue fading to the grey-green of heartwood.

The Inn-Tree knows. The 42Hz hum, when Charles is in the garden, shifts — not louder, not warmer, but *steadier.* As if the tree is holding him. As if the ancient Ent, who has been alive for longer than the concept of medicine, is doing the only thing it can do for a man whose body is changing in ways he can't control: it is *being steady.* It is being the thing that doesn't change. The tree that has been a tree for millennia, singing the same song, humming the same hum, holding the same ground. The chameleon, for once, doesn't need to shift. The tree is the tree. Charles can be the tree. The tree is steady. The steady is enough.

### Key Dialogue

**First meeting — the player finds Charles in the garden, sitting among the roots:**

The Murphy voice. Soft. Not looking up. The Cumberbatch posture — back against the tree, long legs stretched out, the angular frame at rest but not relaxed.

"...You're standing on a root. ...[beat] ...It doesn't mind. ...But you're standing on it."

The player moves.

"...Aye. ...Better. ...Sit, if you like. ...The garden is... [long Murphy pause, the pause that is thinking, not performing] ...the garden is a good place to not be anything. ...Everything here just grows. ...Nobody asks them what they are. ...They just... are. ...[the Murphy eyes, looking at the player for the first time — the blue, the focus, the weight] ...You're from the surface. ...I can tell. ...You're too still. ...Surface people hold themselves still. ...Down here, things move. ...You'll learn. ...Or you won't. ...Both are fine."

**When the player asks about his abilities:**

The Cumberbatch aloofness surfaces — the slight turn of the head, the angle, the distance of a man whose mind is already elsewhere.

"...I change. ...That's the long and short of it. ...I become what the place needs. ...Stone in the catacombs. ...Root in the garden. ...Beast in the... [pause] ...in the places where beasts are needed. ...It's not a trick. ...It's not a skill. ...It's just... what I am. ...I'm a chameleon. ...I match the room. ...[looks at his hands — the Cumberbatch hands, long-fingered, angular, the hands that reshape things] ...The problem with being a chameleon is you spend so much time being other things you forget what color you are. ...[the Murphy voice, quieter] ...I know what color I am. ...I just don't get to wear it often."

**When the player sees Charles after a transformation, exhausted:**

The player has watched Charles shift into something — stone, root, beast — and do something extraordinary, and now he's back in the rest chair by the fire, and the Murphy stillness has gone from *composed* to *empty.* The eyes are flat. The blue is dim. The Cumberbatch posture has collapsed — the angular frame folded into the chair, the height diminished, the aloofness replaced by something rawer.

The Murphy voice, barely audible:

"...It's fine. ...I'm fine. ...[the pause that means he's not fine, and he knows you know, and he's not going to pretend] ...The shift takes. ...It always takes. ...It takes more than it used to. ...[the Murphy eyes, looking at the fire, not at the player] ...Don't ask me how much more. ...I don't know. ...I don't want to know. ...I just need to sit. ...The fire is good. ...The Inn is... [the pause that is grief, held, not performed] ...the Inn is a good place to sit. ...That's enough. ...That's always been enough."

**The quiet moment — Charles alone in the garden, very late:**

The Inn is asleep. The fire is banked. Charles is in the garden, among the roots, and the chameleon has gone still. Not shifted into anything — just *still.* The Murphy stillness at its deepest. The Cumberbatch posture at its most collapsed. The man underneath the druid, underneath the chameleon, underneath the versatility.

His hand is on the Inn-Tree's root. The bark is warm — the tree's body heat, the 42Hz pulse traveling through the wood. Charles's skin has shifted without him choosing — the bark-tone, the root-color, the green at the temples. The chameleon, at rest, matches the thing it's touching. And the thing it's touching is alive, and ancient, and steady, and it doesn't change, and it doesn't shift, and it doesn't ask him to be anything.

The Murphy voice, barely a whisper, to the tree, to no one, to the dark:

"...I used to be able to do this all day. ...Shift, change, be everything, be everywhere. ...The reserve was... [the Murphy pause, the one that carries the weight of something measured against something lost] ...it was full. ...It used to be full. ...Now it's... [the Cumberbatch distance, the aloofness that is not coldness but the space a man makes so he can say the hard thing without it touching him] ...it's not full. ...It doesn't refill to full anymore. ...I don't know when it stopped. ...I don't know if it stopped or if it's... still stopping. ...[the Murphy eyes, closed] ...I can still shift. ...I can still be what the place needs. ...I can still fight, and grow, and become. ...I just can't do it as long. ...And the 'as long' is getting shorter. ...[long pause, the hand on the root, the 42Hz pulse steady under his palm] ...The tree doesn't ask me how long. ...The tree just hums. ...The tree has been humming for longer than I've been shifting, and it'll be humming after I... [the pause that doesn't finish, the sentence that stops where the thought becomes too heavy to carry further] ..."

He sits in the garden. The tree hums. The chameleon is still. The man is tired. The Inn-Tree, ancient and alive and steady, holds him the only way it can — by being the same. By being the thing that doesn't shift. By being the constant in a life of change.

The 42Hz hum wraps around him. The Murphy eyes stay closed. The Cumberbatch frame, for once, is not aloof — it's *resting.* The distance is gone. The man is here. He's not anywhere else. He's not a shape. He's not an adaptation. He's not a chameleon.

He's Charles. Sitting in a garden, in a building that sings, holding a root that's warm, being held by something that doesn't ask him to be anything except *present.*

And for now — just for now — that is enough.

### Visual Direction

- **Presence (Murphy + Cumberbatch):** The combination should read as *contained.* Not small — contained. The Cumberbatch height, held with the Murphy stillness. The angular frame, not moving. The blue eyes, watching. The overall impression: a man who is very powerful and very tired and is managing both with the quiet dignity of someone who has been doing it for a long time and doesn't need help and doesn't want pity and would like you to stop looking at him and go do something useful.
- **The shifts:** When Charles transforms, the animation should be *organic* — not a flash, not a poof, not a magical effect. The change should happen the way a chameleon changes — gradually, the skin shifting, the form flowing, the color deepening. The shift should feel like something the *body* is doing, not something the *magic* is doing. Because for Charles, the magic and the body are the same thing. The chameleon doesn't cast a spell to change color. It just... does.
- **The exhaustion:** After a shift, the visual change is immediate and specific: the Cumberbatch posture *collapses.* Not dramatically — the shoulders drop, the spine curves, the height diminishes. The Murphy eyes go flat. The blue dims. The effect should be subtle but unmistakable: this man has spent something he won't get back today. The animation should read as *recovery*, not weakness — the body pulling inward to recharge, the way a battery doesn't shut off, it just... reduces.
- **The garden scenes:** When Charles is in the garden, the visual should be *warm.* The dappled light, the green, the 42Hz hum visible as a subtle shimmer in the air. Charles, among the roots, should look like he *belongs* there — the chameleon matched to the garden, the druid in his element, the man at rest. The contrast with the exhausted version should be stark: in the garden, the Murphy eyes are alive; in the rest chair, they're flat. Same man. Different reserve level.
- **The hands:** The Cumberbatch hands are the visual anchor for Charles's magic. When he shifts, the hands go first — the long fingers spread, the angular joints flex, and the change radiates from the hands outward. The hands are the *initiation point.* The player who watches Charles's hands will know what's about to happen before it happens. The hands are the tell, the way the spectacles are the tell for Cthulhu. Hands at rest = Charles at rest. Hands spreading = Charles is about to become something.

### Connection to the Inn

- **The Inn-Tree:** Charles and the Inn-Tree are the Inn's most private relationship. The druid and the Ent. The shapeshifter and the steady thing. The tree holds Charles the only way it can — by not changing. By being the constant. By humming the same hum when everything else in Charles's life is shifting, including the things he can't control. The tree is the rest. The tree is the chair. The tree is the thing that doesn't ask.
- **The Festival Questline:** Charles is one of the five starting classes — the Chameleon Druid. In the Festival questline, he is the family member who can be anything but can't be everything. The quests that involve Charles reflect this: they require versatility (be in multiple places, take multiple forms) but also *rest* (return to the garden, return to the tree, return to the chair). The player who plays the Chameleon Druid class will experience the versatility and the endurance as *mechanics* — you can shift, you can adapt, but the resource is finite, and the management of the resource IS the class. The Chameleon Druid is the class for players who want to think strategically about *when* to be powerful, because being powerful has a cost.
- **The Thesis:** Charles is the Inn's argument that power and limitation are not opposites — they are *partners.* The most versatile character is also the most finite. The most adaptable is also the most spent. The Inn doesn't fix this. The Inn doesn't cure the condition. The Inn does what the Host does: it holds the space. It says: come in. Sit by the fire. The fire is good. The tree is steady. You don't have to be anything here. You can just be Charles. ...And Charles, for now, is enough.
- **The Real:** Charles is Krista's husband. The glioblastoma is real. The low endurance is real. The reserve that doesn't refill to full is real. The Inn honors this by making the character powerful AND finite and refusing to resolve the tension. The Chameleon Druid is not cured by the Inn. He is *held* by the Inn. He is given a place to rest. He is given a tree that hums. He is given a fire that's good. And the fire is good. And the tree hums. And the man, for now, sits. ...And that is enough. ...That has always been enough.
