# Geode Inn — The Selkie's Craft: A Three-Part Crafting Quest Line
## Games: Cabochon Cutting, Bead Forming, Wirework
## Character: The Selkie — Krista's Character

---

## THE SELKIE

The Selkie is Krista's character in the Geode Inn. A creature of two worlds — seal in the water, human on the stone. The Selkie lives at the boundary: the tideline, the water's edge, the place where ocean meets land and both are true.

The Selkie is the Inn's master crafter. Not because the Selkie studied craft — because the Selkie IS craft. The ocean is the original lapidary. Every beach pebble is a natural cabochon, tumbled smooth by a million tides. Every shell is a bead, drilled by the sea. Every strand of kelp is wirework, woven by the current. The Selkie learned craft from the ocean, and now teaches it to the player.

The Selkie's workshop is in the Tidal Caves — the water zone where the ocean meets the Inn's foundation. The workshop is half-submerged. The grinding wheel is powered by the tide. The polishing station is the beach itself. The Selkie works with wet hands, because wet hands understand stone better than dry ones. The 42Hz hum is the ocean's frequency here — the deep bass of water moving through stone.

The Selkie's quest line has three acts, each a crafting game:
1. **Cutting Cabochons** — the stone's first form
2. **Forming Beads** — the stone's portable form
3. **Wirework** — the metal that holds the stone

Each act is a game. Each game teaches a real craft. Each craft is a transformation. The Selkie's story is about becoming — the raw becoming the refined, the rough becoming the precious, the formless becoming the formed. The same transformation the Selkie performs every time the skin comes off and the human emerges.

## ACT 1: CUTTING CABOCHONS — "The Stone's First Form"

### The Game

The player stands at the Selkie's grinding wheel in the Tidal Caves. A piece of rough stone sits on the bench — uncut, weathered, hiding its color beneath a rind of matrix. The player's job: reveal the cabochon inside.

**Step 1: Reading the Rough**
- The player examines the rough stone — rotating it, looking at all sides
- The stone's internal structure is partially visible — veins of color, fractures, the hint of pattern beneath the rind
- The player marks the "saw line" — where to split the rough to expose the best color
- Get this wrong: you waste material, the cabochon is small or off-color
- Get this right: you expose the stone's best face
- The Selkie watches and says nothing. The ocean doesn't tell you where to cut. You learn by looking.

**Step 2: Sawing**
- The player draws the rough across the saw (a slow, precise drag — the saw blade is a circular line on the screen)
- The saw cuts through the matrix. The player FEELS the resistance — different stones resist differently (hard stones = slow, soft stones = fast)
- The saw line must be straight and controlled — waver and you waste material or crack the stone
- The saw reveals the stone's interior for the first time — the "window." The color. The pattern. The Selkie's first words: "There. That's what was inside."

**Step 3: Preforming**
- The player grinds the rough into a preform — the basic outline of the cabochon shape (round, oval, or organic)
- The grinding wheel is circular — the player holds the stone against it and moves it to shape the outline
- The player controls: pressure (heavy = fast but risks cracking, light = slow but safe), angle (determines the curve), position (which part of the wheel — coarse grinding on the left, fine on the right)
- The preform must be symmetric (for round/oval) or follow the stone's natural shape (for organic)
- The stone gets HOT — the player must dip it in water periodically (the Tidal Caves' water is right there). Let it get too hot and it cracks. This is real lapidary practice.

**Step 4: Doming**
- The player creates the cabochon's dome — the curved top that gives cabochons their character
- The doming wheel is a curved surface. The player moves the stone in increasing circles, gradually curving the top from flat to dome
- The dome's height is the player's choice: low dome (subtle, elegant), medium dome (classic), high dome (dramatic, shows chatoyancy/asterism best)
- The Selkie explains: "Star stones want a high dome. The star needs room to reach you. Flat stones want a low dome — they're already what they are."
- The player learns which stones want which dome — connecting to the database's opticalPhenomenon field

**Step 5: Polishing**
- The player polishes the cabochon on progressively finer grits — from coarse (like sandpaper) to fine (like silk)
- Each grit removes the scratches from the previous one. The surface goes from matte to satin to glass-smooth.
- The final polish reveals the stone's true color and phenomenon — the star appears, the cat's eye opens, the play-of-color flashes
- The moment of revelation: the stone, finished, sitting in the player's hand. The Selkie: "The ocean spends a thousand years on a pebble. You did it in an afternoon. Same result. The ocean just has more patience."

### The Quest Context

The Selkie gives the player a specific stone to cut — a rough that's been sitting in the Tidal Caves for centuries, tumbled by the tide but never cut. The stone is a phenomenal stone (star sapphire, moonstone, or tiger's eye — determined by the player's zone progress). The cabochon the player cuts becomes a quest item — the first stone the player has CUT rather than collected.

The completed cabochon is placed in the player's inventory — a new category: "cut stones" (distinct from "found stones" collected from games). The Selkie's first lesson: found stones are gifts. Cut stones are craft. Both are valuable. But only one is YOURS.

### Difficulty Progression

- **Tier 1:** Soft stones (turquoise, lapis, malachite) — easy to cut, forgiving, fast results. The tutorial.
- **Tier 2:** Medium stones (quartz, jade, agate) — harder, slower, require more patience. The practice.
- **Tier 3:** Hard stones (sapphire, ruby, corundum) — very hard, slow cutting, high risk of cracking. The mastery.
- **Tier 4:** Phenomenal stones (star sapphire, cat's eye, moonstone) — must be oriented correctly to show the phenomenon. The orientation is a puzzle — the star only appears if the dome is centered on the right axis. The master's test.

## ACT 2: FORMING BEADS — "The Stone's Portable Form"

### The Game

The cabochon sits in the palm. Beautiful. But a cabochon is SET — it lives in metal, in one place. A bead is CARRIED — it goes with you. The Selkie's second lesson: some stones want to stay. Some stones want to travel.

**Step 1: Splitting**
- The player splits the rough into bead-sized pieces — smaller than cabochon rough, more pieces from the same stone
- Each piece must be roughly equal in size — the player marks the split lines
- More pieces = more beads but smaller. Fewer pieces = fewer beads but larger. The player chooses based on the stone's character (some stones want to be one large bead, some want to be many small ones)

**Step 2: Shaping**
- The player shapes each bead on the grinding wheel — choosing from the 8 smooth shapes or 8 faceted shapes (from the Bead Shape Catalog)
- For smooth shapes: grind to the outline (round, barrel, coin, etc.), then tumble-polish
- For faceted shapes: grind the outline, then cut each facet individually — the player controls the angle and pressure of the facet cut
- The faceting minigame: each facet is a cut at a specific angle. The player aligns the stone on the faceting head (a protractor-like interface), sets the angle, and makes the cut. 57 facets on a round brilliant. Each one must be precise. The player who completes a faceted bead has made 57 precise cuts. The Selkie: "The ocean makes one cut — erosion. You make fifty-seven. That's why your bead shines and the pebble doesn't."

**Step 3: Drilling**
- The player drills the hole through the bead — the channel that makes it stringable
- The drill is a vertical boring tool. The player controls: drill speed (fast = risks cracking, slow = safe), pressure (heavy = risks blowing out the exit hole, light = clean), and alignment (the hole must go straight through the center for symmetric beads)
- For asymmetric shapes (teardrop, pear): the hole goes through the narrow end — the player must align correctly
- The drill breaks through the other side — the moment the hole is complete, the bead becomes a BEAD. Not a stone. A bead. Something that can be strung, worn, carried.

**Step 4: Polishing**
- Same progressive polishing as cabochons, but now the bead rolls — the player tumbles beads in a drum (the tumbler is the game's nod to Tumble, the 4-inch fuzzball)
- The tumbler spins. The beads click against each other. The sound is the beads' first composition — random, chaotic, but MUSICAL. Each stone has its own tone (the Mohs-to-frequency system). The tumbler is a preview of the Bead Composer.
- The Selkie: "Listen. That's what they sound like before anyone arranges them. The Song isn't composed. It's found."

### The Quest Context

The Selkie gives the player a rough that yields multiple beads — a stone that wants to travel (turquoise, amber, coral — stones historically carried along trade routes). The player cuts the beads, drills them, polishes them, and strings them on a simple cord.

The strung beads become the player's first bead composition — a single-strand necklace of identical beads. Simple. But the Selkie: "Every composition starts with one string of one stone. The Song starts with one note. You just played it."

This connects directly to the Bead Composer — the beads the player cuts in the Selkie's workshop can be loaded into the Composer for arrangement. The crafting game feeds the composition game. The stone you cut becomes the note you play.

### Difficulty Progression

- **Tier 1:** Soft stones, smooth shapes, large beads — the basics
- **Tier 2:** Medium stones, all smooth shapes, smaller beads — practice
- **Tier 3:** Hard stones, faceted shapes — the faceting minigame unlocks. 57 cuts per bead. Precision work.
- **Tier 4:** Mixed-material beads — combining two stones in one bead (a turquoise bead with a quartz cap, a lapis bead with a metal inlay). The Selkie's master technique.

## ACT 3: WIREWORK — "The Metal That Holds"

### The Game

The player has stones. The player has beads. Now the player needs to HOLD them — to set cabochons, to string beads, to create jewelry. The metal is the frame. The wire is the weave. The Selkie's third lesson: the stone is the story. The wire is the telling.

This is the knotwork game from the previous design doc, now integrated as the Selkie's third act. The two modes (drag and click) are both taught by the Selkie:

**Drag mode** — the Selkie teaches this first. The player weaves cord and wire along guide lines on the leather table. The Selkie: "The ocean doesn't plan. It moves. It finds the path. Weave like water — let the wire find its way."

**Click mode** — the Selkie teaches this after the player has mastered drag mode. The player places waypoints, routes the wire precisely, sets over/under at each crossing. The Selkie: "Water finds the path. But sometimes you need the path to find the water. This is for when you know where you're going."

### The Quest Context

The Selkie gives the player a specific commission: create a wire-wrapped setting for the cabochon cut in Act 1. The cabochon — the stone the player revealed from the rough — now needs a frame. The player designs the wire setting in click mode (or weaves it in drag mode), and the completed piece is a pendant: the cabochon in its wire frame, on a cord of beads from Act 2.

The three acts produce ONE piece of jewelry:
- Act 1 → the cabochon (the stone)
- Act 2 → the beads (the cord)
- Act 3 → the wirework (the setting)

The finished pendant is the Selkie's gift to the player — the proof that the player can transform raw material into finished jewelry. The Selkie: "You took a stone from the beach. You cut it. You drilled it. You wrapped it. You wore it. That's the whole story. Everything else is variation."

### Connecting to the Market

The pendant the player creates can be:
- **Worn** — appears on the player's avatar
- **Listed** — placed on the artisan market as a player-made item
- **Prototyped** — the spec (cabochon cut + bead string + wire pattern) exports to Soulstice's workshop for physical production
- **Gifted** — given to an NPC (the Selkie, a companion, the Keeper)

The digital-to-physical pipeline now has THREE stages: stone cutting → bead forming → wire wrapping → finished piece → workshop export → real jewelry.

## THE SELKIE'S QUEST LINE — NARRATIVE

### The Story

The Selkie's skin is missing. Not stolen — the Selkie made a choice. The Selkie gave the skin to the ocean, and the ocean gave back a stone. The stone is the Selkie's new skin — a cabochon of moonstone, cut by the tide, polished by the beach, carried in the Selkie's human hands.

The quest line is the Selkie's journey of transformation — from sea creature to stone worker, from skin-bearer to craft-maker. Each act is a step in the Selkie's becoming:

**Act 1 (Cabochon):** The Selkie teaches the player to cut stone because the Selkie remembers being cut — shaped by the ocean, tumbled by the tide, polished by the sand. The Selkie knows what it is to be rough and become smooth. The first cabochon the player cuts mirrors the moonstone the Selkie carries — the stone that replaced the skin.

**Act 2 (Beads):** The Selkie teaches the player to form beads because the Selkie remembers being carried — the ocean carries everything, and everything the ocean carries becomes smooth, becomes round, becomes a bead. The first beads the player strings mirror the pearls the Selkie wore as a seal — the ocean's beads, made by water, worn by water.

**Act 3 (Wirework):** The Selkie teaches the player to weave wire because the Selkie remembers being held — the skin held the seal, the water held the skin, the current wove everything together. The first wire the player weaves mirrors the kelp forests where the Selkie slept — woven by the current, held by the sea, a net that was never a trap.

The Selkie's final line, when the player completes all three acts and holds the finished pendant: "I gave my skin to the water. The water gave me a stone. I gave the stone to you. You gave it a frame. Now it can be worn by anyone. The skin that was mine is yours. That's how craft works. You give what you were so someone else can be."

### The Selkie in the Inn

- The Selkie lives in the Tidal Caves (water zone, Royo art style)
- The workshop is half-submerged — the grinding wheel is tide-powered, the polishing beach is at the waterline
- The Selkie appears as human during the crafting games (wet hands, salt-flecked hair, moonstone pendant)
- At certain times (full moon, high tide), the Selkie is absent — the workshop is empty, the grinding wheel still turning with the tide. The player can still craft, but the Selkie is in the water. Being a seal. Being what they were before they chose the stone.
- The Selkie's voice is the Inn's quietest craft voice — not the tabletop questline narrator, not the dramatic NPCs. The Selkie speaks like the ocean: slow, patient, repetitive, wearing down the stone of the player's ignorance through persistence, not force.

### The Selkie and the 42Hz

The Selkie's workshop hums at 42Hz — but it's the OCEAN's 42Hz, not the Inn's. The ocean's frequency is the same frequency as the Inn's, because the Inn is a tree and the tree's roots reach the Hollow Earth and the Hollow Earth's underground ocean IS the same ocean. The Selkie knows this. The Selkie has always known this. The ocean was humming 42Hz before the Inn was built. The Inn didn't create the Song. The Inn SINGS the Song. The ocean TAUGHT it.

## GAMEPLAY SPEC — ALL THREE GAMES

### Shared Interface
- **Perspective:** First-person, hands-on. The player sees their hands working the stone/wire.
- **Controls:** Mouse/touch for drag and click. Keyboard for tool selection. Controller for Steam (analog stick for grinding pressure, trigger for tool activation).
- **Rhythm:** 50 BPM (the Inn's heartbeat tempo). Each cut, each grind, each polish stroke is on the beat. The crafting is rhythmic — the player who finds the beat works faster and with less risk.
- **Material:** Each stone has real physical properties from the database (hardness = cutting speed, opacity = visual rendering, opticalPhenomenon = dome requirement)

### Cabochon Cutting Game
- **Tools:** Saw, preform wheel, doming wheel, polishing progression (coarse → medium → fine → polish)
- **Steps:** Read rough → saw → preform → dome → polish → reveal
- **Risk:** Too much heat = crack. Too much pressure = crack. Wrong orientation = phenomenon doesn't show.
- **Output:** Cabochon (round, oval, or organic) in inventory — tagged with stone ID, shape, dome height, quality

### Bead Forming Game
- **Tools:** Splitting saw, grinding wheel, faceting head (if faceted), drill, tumbler
- **Steps:** Split → shape → facet (if applicable) → drill → tumble/polish → string
- **Risk:** Same as cabochon, plus drilling blowout, plus faceting angle errors
- **Output:** Bead(s) in inventory — tagged with stone ID, shape, size, hole orientation, quality

### Wirework Game (from previous design doc)
- **Tools:** Wire spool, leather cord, pliers (for bending), guide lines on leather
- **Modes:** Drag (experience) and Click-to-Guide (prototype)
- **Steps:** Place waypoints/drag path → set over/under at crossings → tighten → export
- **Risk:** Wrong interlacing = pattern doesn't lock. Wire too thick for tight curves = pattern won't close.
- **Output:** Wirework piece (pendant setting, bracelet, bookmark, wall panel) — tagged with pattern, materials, dimensions

### The Combined Output
When all three games are completed in sequence, the output is a COMPLETE JEWELRY PIECE:
- Stone: cabochon cut by the player
- Cord: beads formed by the player
- Setting: wirework woven by the player
- The piece is a pendant — cabochon in wire setting on bead cord
- The piece is tagged with all three craft stages' quality metrics
- The piece can be worn, listed, gifted, or exported to Soulstice's workshop
