# Geode Inn — The Keepers of Every World
## Regional Wise Ancients | The Keeper Archetype Across Cultures

## Overview

The Geode Inn's Keeper (Aughra, Smith/Dench) is the master archetype — the Inn's own ancient consciousness, tending the Song at the roots of the Great Tree. But she is not alone. Every cultural region in the Inn's open world has its own version: a wise ancient figure drawn from that culture's real mythology and folklore, serving the same narrative function — keeper of knowledge, tender of the world, one who remembers — expressed through that culture's authentic traditions.

These are not copies of the Keeper. They are her *siblings*. Each one remembers a piece of the Song of Making, filtered through their culture's lens. The Inn's Keeper remembers the whole Song. The regional Keepers each remember a verse.

The player who visits every region and finds every Keeper — if they listen, if they're patient, if they don't demand — can collect every verse. Assembled, the verses are the Song. The Inn's Keeper has been waiting for someone to do this. She could sing it herself. She won't. It has to be learned, not given.

## Design Principles

1. **Authenticity.** Each regional Keeper is drawn from that culture's actual mythology, folklore, or living tradition. No invented archetypes. If a culture has a wise-old-woman-who-tends-knowledge figure, we use it. If the figure is male, or animal, or spirit, we use that — the archetype is "the one who remembers," not specifically "old woman."

2. **Respect.** These are living traditions for real people. The Geode Inn presents them on their own terms, not through colonial or racialized lenses (per standing instruction). Each Keeper is written in consultation with the culture's own stories, not external interpretations.

3. **Consistency of function, variety of form.** Every Keeper serves the same narrative role — the one who remembers, the one who tests with patience, the one who tends rather than rules. But the *expression* varies wildly: a mountain herb woman, a goddess on a mountain, a witch in a walking house, a spider grandmother, a sea woman at the ocean floor.

4. **The verse.** Each Keeper carries one verse of the Song of Making — their culture's piece of the universal Song. The verse is not in English. It's in the Keeper's language, sung in their tradition's style. The player collects verses; the verses are the Song.

5. **No quest markers.** The Keepers are found the way the Inn's Keeper is found — by being patient, by asking the right questions, by sitting without demanding. A quest arrow pointing to a wise ancient defeats the purpose. The player hears rumors, follows cultural clues, and eventually finds the Keeper in a place that feels inevitable.

## The Regional Keepers (Beginning Catalog)

### Appalachia — The Granny Woman

**Source:** Appalachian folk tradition. The Granny Woman is the mountain healer, herb doctor, midwife, and keeper of old wisdom in Appalachian culture — descended from Scots-Irish and Cherokee traditions that merged in the mountains. She lives deep in the holler, knows every plant, delivers every baby, and can tell you what the weather will do three days before it does it. She is not a witch (though some called her that). She is the last person who remembers how things were done before the store-bought world.

**In the Inn:** The Appalachian region's Keeper lives in a cabin at the dead end of a mountain holler — the kind of place you only find if you're lost. She sits in a rocking chair on a porch overlooking a creek. She has a jar of moonshine on the porch rail (she doesn't drink it — she keeps it for visitors, same as the Inn Keeper's teapot). Her cabin is surrounded by herbs — every one of them real, every one of them useful, every one of them labeled in a handwriting so old it looks like another script.

**The Smith-Dench energy:** The Granny Woman has the Smith razor — the Appalachian version, which is dryer and quieter than the British version. She doesn't deliver one-liners; she delivers *observations* that sit in the air like smoke. "You walked up the holler in those shoes? Your grandmother would be ashamed." And the Dench stillness — she rocks in her chair and doesn't look at you until she's ready, and when she does, you feel it.

**Her verse:** An old Appalachian ballad — the kind that's been passed down orally for centuries, the melody shifting with each singer. The verse she carries sounds like a mountain hymn: unaccompanied, nasal, ancient. The player who sits on her porch and listens long enough hears her humming it. If they ask, she teaches the verse. She won't write it down. "It's not a thing you write. It's a thing you carry."

**The test:** The Granny Woman tests with *work*. She'll ask you to help — gather herbs, split wood, carry water from the creek. Not because she needs help. Because working together is how you learn someone's character. If you do the work without complaint, without rushing, without asking what the point is — she talks. If you rush, she sends you away with a jar of moonshine and a "come back when you've got time to be here."

### China — Nüwa (女娲)

**Source:** Chinese mythology. Nüwa is the creator goddess who fashioned humans from yellow clay and repaired the sky with five-colored stones when the pillar of heaven was broken. She is one of the oldest figures in Chinese mythology — older than the written tradition. She is the mender, the maker, the one who fixes what the gods break.

**In the Inn:** The Chinese region's Keeper is not an old woman on a porch. Nüwa is a goddess — serene, ancient, present in a way that makes the air feel heavier. She appears in the player's path, not at a destination — sitting by a river, mending something. A pot. A stone. The sky. She is always mending. Her hands are always working. The five-colored stones she used to repair heaven are arranged in a circle around her, humming — each at a different frequency. Five stones, five frequencies, five colors. The player who recognizes the frequencies realizes: these are five of the gargoyle frequencies. The Song's harmonics, expressed in Chinese myth.

**The Smith-Dench energy:** Nüwa is almost entirely Dench — the stillness, the authority, the unhurried certainty. She doesn't make jokes. She doesn't need to. She has been mending the world since before language existed. Her presence is the joke — the absurdity that the sky once broke and one woman fixed it with stones from a riverbed. She is Smith in her precision — she looks at you, assesses you, and decides if you're worth talking to in about three seconds. If not, she returns to mending. If yes, she sets down what she's holding and gives you her full attention, which is overwhelming.

**Her verse:** A melody played on the *guqin* (ancient seven-stringed zither). The guqin has been associated with wisdom and contemplation for over 3,000 years. The verse she carries is not sung — it's played, slowly, on a guqin that appears to be made of the same five-colored stones. The player hears it if they sit by the river long enough. If they ask, she teaches the melody — not the notes, but the *feeling* of the notes. "The stone doesn't sing because you play it. You play it because the stone already sings. You're just... agreeing."

**The test:** Nüwa tests with *silence*. She doesn't ask you to do anything. She asks you to *not* do anything — not speak, not move, not try to help. Just sit. Be present. The player who can sit by the river in silence for two in-game minutes without opening a menu, without checking inventory, without doing anything at all — has passed. Nüwa says: "You can be still. That's rarer than you think."

### Norse — The Norns (Urd, Verdandi, Skuld)

**Source:** Norse mythology. The three Norns who tend Yggdrasil (the World Tree) at the Well of Urd. They water the tree's roots, weave the fate of gods and men, and know everything that was, is, and will be. Urd (What Was), Verdandi (What Is), Skuld (What Shall Be). They are the Keepers of the Tree — which makes them the most structurally perfect mirror of the Geode Inn's own Keeper, who tends the Great Tree's roots in the Geode Cave.

**In the Inn:** The Norse region's Keepers are three, not one. They sit at the base of the World Tree's northern root — the root that runs through the Norse zone. They weave. The thread they use glows faintly — amber, like the Inn's lighting. The pattern they weave is the Song of Making, expressed as fate. They don't sing the verse; they *weave* it.

**The Smith-Dench energy distributed across three:** Urd (the eldest) is Dench — the stillness, the memory, the authority of everything that has already happened. Verdandi (the middle) is Smith — the sharp wit of the present moment, the cutting observation, the "you're doing it wrong and I can see it right now." Skuld (the youngest) is neither — she's something else. She doesn't speak. She watches. The player who asks Skuld a question gets no answer — just a look that says "you'll find out." She is the future. The future doesn't answer questions.

**Their verse:** The Norns don't give a verse — they give a *thread*. A single strand of the woven fate, glowing amber. The player who receives it can hold it up to the light and see the Song's pattern in the weave. It's not a melody — it's a shape. The Norse expression of the Song: not sung, but woven. "The Song is not always heard. Sometimes it is seen. Sometimes it is touched. Sometimes it is lived."

**The test:** The Norns test with *truth*. They ask one question: "Why are you here?" There is no wrong answer — but the answer determines what they give you. If you say "to learn," Urd gives you the thread. If you say "to find something," Verdandi gives you a riddle instead. If you say "I don't know," Skuld looks at you for the first time — and gives you both.

### Celtic (Irish/Scottish) — The Cailleach

**Source:** Celtic mythology. The Cailleach (the Veiled One) is the winter queen, the hag of the storm, the shaper of mountains. She is ancient beyond counting — some traditions say she is the oldest being in the world, older than the gods. She carries a staff that freezes the ground. She is fearsome, but she is not evil. She is *necessary*. Winter is her gift. Without the freeze, nothing rests. Without rest, nothing grows.

**In the Inn:** The Celtic region's Keeper appears on a mountain pass during a snowstorm — the kind of storm that the player cannot walk through until they stop trying. She stands in the storm, unaffected, leaning on her staff. The storm is hers. If the player fights the wind, they go nowhere. If the player stops walking and waits, the wind drops — just around them, just enough. The Cailleach watches.

**The Smith-Dench energy:** The Cailleach is Smith at full power — the version that isn't funny, that isn't warm, that is pure assessment. She has been winter for so long that she has forgotten how to be anything else. Her Dench quality is in her *patience* — she has watched mountains form and rivers freeze. She does not rush. She does not soften. But if the player passes the test, she shows the Dench underneath: the veiled face, the eyes that have seen everything, the voice that says "you survived. Good. Not everyone does."

**Her verse:** The Cailleach's verse is not sung or played — it is *chanted*. A low, rhythmic Gaelic chant, the kind that sounds like the wind if the wind had words. The player hears it in the storm — not from the Cailleach, but *from the storm itself*. The storm is the verse. The Cailleach shaped the storm from the Song. The player who listens to the wind hears the verse. If they can hum it back to her, she nods. One nod. Dench.

**The test:** The Cailleach tests with *endurance*. The player must stand in the storm. Not fight it, not flee it, not cast a spell against it. Stand in it. The storm gets worse. The wind pushes. The cold bites. The player's health doesn't decrease — this isn't a survival mechanic — but the visual and audio intensity escalates until it's almost unbearable. Then it stops. Completely. Silence. The Cailleach says: "The storm isn't meant to hurt you. It's meant to show you what you can stand. Now you know."

### Egypt — Seshat

**Source:** Egyptian mythology. Seshat is the goddess of writing, wisdom, knowledge, accounting, and architecture. She is the "She who scrivens" — the divine record-keeper who inscribed the king's reign on the leaves of the sacred persea tree. She wears a leopard skin and a headdress with a seven-pointed star. She is the keeper of the *written record* — the one who ensures that what happened is never forgotten.

**In the Inn:** The Egyptian region's Keeper sits in a chamber covered in hieroglyphics — walls, floor, ceiling, every surface inscribed. She writes. Constantly. Her pen never stops. She is recording everything that happens in the zone — every player who enters, every stone collected, every NPC interaction. The walls grow. New hieroglyphics appear in real time as the player explores. The player who watches closely sees their own name appear. She is writing them into history.

**The Smith-Dench energy:** Seshat is Dench — the patient recorder, the one who has been writing for so long that writing is breathing. She doesn't look up. She doesn't need to. She knows you're there. Her Smith quality is in her *corrections* — if the player mispronounces an Egyptian name or misunderstands a hieroglyphic, Seshat corrects them without looking up, without stopping writing, in a voice so calm it feels like the correction was always there, waiting.

**Her verse:** Seshat's verse is *written*, not sung. She inscribes it on a scroll and hands it to the player. The hieroglyphics on the scroll, when read aloud in ancient Egyptian, produce the verse — a chant that sounds like the Inn's 42 Hz hum, but higher, sharper, like a crystal singing. The written form of the Song: not felt (the hum), not heard (the melody), but *read*. The Song exists in every medium.

**The test:** Seshat tests with *knowledge*. She asks the player to read a hieroglyphic — one that appears nowhere else in the Inn, in a script the player has never seen. If the player tries to guess, Seshat waits. If the player says "I don't know," Seshat nods and teaches them — one symbol, one meaning, one sound. The test isn't knowing the answer. The test is admitting you don't.

### Andean (Peru) — Pachamama

**Source:** Andean indigenous tradition. Pachamama is the earth mother — the living ground beneath everything. She is not a figure on a throne; she is the *land itself*. She doesn't sit above the world; she *is* the world. To honor Pachamama is to pour a little of your drink on the ground before drinking — a gift to the earth that gives you everything.

**In the Inn:** The Andean region's Keeper is not a person you visit. Pachamama is *everywhere* in the Andean zone. The ground vibrates. The mountains hum. The player feels her presence as a constant warmth from below — the same warmth as the Geode Cave, but under the open sky. She speaks through the land: a rumble, a shift in the wind, a flower blooming where the player steps.

**The Smith-Dench energy:** Pachamama is Dench without the Smith — no wit, no cutting remarks. Just presence. The authority of something so vast it doesn't need to speak. When she does speak — through a vibration, through a feeling, through a knowing that arrives without words — it is the Dench voice: "You are standing on me. You have always been standing on me. That's not a problem. That's the point."

**Her verse:** Pachamama's verse is felt, not heard. The player receives it by *standing still* on the earth in the Andean zone and feeling the vibration. The 42 Hz hum rises from the ground, through the player's feet, through their bones. The verse is the same hum as the Geode Cave — the same frequency, the same Song — but experienced as *gravity*. The earth is singing. You are standing in its mouth.

**The test:** Pachamama tests with *offering*. The player must pour something on the ground — a drop of water, a crumb of food, a stone from their inventory. Not because Pachamama needs it, but because the act of giving back is the test. The player who takes without giving will never hear the earth sing. The player who gives hears the verse in their bones.

### Slavic — Baba Yaga

**Source:** Slavic folklore. Baba Yaga is the witch of the deep forest who lives in a hut that stands on chicken legs. She is fearsome, ambiguous, ancient, and powerful. She eats children (in some stories). She helps heroes (in others). She is the test — the one you must approach correctly or not at all. She flies in a mortar, steers with a pestle, sweeps her tracks with a broom. She is the forest's wisdom and the forest's danger, inseparable.

**In the Inn:** The Slavic region's Keeper lives in a hut that the player finds in the deepest part of the forest — but only if they're not looking for it. The hut is standing on chicken legs. It is rotating slowly. The player must say the rhyme (the real Slavic folk rhyme) to make it stop and turn its door toward them: "Hut, hut, stand as you were placed, with your back to the woods and your door to my face." If they say it, the hut stops. If they don't, it keeps turning and the door never faces them.

**The Smith-Dench energy:** Baba Yaga is Smith at maximum volume — the version that's genuinely scary, not just sharp. She is the Keeper who might *not* be kind. She tests harder than any other Keeper, and her wit has teeth. "Why are you here? Nobody comes to the forest to be helped. They come to the forest because they're lost. Which are you?" But underneath — deep underneath, past the teeth and the hut and the bones — the Dench warmth. She has been the forest's guardian for so long that she has *become* the forest. She is terrifying because the forest is terrifying. She is kind because the forest is kind. Both are true.

**Her verse:** Baba Yaga's verse is a *whisper*. She leans close — close enough that the player can see every line in her ancient face — and whispers a single word in Old Slavic. The word means "live." That's the verse. One word. The player carries it. When they assemble the full Song, Baba Yaga's verse is the one that tells the Song to be *alive* — not just sung, not just carried, but *lived*. The Song isn't a melody. It's a life.

**The test:** Baba Yaga tests with *fear*. She gives the player a task that is genuinely frightening — not combat, not a puzzle, but a choice. She puts the player in a position where the right thing to do is also the terrifying thing. The test is whether the player does it anyway. If they do, Baba Yaga respects them. She doesn't say so. She gives them the verse and turns her hut away. The door faces the woods. The audience is over.

### Japan — Sarutahiko Ōkami

**Source:** Japanese mythology / Shinto tradition. Sarutahiko is the earthly *kami* (spirit/god) who stood at the crossroads between heaven and earth to guide the goddess Amaterasu's descent. He is associated with the earth, with crossroads, with guidance, and with the physical body's strength. In some traditions, he is the guardian of the boundary between worlds — the one who stands at the threshold and decides who passes.

**Note:** Sarutahiko is male, not female — and that's important. The Keeper archetype is "the one who remembers and tends," not "old woman." The Geode Inn respects each culture's version. In Japan, the threshold-keeper is this figure. We use the authentic version.

**In the Inn:** The Japanese region's Keeper stands at a torii gate at the edge of the zone — the boundary between the player's world and the sacred space. He does not sit. He does not rock in a chair. He stands. He has been standing since before the zone was built. His feet are buried in the earth — roots have grown around them. He is part of the land now. He holds a staff. He does not move except his eyes, which track the player.

**The energy:** Not Smith or Dench — *different*. Sarutahiko has the energy of a *gate*. He is not warm. He is not sharp. He is *certain*. He has been at this threshold for so long that he IS the threshold. His voice, when he speaks, is like stone: low, steady, final. "You may pass. Or you may not. The gate does not decide for you. The gate is the question. You are the answer."

**His verse:** Sarutahiko's verse is *silence*. He gives the player nothing — no sound, no word, no melody. But when the player stands at the torii gate and listens to the silence, the silence has a *texture*. It vibrates. At 42 Hz. The verse is the space between sounds — the rest in the music, the pause in the song. The Song of Making, expressed as the Japanese concept of *ma* (間) — the meaningful silence between things. The player carries the silence. When assembled with the other verses, Sarutahiko's silence is the space between notes — the thing that makes the Song *music* instead of noise.

**The test:** Sarutahiko tests with *presence*. The player must stand at the gate and do nothing. Not meditate (no game mechanic). Not pray. Just stand. For three in-game minutes. The gate does not open. The gate does not close. The player must decide to walk through on their own. The test is whether the player can make a choice without being told what to choose. If they walk through, Sarutahiko nods — once — and the silence follows them.

### Indigenous Australian — The Spider Grandmother

**Source:** Multiple Aboriginal Australian traditions. The Spider Grandmother is a creator figure in several Aboriginal cultures — she wove the world into existence, she carries the stories, she connects all things through her web. She is the original weaver, the keeper of the songlines. In Aboriginal tradition, the land itself is sung into existence through songlines (Tjukurpa) — ancient paths that are both physical routes and songs. The Spider Grandmother is the keeper of the songlines.

**In the Inn:** The Australian region's Keeper is a spider — not a giant monster spider, but an old, patient spider who sits at the center of a web that spans the entire zone. The web catches light. Each strand hums. The web is the Songline — a map of the land expressed as music, and a map of the music expressed as land. The player who follows the web's strands is walking the songline, and the songline IS the Song of Making, expressed in the oldest continuous oral tradition on Earth.

**The energy:** The Spider Grandmother is Dench — the patience of something that has been weaving since before time was measured. She doesn't speak often. When she does, each word is a thread — placed, not thrown. Her Smith quality is in her *assessment* — she watches the player walk her web and knows, from the way they move, whether they're walking it or just crossing it. There's a difference. She can tell.

**Her verse:** The verse is the *songline itself*. The player walks the web. Each strand produces a tone. The sequence of tones — walked in order, walked with intention, walked as a path, not a shortcut — is the verse. It's not sung to the player; it's *walked* by the player. The Australian expression of the Song: the music IS the land. The land IS the music. Walking is singing.

**The test:** The Spider Grandmother tests with *respect*. The player must walk the songline in order — not skip strands, not cut corners, not rush. The web is delicate. The songline is ancient. If the player rushes, the strands snap (not literally — the tones stop). If the player walks with care, each strand holds, each tone sounds, and the verse is complete. The Spider Grandmother says: "You walked it. Now it's in your feet. It will be there when you need it. You won't need to remember it. Your feet will."

## The Song Assembled

When the player has collected every verse from every Keeper — every cultural expression of the Song — the verses can be assembled. Not by the player. By the Inn's Keeper. The player brings the verses to the Geode Cave. The Keeper listens to each one. She nods after each. (Dench nod — minimal, complete.) When all are collected:

The Keeper stands. For the first time. She has been sitting for millennia. She stands. She takes her crystal eye out. She holds it up. She says: "You learned it. Every language. Every tradition. Every way it's sung — in words, in silence, in thread, in stone, in the earth itself. You learned it all."

She places the eye back in her socket. She sits. She rocks.

"Now sing it."

And the player sings the Song. In the game, this is the final, optional, hidden endgame — not a boss, not a cutscene. The player sings the Song of Making. Every verse, in order, in its original form. The Inn resonates. Every stone vibrates. Every gargoyle activates. The Singing Tree's leaves chime. The brownies stop moving. Grit, nested in the Keeper's hair, glows. The Owl, perched on the chair's arm, closes its eyes.

The Geode Inn *becomes* the Song. For ten seconds, the player sees what the Keeper's crystal eye sees: the building as music, every stone a note, every being a voice, every culture a verse, all of it one Song, carried by everyone, owned by no one.

Then it's over. The Inn is the Inn again. The Keeper rocks. The teapot steams. The brownies resume. Grit gets stuck in the teapot.

The Keeper says: "Good. Now do it again tomorrow."

The Song doesn't end. It's carried. That's the point.
