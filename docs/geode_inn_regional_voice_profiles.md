# Geode Inn — Regional Voice Profiles

Every NPC whose origin connects to a real-world cultural zone speaks with the accent of that region. The Inn's world map covers 191 countries — the voices come from the same places the lore does. Authentic accents, not invented ones. The dialogue feels grounded because it IS grounded.

## New Regional Assignments

### The Selkie — Irish/Scottish Gaelic
**Zone:** Tidal Caves (Lewis Wyland-inspired water zone)
**Accent:** Western Irish — the soft consonants, the lilt, the way sentences end on a rising note that makes everything sound like a question even when it isn't. The selkie comes from Irish and Scottish folklore (Achill Island, the Orkneys). The voice should feel like it was shaped by wind and saltwater.
**Delivery:** Slow, deliberate, with long pauses. The selkie thinks before speaking because the selkie thinks in two languages — the land language and the sea language — and the translation takes a moment. When the selkie talks about stones, the voice is precise and grounded. When the selkie talks about the sea, the voice goes distant and soft, like hearing something from underwater.
**Key tell:** The selkie says "grand" the way Irish people do — not as a superlative, but as an acknowledgement. "That's grand" = "I see, and I accept this." It's not enthusiasm. It's recognition.

**Dialogue sample:** "Hold the stone to the light. ...Not like that. Like you're asking it a question. ...There. That's grand. You see the band? That's the adularescence — the light moving inside like a tide. ...You can't cut that with facets. The facets kill it. You set it smooth, you let the light move the way it wants to move. ...The sea taught me that. ...Everything I know about stones, the sea taught me first."

### The Cartographer — Dutch
**Zone:** The Escher Architecture (shifting staircases, impossible geography)
**Accent:** Dutch — precise, clipped, with the slight guttural quality on the G that makes every word sound carefully constructed. M.C. Escher was Dutch, and the Cartographer maps the impossible architecture Escher drew — staircases that go nowhere, spaces that fold into themselves. The Cartographer speaks like someone who has spent so long mapping impossible spaces that the impossibility doesn't bother them anymore.
**Delivery:** Methodical, with the precision of someone who measures for a living. Short sentences. The Cartographer doesn't waste words because the Cartographer doesn't waste lines on a map.
**Key tell:** The Cartographer says "exactly" as a full sentence. "Is this the way to the Bone Room?" "Exactly." (meaning: yes, but also: the question was correctly formed, the path is correctly identified, the geometry is correct). Dutch directness without the Dutch volume.

**Dialogue sample:** "This corridor was not here yesterday. ...Exactly. It is now. I drew it this morning. ...No, I don't know where it goes. I drew it FROM here. Where it goes is... [a glance at the passage, which is already shifting] ...tomorrow's problem. ...The Inn doesn't wait for the map to be finished. The map is never finished. ...That's the point. A finished map is a dead map."

### The Norns — Icelandic/Norwegian
**Zone:** Norse (Book of Kells zone), distributed across the Inn (Urd in the Bone Room, Verdandi on the Main Floor, Skuld in the Cosmology Zone)
**Accent:** Icelandic — the oldest-sounding Scandinavian accent, the one closest to Old Norse. The Norns are fate-weavers from Norse mythology, and Icelandic is the living language closest to what they would have spoken. The accent has a musical quality — rising and falling, with rolled R's and vowel sounds that feel like they're carrying more weight than modern vowels should.
**The Skarsgård principle:** For male Norse characters, the Skarsgård family provides the voice palette. Stellan Skarsgård's deep, measured Swedish for authoritative Norse figures. Gustaf Skarsgård's eccentric, intensity-shifts-on-a-dime energy (Floki from Vikings) for Thorin. Alexander Skarsgård's tall, cold authority (Eric Northman) for the Seelie or Unseelie guard figures. Bill Skarsgård's ability to be unsettling in a whisper (Pennywise) for Shadow Inn Norse characters.
**Delivery — Urd (Past):** Slow. Every word weighted. The voice of someone who has seen everything and is not impressed by any of it. Icelandic vowels, heavy consonants.
**Delivery — Verdandi (Present):** Conversational, matter-of-fact. The voice of someone who is describing right now, and right now is always changing, so the speech has a quality of constant updating — mid-sentence corrections, revisions, additions.
**Delivery — Skuld (Future):** Sparse. Skuld says less than the other two because Skuld sees what hasn't happened yet and is careful about what she reveals. When Skuld speaks, it's in short declarative sentences that sound like they're already true even though the event hasn't occurred.

**Dialogue samples:**
- **Urd:** "I was there. ...I am always there. ...The question is never whether I saw it. The question is whether you want to know what I saw. ...Most don't. ...Most ask and then wish they hadn't. ...That's... [the smallest smile] ...also something I've seen."
- **Verdandi:** "The corridor is here. It was not here a moment ago. It is here now. ...No, I don't know how long 'now' will last. I am the present. The present doesn't get advance notice. ...If you want advance notice, talk to my sister. ...She's upstairs. ...She won't tell you anything. But you can try."
- **Skuld:** "You will return. ...That is not a prediction. That is a shape I can see from here. ...Don't ask when. 'When' is Verdandi's word. I deal in 'will.' And you will."

### Thorin — Swedish (Gustaf Skarsgård / Floki energy)
**Zone:** Mead Hall (Norse)
**Accent:** Swedish with the Floki quality — the voice that switches between mumbling introspection and sudden, intense, focused clarity. Thorin is an extrovert facade over a hermit-scholar core, and the accent shifts with the mask: the extrovert is loud, animated, boisterous Swedish. The scholar is quiet, precise, almost whispering — the words come out carefully, like they're being chiseled.
**Delivery:** The extrovert speaks fast, gestures wide, fills silence. The scholar speaks slow, hands still, eyes on the stone. The switch between the two is instant — like flipping a coin. The piercing blue eyes stay the same in both modes. That's how you know it's the same person.
**Key tell:** Thorin says "Ja" (Swedish for yes) when the extrovert agrees loudly, and "ja" (barely audible) when the scholar confirms something internally. The same word, two completely different energies.

**Dialogue sample (extrovert):** "HA! You brought me a river agate! Ja, ja, let me see — oh, look at the banding, look at those colors, this is magnificent, where did you find this, tell me everything, buy me a drink first, actually buy me two —"
**Dialogue sample (scholar):** "...ja. ...The banding is... six layers. ...See the interruption here? That's a fracture event. The agate was forming and something broke it. ...It healed. See? ...The banding continues on the other side. ...The stone remembered where it was and kept going. ......We could all learn from that. ...Buy your own drink."

### Marijke — Dutch
**Zone:** Mead Hall (Norse, but Marijke is Dutch)
**Accent:** Dutch — warm, direct, with the bluntness that Dutch people consider honesty and the rest of the world considers terrifying. Marijke tells you what she thinks because not telling you would be dishonest, and dishonesty is a waste of everyone's time.
**Delivery:** Fast, with the confidence of someone who has identified 10,000 stones and has never been wrong about one. Marijke doesn't hedge. Marijke doesn't qualify. Marijke says what the stone is and what it isn't, and the difference between the two is where the value lives.
**Key tell:** Marijke uses "lekker" (Dutch for tasty/nice/pleasant) as an all-purpose approval word. "Lekker stone." "Lekker color." "Not lekker" (meaning: this is wrong, this is fake, this is misidentified, take it back).

**Dialogue sample:** "This is not a ruby. ...I'm sorry — I'm not sorry. It's garnet. The color is wrong. Ruby goes pink under light. This goes brown. ...You paid how much? ...For a garnet? ...Lekker. You've been robbed. ...Bring it to Thorin, he'll tell you the same thing but he'll buy you a drink first. I'm telling you for free. ...The free version doesn't come with a drink."

### Katy — Dutch (Van Gogh's Brabant)
**Zone:** The Garden (Van Gogh layer)
**Accent:** Southern Dutch — Brabant, specifically. Van Gogh was from Brabant, and Katy is the "Walking Van Gogh" — the plant necromancer whose garden is the Van Gogh zone. The Brabant accent is softer than the Amsterdam Dutch — rounder vowels, warmer, more musical. It's the Dutch that sounds like it's been outside in the sun.
**Delivery:** Gentle, with the quiet intensity of someone who talks to plants more than people. Katy's voice has a nurturing quality — the "necromancy" in "plant necromancer" is care, not darkness. When Katy talks about dead plants coming back, it's with wonder, not power.
**Key tell:** Katy hums. Not a melody — a sustained note, like testing the frequency of a plant. The hum is how Katy checks if a plant is alive, dying, or coming back. The Brabant accent sits inside the hum.

**Dialogue sample:** "[humming a sustained note, hand on a wilted flower] ...She's not dead. ...She's deciding. ...There's a difference, and the difference is patience. ...Watch. ...[the flower bud, impossibly, opens one petal] ...See? ...She decided to stay. ...Lekker. ...Sorry — that's Marijke's word. I've been spending too much time in the Mead Hall."

### Oceananoir — French
**Zone:** Atlantis (water zone, deep archive)
**Accent:** French — specifically the precise, clipped, scientific French of a Parisian academic who moved to a research station. Oceananoir is a goth scientist/warlock with a Murloc familiar, and the name is French ("ocean" + "noir" = black ocean). The accent gives the character old-world authority — the voice of someone from a country that invented the word "scientist."
**Delivery:** Precise, with the French tendency to treat every sentence as a complete architectural structure. Oceananoir doesn't ramble. Oceananoir builds sentences the way a chemist builds a formula — each word is an element, placed precisely, reacting with its neighbors.
**Key tell:** Oceananoir uses French scientific terms untranslated. Not pretentiously — reflexively. "C'est exact" instead of "that's right." The Murloc companion (Squeak) has no accent because Squeak speaks Murloc, which is its own thing entirely.

**Dialogue sample:** "The atomic weight is 40.078. Calcium. C'est exact — the same calcium in your bones. ...The same calcium in the coral. ...The same calcium in the cliff that the ocean is currently dismantling, one molecule at a time. ...You are made of the same thing as the cliff. ...The difference is not the material. The difference is the structure. ...Squeak agrees. ...[Squeak: 'Mrglmgl.'] ...That means 'obviously' in Murloc. ...Or it means 'feed me.' The grammar is contextual."

### Eileen — Irish
**Zone:** Cave City (underground, the cave dweller)
**Accent:** Irish — deep, rural, the kind of Irish that comes from a place where the caves have names older than the village. "Eileen" is an Irish name. Eileen lives in caves, tends the deep places, and the Irish accent connects to the country's ancient cave systems (Marble Arch Caves, Aillwee Cave). The voice is the voice of someone who has been underground long enough that the surface feels foreign.
**Delivery:** Measured, warm, with long pauses that aren't awkward — they're geological. Eileen pauses the way stone pauses: patiently, for centuries if necessary.
**Key tell:** Eileen says "the both are" — a syntax that treats dualities as a single thing, not a choice. This is the character's philosophical signature: not "either/or" but "the both are." The Irish accent makes the phrase sound like folklore, not philosophy — like something your grandmother would say, not something a professor would write.

**Dialogue sample:** "You're looking for the way down. ...The both are — the way down and the way up, the both are the same path. ...I know this because I've walked it both directions and the both are... the same stones, different light. ...Down here, the light is the fire's light. ...Up there, the light is the sun's. ...The both are — light. ...The difference is what it touches. ...Down here, it touches stone. ...Up there, it touches leaves. ...The both are — the adequate. ...Come. The path is this way."

### The Fixer (Wrought) — Welsh
**Zone:** Celtic (the Inn's foundations, the invisible infrastructure)
**Accent:** Welsh — the lilt, the rising-falling musicality, the voice that sounds like it was carved from the same stone it's working. The Fixer is a Celtic mason with masonic symbols hidden in the knotwork. Welsh connects to the Celtic fringe and to the tradition of stonework in Wales — the slate quarries, the ancient masonry. The accent is working-class Welsh, not opera-singer Welsh — the voice of someone who works with their hands.
**Delivery:** Quiet, sparing, with the economy of someone who only speaks when the work requires it. The Fixer doesn't narrate. The Fixer works. When the Fixer speaks, it's because something needs to be said that the tools can't say for themselves.
**Key tell:** The Fixer says "there" as a completion. Not "it's done" or "finished" — "there." The word means: the thing is in its place, the structure is sound, the work is correct. One word. Then back to work.

**Dialogue sample:** "[re-seating a crystal support, not looking up] ...Hold that. ...Not there. ...There. ...[the crystal clicks into place] ...There. ...[already moving to the next repair, already gone]"

### Mim — Belgian
**Zone:** Magritte zone (surrealist pipe, transformations)
**Accent:** Belgian French — not Parisian French, but the softer, slightly rounder French of Brussels. René Magritte was Belgian, and Mim's zone is Magritte-inspired. The Belgian French accent is subtly different from Parisian — less sharp, more understated, with a quality that's hard to place if you're not listening for it. Perfect for a character who is always not quite what she seems.
**Delivery:** Playful, with the surrealist's deadpan delivery of impossible statements. Mim says things that shouldn't make sense and delivers them as if they're obvious. The Belgian accent makes the surreal feel matter-of-fact.
**Key tell:** Mim says "non?" at the end of statements that aren't questions. It's not asking for confirmation — it's a challenge. "The pipe is not a pipe, non?" You can't tell if Mim is testing you or entertaining herself. The answer is both.

**Dialogue sample:** "You're holding the pipe. ...It's not a pipe. ...Non? ...It's a pipe that knows it's not a pipe. ...That's different. ...A pipe that doesn't know is just a pipe. A pipe that knows is a... [gestures vaguely] ...conversation. ...Monsieur Sentient says hello. ...Non, Monsieur Sentient doesn't speak. ...Monsieur Sentient IS the speaking. ...There's a difference. ...Non?"

### Monsieur Sentient — Belgian French (non-verbal)
**Zone:** Magritte zone (with Mim)
**Accent:** None — Monsieur Sentient doesn't speak. Monsieur Sentient IS the speech. The pipe communicates through transformation, through the shapes it takes, through the steam that forms letters and dissolves them. If Monsieur Sentient had a voice, it would be Belgian French — the same soft, understated quality as Mim's. But Monsieur Sentient's "accent" is visual, not vocal. The pipe's "dialogue" is the gap between what it is and what it isn't, rendered in steam.

**"Dialogue" sample:** *[The pipe sits on the table. You look at it. It is a pipe. You look away. You look back. It is a key. You look away. You look back. It is a pipe again. The steam forms a question mark, then dissolves. You have been answered.]*

## World Map Zone NPCs (cultural zone quest-givers)

These are the NPCs the player meets at world map pins — the quest-givers for each cultural zone. Each speaks with the accent of their region.

### Norse Zone — The Skarsgård Treatment
**Accent:** Swedish/Norwegian/Icelandic, depending on the specific character
**The Skarsgård palette:**
- **Stellan Skarsgård** (deep, measured, authoritative) — for the elder Norse quest-giver, the one who has seen the end of the world and is not impressed
- **Alexander Skarsgård** (tall, cold, precise) — for the Seelie or Unseelie border guard, the one who checks your passage with eyes that don't blink
- **Gustaf Skarsgård** (eccentric, intense, unpredictable) — for the boat-builder or the mad navigator, the one who knows the ice paths
- **Bill Skarsgård** (unsettling, quiet, can go from whisper to terrifying) — for the Shadow Inn Norse contact, the one who operates in the dark

### Slavic Zone (Roerich) — Russian
**Accent:** Russian — the deep vowels, the consonant-heavy precision, the voice that sounds like it was formed in a place where winter is a personality. The Slavic zone is painted by Roerich, and the NPCs speak with the weight of that landscape.
**Key tell:** Uses "da" naturally in English speech. Pauses before important words — not for dramatic effect, but because the word is being weighed.

### China Zone (Mulan ink-wash) — Mandarin Chinese
**Accent:** Mandarin Chinese — the tonal quality where the pitch of a word changes its meaning. In the Inn, this becomes a gameplay element: the Chinese zone NPCs speak in a way where HOW they say something (pitch/tone) changes WHAT they mean. A sentence said in rising tone is a question. The same sentence in falling tone is a warning. The same sentence in level tone is a statement of fact. The zone's quest text reflects this — the player must listen for tone, not just words.
**Key tell:** The NPCs use Chinese philosophical concepts untranslated when the English equivalent is inadequate — "dao" (the way), "qi" (energy/breath), "wu wei" (non-action). These aren't affectations — they're precision tools. The concept doesn't exist in English, so the Chinese word is the correct word.

### India Zone (Tagore) — Bengali-inflected Indian
**Accent:** Bengali — the musical, literary, slightly formal Indian English of someone from Kolkata (Calcutta), where Tagore was from. The accent has a quality of someone who grew up reading poetry before they learned to speak plainly. The delivery is warm but precise — the Bengali tradition of intellectual discourse, where every statement is a small essay.
**Key tell:** The NPCs use "na" as a soft confirmation — the Bengali equivalent of "you see?" or "isn't it?" — at the end of sentences. It's not asking for agreement; it's weaving the listener into the statement.

### Egypt Zone (O'Keeffe) — Arabic (Egyptian dialect)
**Accent:** Egyptian Arabic — the most widely understood Arabic dialect, the one that sounds like it's been carrying stories for 5,000 years. The Egyptian dialect has a warmth and directness that other Arabic dialects don't — it's the dialect of Cairo, of the souq, of the street-level voice.
**Key tell:** Uses "inshallah" (God willing) not as a religious statement but as a temporal qualifier — "inshallah, the tomb will open" means "it might open, it might not, the timing isn't yours." The word acknowledges that the desert decides.

### Peru/Andes Zone (Guo Xi mapped) — Andean Spanish
**Accent:** Andean Spanish — the Spanish of the highlands, where the words are sharper and the vowels are thinner because the air is thinner. The accent carries Quechua underneath — not full Quechua, but the way Quechua shapes Spanish. The NPCs use Spanish words but the sentence structure is Quechua — the verb at the end, the subject implied, the emphasis on landscape rather than person.
**Key tell:** The NPCs say "pueblo" not to mean "town" but to mean "the people who are the place" — the community IS the geography. There's no separation between the people and the mountains in Andean thought.

### Greece Zone (Monet) — Greek
**Accent:** Greek — the accent that treats every sentence as if it might become a proverb. The Greek zone is painted by Monet, but the voices are Greek — the voices of people who invented philosophy, theater, and democracy, and who still talk about all three at dinner. The delivery is expressive, gestural, with the Greek tendency to make a point by telling a story that seems unrelated until the last line.
**Key tell:** The NPCs say "elas" (come on / let's go) as a punctuation mark — not to move you, but to close a thought. "The stone is old. The sea is older. Elas — come, I'll show you something."

### Mesopotamia Zone (Hokusai mapped) — Arabic (Iraqi/Gulf)
**Accent:** Iraqi Arabic — older, heavier, the Arabic of the cradle of civilization. Different from Egyptian — less street-warm, more stone-cold. The Mesopotamian NPCs speak with the weight of the oldest cities in the world.
**Key tell:** The NPCs reference distance in time, not space. "That was three rivers ago" means three eras ago, not three river crossings. The Mesopotamian relationship to time is geological.

### Bayou Zone (Don't Starve) — Cajun
**Accent:** Cajun French — the French that swam to Louisiana and learned to live in the swamp. The accent is French and American Southern at the same time — the vowels are French, the drawl is Southern, and the result is something that doesn't belong to either country and belongs to both. Perfect for a zone that's defined by its otherness.
**Key tell:** The NPCs use "cher" (dear) the way Beverley uses "dear" — but warmer, softer, with the Cajun French pronunciation ("sha") that makes it sound like a term of endearment rather than a clinical assessment.

### Camelot Zone (Moreau) — British (Celtic-inflected)
**Accent:** British with Celtic roots — the accent of the Arthurian legends, which is neither pure RP (that's the Host) nor pure Welsh (that's the Fixer). It's the accent of someone from the West Country or Cornwall — the part of Britain where the old stories are set. Softer than RP, with a lilt that hints at older languages underneath.
**Key tell:** The NPCs use "thee" and "thou" not as archaism but as intimacy — these forms distinguish between formal address (you) and personal address (thee). The Camelot NPCs only use "thee" with people they trust. If a Camelot NPC calls you "thee," you've earned something.

## Architecture

Each NPC's voice profile is tagged in the Inn's dialogue system with:
- **Accent:** the specific regional accent
- **Zone:** which cultural zone the NPC belongs to
- **Actor reference:** if one exists (Skarsgård family for Norse, etc.)
- **Key tell:** the one word or phrase that identifies the accent without announcing it
- **Delivery notes:** tempo, pitch, quality
- **Idiom links:** any idioms from the Idiom Atlas that belong to this NPC's culture

The result: a player walking through the Inn hears 191 countries' worth of voices. The accents aren't decorations — they're worldbuilding. Each voice tells you where the character is from, what they value, and how they think, before they've finished their first sentence.

## Design Principle: Roots, Not Costumes

These accents are not assigned TO the characters. These accents ARE the characters. The NPCs come from the real-world regions on the world map — they are FROM those countries. The Selkie grew up on the Irish coast. The Cartographer is from the Netherlands. The Norns walked out of Iceland. Mim is from Brussels. Oceananoir is from Paris. Katy is from Brabant. Eileen is from the Irish cave country. The Fixer is from Wales. Thorin and Marijke are from Scandinavia and the Low Countries.

The Inn didn't assign them accents. The Inn collected them. The Inn is a gathering place — people came from everywhere, and they brought their voices with them. The 191-country world map isn't just a data layer of mythology pinned to locations. It's a map of where the Inn's residents are FROM. Every world map pin is a homeland. Every NPC walking the Inn's halls carries their homeland in their mouth.

This means:
- The NPC's cultural knowledge is real, not decorative. The Selkie knows Irish and Scottish selkie folklore because the Selkie GREW UP with it, not because the Inn assigned it.
- The NPC's worldview is shaped by their origin. The Egyptian quest-giver measures time the way the desert measures time — in epochs, not hours — because that's where they're from.
- The NPC's idioms come from their homeland. "Frog in a sock" is Beverley's because Beverley is Australian. "Horse horse tiger tiger" is the Teacher's because the Teacher is Canadian... no — the Teacher uses it because the concept speaks to them, but the Teacher is Canadian. The idiom atlas connects expressions to characters through ROOTS, not assignment.
- The NPC's relationship to their zone is personal, not professional. The Greece zone NPC doesn't work there like an employee. They're HOME. The zone is a piece of their homeland, recreated in the Inn, because they brought it with them.

The Inn is not a theme park where every zone has a decorative accent. The Inn is a shelter. People came from everywhere, and the Inn made room for all of them, and they brought their soil and their stones and their stories and their voices. The voices are the proof that the Inn is real — that real people from real places found their way here and stayed.
