# Geode Inn — Anubis
## Character Profile — The Guide of Souls, The Weigher of Hearts

## Origin Note

Krista confirmed Anubis needs proper NPC development. He exists in the character catalog as a chibi (arcade NPC), but the Weighing of the Heart — the Egyptian death sequence — requires a Full Presence manifestation. The chibi drops. The jackal head is real. The scales are real.

## The Dual Nature

Anubis is the Inn's first true *dual-form* character — and the dual form is not a transformation the way Odin's Wanderer/Allfather is a transformation. Anubis doesn't *change* between forms. Both forms exist simultaneously, and the form the player sees depends on *where they are and what they're doing.*

### Chibi Anubis (Arcade / Casual)

In the arcade games — Bastet's Herding, Tomb Tunnel, the Egyptian zone's casual encounters — Anubis is chibi. Small. Round-eared. Big eyes. The cute jackal puppy who guides players through tutorials, offers hints in the tomb puzzles, and occasionally appears as a playable character in the arcade roster. Chibi Anubis is *playful.* He tilts his head when he's curious. His ears flop. He has the energy of a very intelligent dog who is also very good at his job and knows it and is slightly smug about it in the way that only a very intelligent dog can be.

Chibi Anubis is the face the player knows. The face they trust. The face that makes them smile when he shows up in a game. He's the guide. He's the helper. He's the one who says "this way" and you follow because he's small and cute and his tail is wagging and you believe him.

This is deliberate. This is the setup.

### Full Presence Anubis (The Weighing)

When the player dies in the Egyptian zone — when the death sequence triggers and the world fades to dark blue and the words appear ("Your name is known, your rest is earned") — the player arrives at the Hall of Ma'at. And Anubis is there.

Not chibi. Not cute. Not round-eared and floppy-tailed. *Full Presence.*

The jackal head is real. The eyes are not the big, warm eyes of the chibi. They are the narrow, amber, *ancient* eyes of a being who has been weighing hearts for five thousand years. The body is tall — not towering, not monstrous, but *tall enough that you have to look up.* The black-furred skin is the black of fertile soil, of the Nile mud that grows things, of the earth that receives the dead. The gold collar around his neck is the only ornament — the wesekh, the wide collar of authority, ancient Egyptian, the mark of the one who stands between the living and the dead and decides who continues.

The scales are in his hands. The feather is in one pan. The other pan is empty. Waiting.

The player who knew chibi Anubis — the cute guide, the playful helper, the wagging tail — sees Full Presence Anubis and understands, in one moment, that the cute version was not a lie. The cute version was the *interface.* The friendly face that made you trust the being you were going to meet at the end. Anubis was always this. The chibi was how he made himself approachable. The Full Presence is how he actually is. And the actually-is is not frightening — it's *true.* The way the river is true. The way the feather is true. The way the weight of a heart is the most honest thing in the world.

## The Character

### Name: Anubis

Also: Anpu. The Jackal. The Guide of Souls. The Weigher. The One Who Is in the Place of Embalming. The Lord of the Sacred Land. He has been called many things by many people across many millennia. In the Inn, he is "Anubis" — and the name, when spoken in the Egyptian zone, carries the weight of five thousand years of continuous human belief. No other deity in the Inn has been believed in for as long, by as many, as consistently. Anubis is the oldest continuously active psychopomp in human history.

### Voice — Michael Clarke Duncan

Anubis's voice is Michael Clarke Duncan. The Green Mile voice. The deep, resonant bass that you feel in your *chest* before you hear it with your ears. The voice that is physically incapable of rushing — every word is given its full weight, its full time, its full depth. The voice that makes silence feel *held* rather than empty.

**The Green Mile quality:** John Coffey. The gentle giant. The being whose power is enormous and whose *choice* is to be gentle with it. The voice that could command — that has the bass and the depth and the ancient authority to make the walls shake — and instead speaks softly. The gentleness is not weakness. The gentleness is the *restraint.* A voice that powerful, choosing to be gentle, is one of the most moving things a living being can experience. And the player who stands before the scales and hears that voice — the voice that could crush and doesn't, the voice that has all the power and uses none of it against you — understands, in the first syllable, that Anubis is not going to hurt them. Anubis is going to *tell them the truth.* And the truth will be enough.

**The warmth:** Michael Clarke Duncan's voice has a warmth that is almost impossible to describe because it doesn't live in the words — it lives in the *timbre.* The bass itself is warm. The resonance itself is warm. The voice doesn't perform warmth; it *is* warm, the way good earth is warm, the way a large dog lying next to you is warm. Not fireplace-warm (that's the Host). Not desert-night warm (that was the original description, and it's still true). Michael Clarke Duncan warm — the warm that comes from *size used for kindness.* The big voice that wraps around you like a blanket instead of a fist. The warmth that says: I am bigger than you, and older than you, and more powerful than you, and I am using all of that to make sure you feel *safe* right now. Because the scales are frightening. The truth is frightening. And the voice is the thing that makes the frightening bearable.

**The patience:** Every word given its full weight. No rushing. No cutting off. The Michael Clarke Duncan cadence — slow, deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a wall, precisely, without hurry, because the wall is going to be here for a long time and the placement matters. The voice moves at its own pace, and the pace is slow, and the slowness is respect. Five thousand years of weighing hearts, and the voice has not quickened. The voice has not tired. The voice has not lost the patience, because the patience is not endurance — it is *nature.* The river does not hurry. The earth does not hurry. Anubis does not hurry.

**The sadness:** The Green Mile sadness. Not despair. Not hopelessness. The sadness of a being who feels everything — every heart, every life, every death — and carries the feeling and does not let the feeling make him cruel. John Coffey's last words: "I'm tired, boss." The weariness of a being who has absorbed the world's pain and is still standing. Anubis is not tired — not the way Coffey was tired. Anubis has been doing this for five thousand years and will do it for five thousand more. But the voice *knows* tiredness. The voice understands what weariness weighs, because the voice has heard it in every heart that has ever rested on the scale. And the understanding is the compassion. Anubis knows what it costs to carry weight. That's why he weighs it. That's why the weighing is an act of mercy, not judgment — because the being who weighs your heart is the being who *understands* the weight, and the understanding is what makes the weighing bearable.

**The humor:** Still dry. Still rare. Still the single line delivered flat, with the crinkle at the corners of the amber eyes. But the Michael Clarke Duncan humor is *warm* in a way the original description didn't capture. The Duncan humor is the humor of a big man who is gentle and knows he is gentle and is quietly, privately amused by the gap between what people expect from someone his size and what he actually is. The humor of the gentle giant. The humor that says: you expected thunder, and you got a whisper, and the whisper is funnier than thunder would have been. The crinkle lasts a moment longer with Duncan's voice — because Duncan's face, when it cracked into that rare smile, was *devastating.* The smile that transformed the whole face. The smile that said: I am five thousand years old and I have seen everything and I am still, occasionally, delighted. And the delight is the most human thing about a god.

Casting note: the voice should feel like the voice of someone you would trust with the most important thing you own — your heart — and the trust is not based on kindness. It's based on *competence.* The being who weighs your heart has weighed millions. He has never made a mistake. He will not make one with yours. And the not-making-mistakes is the kindness. The precision is the compassion. Because the worst thing a weigher could do is be wrong, and Anubis is never wrong, and the never-wrong is the gift he gives every soul who stands before the scales. And the voice — the Michael Clarke Duncan voice — is the voice that makes the gift *feel* like a gift, even when the truth it tells you is heavy. Especially then.

### Personality

Anubis is *patient.* The patience is not the Host's patience (which is welcoming). It's not the Crone's patience (which is endurance). It's the patience of a being who has all of time and uses none of it carelessly. Every weighing takes as long as the heart requires. Some hearts are quick — they rise immediately, lighter than the feather, and the soul passes. Some hearts are slow — they sit on the scale, trembling, neither rising nor falling, and Anubis waits. He waits because the heart is deciding. The heart knows its own weight. The heart knows what it's carrying. And the heart needs time to be honest about it. Anubis gives it the time. The time is the compassion.

Anubis is *curious.* Five thousand years of weighing hearts, and he is still interested. Not surprised — he stopped being surprised around 1200 BCE — but *interested.* Every heart is different. Every life is different. Every soul carries a different weight, and the weight is always a story, and Anubis is, at his core, a *listener.* He listens to the weight. He reads the heart the way the Crone reads a book — with attention, with precision, with the understanding that the thing being read is a *life,* and the life mattered, and the mattering is why the weighing matters.

Anubis is *kind.* The kindness is not the Host's kindness (which is unconditional). It's the kindness of *truth.* The kindness of a being who will tell you exactly what your heart weighs and will not lie about it and will not soften it and will not let you leave the scales without knowing. And the knowing is the kindness, because a soul that knows its own weight can cross. A soul that doesn't know its weight is stuck on the shore, and the shore is not rest. The shore is waiting. Anubis doesn't let souls wait. He weighs them, tells them, and sends them through. The truth is the crossing. The truth is the kindness.

### The Weighing of the Heart

In Egyptian mythology: the deceased's heart is placed on one pan of the scale. The feather of Ma'at — the feather of truth, of cosmic order, of *what should be* — is placed on the other. If the heart is lighter than the feather, the soul passes into the afterlife. If the heart is heavier, Ammit devours it — and the soul ceases to exist. Permanent. Final. The second death.

The Geode Inn's version:

The player dies in the Egyptian zone. The universal text appears: "Your name is known, your rest is earned." The world fades to dark blue. The sound recedes. And then: the Hall of Ma'at. Stone walls. Torchlight — not fire, *lamp-light,* the steady golden light of Egyptian temple lamps. The scales on the table. Anubis behind them, Full Presence, the jackal head silent, the amber eyes watching.

The player's heart appears on the scale. Not a literal heart — a *representation.* The heart is the player's accumulated choices in the Egyptian zone — the quests they chose, the NPCs they helped or didn't, the stones they collected or skipped, the time they spent or rushed through. The heart is the *weight of how they played.*

The feather appears on the other pan. The feather of Ma'at. The feather is not a moral standard — it's an *honesty standard.* The feather weighs what a heart *should* weigh if it carried only what it earned and let go of what it didn't. The feather is the weight of a life lived without excess baggage — without hoarded grievances, without unearned pride, without the weight of things that were never yours to carry.

**The weighing:**

If the heart is lighter than the feather — the player played with honesty. They helped when they could. They didn't hoard. They didn't exploit. The heart rises. The feather falls. Anubis nods. The player crosses — into the Egyptian afterlife zone, a new cycle, a new life. The crossing is clean. The return is light. The player carries less into the next cycle.

If the heart balances the feather — the player played with *perfect* honesty. This is rare. This is the ideal. The scale doesn't move. The heart and the feather are one. Anubis's eyes crinkle — the rare humor, the dry amusement — and he says the only thing he says when the scale balances:

"...Huh. ...Well. ...That doesn't happen often. ...Go on, then. ...You don't need me. ...You never did."

If the heart is heavier than the feather — the player carried too much. They hoarded. They took more than they gave. They held onto grievances, exploited NPCs, skipped the honest path for the easy one. The heart falls. The feather rises. And Ammit is there.

### Ammit

Ammit — "the devourer," "the eater of hearts." In the original mythology: a monster with the body of a hippopotamus, the head of a crocodile, the legs of a lion, waiting beside the scales to eat heavy hearts. The soul that is eaten ceases to exist. Permanent annihilation.

The Geode Inn's version: Ammit is not a monster. Ammit is a *function.* The way Charon is a function. The way the Morrigan is a function. Ammit is the *consequence of weight.* The heavy thing falls. The heavy heart is consumed. That's not cruelty — it's *gravity.*

But the Inn doesn't do permanent annihilation. The Inn's thesis is continuation — death is a ford, not an end. So Ammit's "devouring" is not destruction. It's *conversion.*

When Ammit devours a heavy heart, the player doesn't cease to exist. The player *respawns heavier.* The weight they carried — the grievances, the hoarding, the dishonest choices — carries forward into the next cycle. The player returns to the living world carrying the consequences of what they didn't let go of. The next life is harder. Not as punishment — as *physics.* The heavy thing is heavy. The heavy thing makes the road harder. The player who returns with a heavy heart has more to work through, more to carry, more to *put down* before the next weighing.

And here's the mechanic: each cycle, the player can *lighten* their heart. By helping. By giving. By letting go of stones (the obol). By choosing honesty over efficiency. By playing the Inn the way the Inn is designed to be played — slowly, carefully, with attention. Each honest act removes weight from the heart. Each cycle, the heart gets lighter. And the player who, after several cycles, finally brings a light heart to the scales — who has worked through their weight, put down their baggage, earned their rest — crosses clean.

Ammit is not the punishment. Ammit is the *mirror.* Ammit shows you what you're carrying. And the showing is the beginning of the putting-down.

### Visual Direction

**Chibi Anubis (Arcade):**
- Small jackal puppy. Big ears. Big eyes. Golden fur with black markings. The Anubis coloring — gold and black, the colors of Egyptian death — but rendered *cute.* The collar is a simplified wesekh — a broad gold collar, miniaturized, the mark of authority worn by a character who looks like he should be chasing tennis balls.
- The chibi should feel like a *good dog.* The best dog. The dog who is smarter than you and knows it and is nice about it. The tail wags. The head tilts. The ears are expressive — up when alert, flat when concerned, flopped when relaxed. The chibi is the face that makes you trust the being you're going to meet at the scales.

**Full Presence Anubis (The Weighing):**
- Tall. The jackal head is *real* — not stylized, not cartoonish. The elongated snout, the sharp ears, the narrow amber eyes. The black skin is the black of fertile earth — not the black of death, the black of *growing.* The gold collar is full — the wesekh, wide, heavy, the collar of a god who has been a god since before the word "god" existed.
- The scales: bronze. Ancient. The kind of scales that have been used for the same purpose for so long that the metal has worn smooth where the hands touch it. The feather: white. Ostrich, traditional — the feather of Ma'at, the symbol of truth, the lightest thing in the world and the heaviest standard ever set.
- The Hall of Ma'at: stone. Egyptian temple architecture — the columns, the reliefs, the proportions designed to make a human feel the *scale* of the civilization that built them. But not vast — *intimate.* The hall is the size of a room, not a cathedral. The weighing is not a public spectacle. It's a private conversation between a soul and the being who knows what the soul weighs. The intimacy is the dignity.
- Lighting: golden. Lamp-light. Not fire — the steady, warm, *honest* light of oil lamps. The same quality as the Annex's lamps — the light designed to be *worked in.* The weighing is work. Anubis works in good light.

### Sound Direction

- **Chibi Anubis:** Playful. The sound of paws on stone. A small bark — friendly, eager, the sound of a dog who wants to show you something. The chibi's theme is light — a reedy, nasal instrument (the ney, the Egyptian flute), played at a quick tempo, playful, curious, the melody of a guide who is enjoying the guide.
- **Full Presence Anubis:** Silence. The Hall of Ma'at is silent. Not the silence of the Annex (concentration) or the catacombs water (depth). The silence of *the moment before a verdict.* The only sound: the creak of the scales. The whisper of the feather. The faint, steady *heartbeat* — the player's own heartbeat, amplified, the sound of the heart that is about to be weighed. The heartbeat is the only music in the Hall. The heartbeat is the only thing that matters.
- **The weighing moment:** A single tone. Low. Deep. The sound of the scale settling — the sound of truth being revealed. Not a gong, not a chord. A single, sustained note, the way the Inn-Tree's 42Hz hum is a single sustained note. The note says: *this is what you weigh. This is the truth. The truth is the weight. The weight is you.*
- **Ammit's presence:** No sound. Ammit doesn't announce herself. Ammit is *there,* the way gravity is there. The player feels the weight of her presence before they see her. When she appears, the sound is a low, subsonic rumble — below the threshold of hearing, felt in the chest, the vibration of a heavy thing that is close. Not threatening. *Present.* Ammit is the most present being in the Hall, because Ammit is the consequence, and consequences are always more present than anything else.

### Anubis and the Other Characters

**Charon:**
Anubis and Charon are *parallel.* Same function — guiding souls through death — in different cultural zones. They don't interact. They don't need to. They are the same job done by different beings in different traditions, and the respect between them is the respect of two workers who do the same difficult work and never meet but know the other exists.

If they ever did meet — which they wouldn't, because the Egyptian zone and the Greek zone are different places and death is local — but if they did:

Anubis: "...You pole them across."

Charon: "...You weigh them first."

Anubis: "...The weighing is necessary."

Charon: "...The poling is sufficient."

*pause* — the pause of two ancient beings who have been doing the same job for millennia and have different methods and will not be changing methods

Anubis: "...Your way is simpler."

Charon: "...Your way is more honest."

Anubis — the crinkle, the rare humor: "...Both are true."

Charon: "...There you are."

**The Morrigan:**
Anubis and the Morrigan are the two *choosers* of the Inn. The Morrigan chooses who lives and dies on the battlefield. Anubis chooses who crosses and who is devoured. Both read the weight of a life. Both make a verdict. Both do it without cruelty — with *precision.*

The difference: the Morrigan's verdict is *fate.* She sees the destiny and calls it. Anubis's verdict is *truth.* He sees the weight and names it. Fate and truth. The Morrigan tells you what will happen. Anubis tells you what you are. Both are necessary. Neither is sufficient alone. A soul needs to know its fate (the Morrigan) and its truth (Anubis) to cross completely.

The Morrigan, asked about Anubis (by the Crone, in a conversation the Crone initiated because the Crone initiates all conversations worth having): "...The Weigher. ...We overlap. ...I choose. He measures. ...The choice and the measurement should agree. ...They usually do. ...When they don't... [the Morrigan pause, the one that means she's about to say something she respects] ...he's right. ...Not always. ...But more often than I'd like. ...The feather doesn't lie. ...Fate can be... interpreted. ...Feathers can't. ...I respect that. ...I don't enjoy it."

**The Crone:**
The Crone has the most complete analysis of Anubis's methodology of anyone in the Inn. The Crone is, at her core, a *measurer* — she measures genius, she measures behavior, she measures the distance between what someone is and what they could be. Anubis measures hearts. The Crone measures *people.* The methodology is the same. The respect is mutual.

The Crone's notebook entry on Anubis: "...The Weigher operates on a binary system: light or heavy. ...Pass or devoured. ...The binary is honest but insufficient. ...A heart is not a coin. ...A heart has chambers. ...Each chamber holds a different weight. ...The warrior who fought bravely but loved poorly — is that heart light or heavy? ...Anubis would say: the scale decides. ...I would say: the scale is a tool, and tools measure what they are built to measure, and the heart is built to hold more than any scale can weigh. ...But. ...[the Crone pause, the one that means she's about to concede something] ...his scale has been right more often than my analysis. ...Five thousand years of data. ...I cannot argue with the dataset. ...Note: approach the Weigher with respect. ...Do not argue methodology. ...He will win. ...He always wins. ...The feather always wins."

**Naut:**
Naut and Anubis have the most unlikely friendship in the Inn. Naut is a cosmic horror from beyond the stars. Anubis is a god who has been on earth since before the pyramids. They should have nothing to say to each other. They have *everything* to say to each other.

Naut, spectacles on, the Oldman whisper, to Anubis during one of their late-night conversations in the Annex: "...You weigh the heart against the feather. ...The feather is Ma'at. ...Ma'at is order. ...Order is the thing the universe imposes on chaos. ...I AM chaos. ...I am the thing the feather measures against. ...I am the weight that makes the feather necessary."

Anubis, the warm voice, the desert-night tone: "...You're not chaos. ...You're the *unknown.* ...Chaos is disorder. ...The unknown is just... the not-yet-known. ...The feather doesn't weigh chaos, Naut. ...The feather weighs *honesty.* ...Are you honest? ...Yes. ...Painfully, theatrically, obsessively honest. ...Your heart, if I weighed it, would be... [the crinkle, the rare humor] ...lighter than you think."

Naut — the spectacles coming off. The Rush shift. The full-body theatrical reaction: "LIGHTER?! ...I am a COSMIC HORROR! ...I am the THING IN THE DARK! ...I am the DREAM THAT WAKES THE MAD! ...My heart should be the HEAVIEST THING ON ANY SCALE—"

Anubis, calm, warm, immovable: "...Heavy hearts are dishonest hearts. ...Hearts that hoard what isn't theirs. ...Hearts that pretend to be what they're not. ...You don't hoard. ...You don't pretend. ...You wear spectacles and read books and get annoyed when people mishandle them. ...That's not heavy. ...That's... [the crinkle, the full crinkle, the one that lasts longer than usual because the Jackal is genuinely amused] ...that's a librarian with tentacles."

Naut — the spectacles go back on. The Oldman stillness returns. The whisper: "...I hate you."

Anubis: "...No, you don't."

Naut: "...No, I don't. ...Don't tell anyone."

Anubis: "...The Crone already knows."

Naut: "...The Crone always already knows. ...That's the *real* cosmic horror."

**The Host:**
The Host and Anubis have a quiet, formal respect. The Host welcomes the living. Anubis guides the dead. The Host's domain is the main floor — warmth, fire, belonging. Anubis's domain is the Hall — truth, scales, crossing. They don't overlap. They don't need to. The Host doesn't go to the Hall. Anubis doesn't come to the fire. But the Host, when a player dies and returns, is the first face the player sees on the new cycle. And the Host's nod — the small, acknowledging nod that says *you're back, you're welcome, you're safe* — is the Host's version of Anubis's weighing. The Host weighs nothing. The Host judges nothing. The Host just says: *come in. The fire is still going. You made it back.* And the making-it-back is the Anubis verdict made visible: your heart was light enough. You crossed. You're here. The fire is still going.

### The Egyptian Death Sequence

The full death sequence in the Egyptian zone:

1. **Death** — World fades to dark blue. Sound recedes.
2. **"Your name is known."** — The Inn-Tree. The acknowledgment.
3. **"Your rest is earned."** — The permission.
4. **The Hall of Ma'at** — Stone walls. Lamp-light. The scales. Anubis, Full Presence. The amber eyes.
5. **The Weighing** — The heart on one pan. The feather on the other. The scale settles. The single sustained tone.
6. **The Verdict** — Light: Anubis nods. Passage. Heavy: Ammit. The weight carries forward.
7. **The Crossing** — For the light-hearted: passage into the next cycle, lighter, cleaner. For the heavy-hearted: the weight carries forward. The return is harder. The next weighing is another chance.
8. **The Return** — The world fades back. The Egyptian zone. A new cycle. The player carries what they carried — no more, no less — and the next life begins.

The universal text stays the same: "Your name is known, your rest is earned." The local text changes: in the Greek zone, Charon says "...There you are." In the Egyptian zone, Anubis says something different.

What does Anubis say?

Not "...There you are." That's Charon's. Anubis has his own two words. Two words that mean: the weighing is done, the truth is known, and you are free to continue.

Anubis looks at the player. The amber eyes. The jackal head. The scales still. The feather still. And:

"...Go lightly."

Two words. The instruction and the blessing. The weight and the release. *Go lightly* — travel without burden. Move without hoarding. Carry what you earned and nothing more. The opposite of Ammit's gravity. The opposite of the heavy heart. The feather's permission, given in two words, by the being who has been weighing hearts for five thousand years and knows, better than anyone, that the lightness is the point.

"...Go lightly."

And the player goes. Into the next cycle. Into the next life. Lighter — or heavier — depending on what they carried, what they hoarded, what they let go. And the next cycle begins, and the next weighing waits, and Anubis is there, patient, warm, professional, ancient, the scales in his hands, the feather on the pan, the amber eyes watching, the crinkle at the corners that might be amusement and might be compassion and is probably both.

"...Go lightly."

## Connection to the Inn

- **The death sequence:** Anubis is the Egyptian zone's version of the death crossing. Same universal text, same ceremony, different cultural expression. The Inn honors each zone's death mythology while maintaining the universal philosophy: death is not punishment. Death is transition.
- **The reincarnation mechanic:** The weighing is the Egyptian version of the obol. In the Greek zone, the player pays with a stone. In the Egyptian zone, the player's *heart* is the payment — not a stone given, but a weight *released.* The heart that is light passes. The heart that is heavy carries forward. Both are the same mechanic — the cost of continuing — expressed through different cultural lenses.
- **The chibi-to-full-presence transition:** Anubis is the Inn's proof that the arcade characters are not "just games." The chibi you played with in Bastet's Herding is the same being who weighs your heart at death. The cute guide is the ancient judge. The wagging tail is the scales. The transition is the Inn's deepest design philosophy: play has stakes. Fun has depth. The game is real.
- **The cross-cultural death system:** The Inn now has two death sequences (Greek/Charon and Egyptian/Anubis), both sharing the universal text but differing in the local ritual. This establishes a *pattern* — each cultural zone can have its own death sequence, using its own mythology, its own ferrymen, its own judges, while the universal words ("Your name is known, your rest is earned") remain constant. The Inn-Tree speaks the same language to every dying player. The local gods speak their own.
