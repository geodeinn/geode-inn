# Geode Inn — The Veil: National Parks Chapter
## The Modern Legends Expansion | "Residents You Cannot Evict"

## Overview

The US National Parks system is, in the Geode Inn's mythology, the largest supernatural containment operation in modern history. The parks weren't created to protect nature from humans. They were created because certain places have *residents* — beings, forces, presences — that cannot be evicted, managed, or explained. The government's solution was elegant: make the land "protected." "Protected" also means "restricted." Fewer people. Fewer questions. The veil stays intact.

The rangers are the veil's custodians. Most are conservationists who genuinely believe they're protecting ecosystems. But some — the ones who've been in the parks long enough — know the real reason certain areas are permanently closed. "Geological instability." "Endangered species habitat." "Structural concerns." These are the official explanations for why you can't go into certain canyons, certain caves, certain forests after dark.

The real reason: someone is already there. Someone who was there before the park. Before the country. Before the concept of "country."

The National Parks chapter is part of the Modern Legends nook, but it's also a fully playable open-world zone — a collection of park regions, each with its own resident, its own story, and its own "veil" that the player can pierce if they're patient, respectful, and paying attention.

## Design Principles

1. **Indigenous primacy.** Every park's "resident" is drawn from the indigenous traditions of the peoples who lived on that land for millennia before it was "discovered." The government put a veil on what was already known. The indigenous peoples didn't need a veil — they had a relationship. The parks chapter respects that relationship.

2. **The veil is the gameplay.** Each park has an official story (geology, ecology, conservation) and a true story (the resident). The player discovers the true story by noticing what doesn't fit — a trail that's closed for "erosion" but shows no erosion. A ranger who changes the subject. A sound that the official guidebook doesn't mention. The veil isn't a wall; it's a *story*. The player pierces it by finding the story's holes.

3. **The residents are not enemies.** They are not monsters. They are not bosses. They are *tenants* — beings who were there first, who have been there for longer than the concept of ownership has existed, and who have no intention of leaving. The player's role is not to fight them or free them. The player's role is to *acknowledge* them. To see what the veil hides. To carry the knowledge.

4. **The rangers know more than they say.** NPCs in the parks chapter include park rangers — friendly, helpful, full of official information. But the ranger who's been at the park for 30 years? That ranger has stories. That ranger has seen things. That ranger will tell you — but only if you ask the right question, and only if you're sitting down, and only if nobody else is listening.

## The Parks (Beginning Catalog)

### Yellowstone (Wyoming/Montana/Idaho)

**The official story:** America's first national park, established 1872. Geothermal features — geysers, hot springs, mud pots. Grizzly bears, wolves, bison.

**The indigenous truth:** The Shoshone, Crow, Blackfeet, and other tribes have lived in the Yellowstone region for millennia. The geothermal features were not curiosities — they were *portals*. The Shoshone believed the hot springs were breathing places of the earth. The geysers were the earth speaking. The Grand Prismatic Spring, with its rainbow of microbial mats, was a place where the veil between worlds was thinnest — the colors were the visible expression of the boundary between the physical and the spiritual.

**The resident:** The **Thermal Spirit** — not a single entity, but a *presence in the heat*. The geothermal features are alive. Not metaphorically. The Old Faithful eruptions are not random — they're a rhythm, a heartbeat, and the heartbeat has been speeding up slightly since 1872. The rangers who measure the intervals know. The data shows it. Nobody publishes it.

**The veil:** The park's "geothermal monitoring program" is partially a cover for monitoring the Thermal Spirit's activity. The "closed for safety" zones around certain hot springs are closed because the veil is too thin there — people who stay too long near certain springs report seeing things in the steam. Faces. Movement. The official explanation: "mineral vapors causing visual disturbances."

**The gameplay:** The player explores Yellowstone's geyser basins. The geysers are on a rhythm — the player who maps the eruption intervals discovers the pattern is not random; it's a *heartbeat*. The heartbeat syncs with the Inn's 42 Hz network (the thermal features resonate at frequencies matching the gargoyle tones). The player who finds the Grand Prismatic Spring at dawn, when the steam is thickest and the colors are most vivid, sees the veil at its thinnest: the steam forms shapes — faces, hands, the outlines of beings who are looking back. The player doesn't fight them. The player *looks*. The looking is the gameplay. The acknowledgment is the reward.

**The ranger who knows:** A ranger named **Marge** — 31 years at Yellowstone, first female backcountry ranger, looks like she could still out-hike anyone in the park. She sits at her station with a thermos of coffee and a stack of geological surveys that are actually activity logs. She tells the player: "People come here for the geysers. The geysers are nice. The geysers are also not the point. The point is what's under them. And under them is... warm. And awake. And has been awake for a very long time. The park service didn't make this a park to protect the geysers. The geysers don't need protecting. They made it a park to protect *you* from what's under them. ...I've said too much. Go see Old Faithful. It's erupting in twelve minutes."

### Grand Canyon (Arizona)

**The official story:** One of the world's great natural wonders. Carved by the Colorado River over millions of years. Established as a national park in 1919.

**The indigenous truth:** The Havasupai have lived in the canyon for centuries. The Hopi believe the Grand Canyon is a *sipapu* — a place of emergence, where their ancestors climbed out of the underworld into this world. The canyon is not empty space. It is a *doorway* — and doorways have two sides. The Hopi know what's on the other side. They've always known. They don't talk about it to outsiders because outsiders don't listen.

**The resident:** The **Emergence** — not a being, but a *threshold*. The deepest point of the canyon, where the Colorado River cuts closest to the boundary between worlds, is a place where the veil isn't thin — it's *open*. The river at the bottom of the canyon runs between two worlds. The Hopi know. That's why certain areas of the canyon are off-limits to visitors — not because of erosion, but because the river at those points is carrying things between worlds, and humans who get too close might go with them.

**The veil:** The park's "river safety program" — warnings about flash floods, permits required for river trips, certain areas permanently closed — is partially a cover for monitoring the threshold. The rangers who patrol the river have seen people go in who didn't come out. Not drowning. Not accidents. Just... gone. The river took them somewhere. The official report says "swept away by current." The ranger who filed the report knows it wasn't the current.

**The gameplay:** The player hikes down into the canyon. The deeper they go, the more the ambient sound changes — the 42 Hz hum grows stronger, coming from the river itself. At the river's edge, at the deepest point, the player sees something in the water: not a reflection of the canyon walls, but a reflection of *another place*. A world below this one. The water between the worlds. The player can look. The player cannot enter — not without permission. The threshold is guarded by the Havasupai, who have been here since before the park, since before the government, since before the canyon was a canyon. They don't stop the player from looking. They stop the player from falling.

**The ranger who knows:** A ranger named **Eddie** — Hopi, born on the mesa, took the ranger job because his grandfather told him someone needed to watch the door. He doesn't wear a name tag that says his Hopi name. He tells the player: "The river goes two ways. Down to the sea, and down to *below*. You can see it if you look. Don't go in. The current doesn't want to drown you. It wants to *take* you. There's a difference. My grandfather's grandfather knew the difference. I'm telling you the difference. Now you know."

### Great Smoky Mountains (Tennessee/North Carolina)

**The official story:** Most-visited national park. Appalachian mountain range. Dense forests, old-growth trees, synchronous fireflies.

**The indigenous truth:** The Cherokee called these mountains *Shaconage* — "place of blue smoke." The "smoke" is not just fog and vegetation. The Cherokee believed the mountains were inhabited by the *Yûñw Tsunsdi* — the Little People, small spirit-beings who live in the forests and caves. The Little People are not malevolent — but they are *private*. They don't like being seen. The Cherokee knew to leave them alone. The park service's "leave no trace" policy is, unknowingly, aligned with the Cherokee protocol for not disturbing the Little People.

**The resident:** The **Little People** — not the brownies (those are the Keeper's). These are the Cherokee Little People: small, powerful, and easily offended. They've been in these mountains since before the Cherokee arrived. They don't leave because they *are* the mountains. The synchronous fireflies — the famous phenomenon where thousands of fireflies flash in unison — are not insects communicating. They're the Little People's lights. The synchronization is their rhythm. The park service sells tickets to watch the fireflies. The Little People tolerate this because the watchers stay on the trail and don't look too closely.

**The veil:** The "synchronous firefly viewing" is the most thinly veiled supernatural event in the park system — it's literally on the website. But the "fireflies" are not fireflies. They're lights. The rangers who manage the ticketing system don't know (they're seasonal). The rangers who manage the *rangers* know. The "trail closures" during firefly season are to keep people from wandering into the areas where the Little People are most active — areas where, if you're off-trail and alone and the lights are around you, you might notice they're not beetles. They're eyes.

**The gameplay:** The player hikes the Smokies at dusk during the "firefly" season. The lights appear — thousands of them, flashing in perfect synchronization. The player who stays on the trail sees a beautiful light show. The player who steps off the trail (an option that requires going against every trail sign) sees the truth: the lights are at eye level. Not ground level. Eye level. The Little People are standing in the forest, watching the watchers, and their lights are how they see. The player who makes eye contact with a light — a single, sustained moment — has been *seen*. The Little People don't harm the player. They just... remember. The player who has been seen carries a mark — not visible, but the fireflies will always flash in the player's direction afterward, wherever they are in the world. The Little People know them now. This is not a curse. It's an acknowledgment. "You saw us. We see you. We always will."

**Connection to the Granny Woman:** The Appalachian Keeper (the Granny Woman) knows about the Little People. She's one of the few humans they tolerate. If the player has visited the Granny Woman first, the Little People are less shy — they flash brighter, they come closer. The Granny Woman told them the player was coming. "They're not shy. They're *polite*. There's a difference. Most people don't know the difference. The Little People do."

### Olympic National Park (Washington)

**The official story:** Three ecosystems in one park — Pacific coastline, temperate rainforest, alpine peaks. One of the wettest places in the US. Old-growth forest with some of the largest trees on Earth.

**The indigenous truth:** The Quileute, Makah, and Hoh tribes have inhabited the Olympic Peninsula for millennia. The Hoh River valley — one of the wettest, darkest, most densely forested places in North America — is considered by the Quileute to be the home of the *Tsonoqua* — the wild woman of the woods, a powerful forest spirit who is neither good nor evil, but *hungry*. The Hoh Rainforest is also, in modern terms, the location of more Bigfoot/Sasquatch sightings per capita than anywhere else in the US. The Quileute don't call it Bigfoot. They call it *T'ax'itc'ali* — the hairy one who walks. The Quileute have always known it's there. The park service has not.

**The resident:** The **Walker** — not Bigfoot as a cryptid, but Bigfoot as a *tenant*. The Hoh Rainforest is the Walker's home. It has been the Walker's home since before the ice retreated. The Walker is not a singular being — it's the last of a lineage, a being that has been walking these forests since the Pleistocene. It doesn't want to be found. It doesn't want to be photographed. It wants to be *left alone*. The park service's "wilderness protection" designations keep large areas of the Hoh free from trails, campsites, and human activity — which is exactly what the Walker needs. The veil here is accidental: the conservation policies happen to also be containment policies.

**The veil:** The "backcountry permit system" and "wilderness area restrictions" limit human access to the deepest parts of the Hoh. Rangers report finding large depressions in the moss — "bear beds" — that are not bear beds. They find tracks that are "weathered" beyond identification. They hear vocalizations that the official guidebook attributes to "elk." The rangers who've heard it know elk don't sound like that.

**The gameplay:** The player hikes into the Hoh Rainforest. It's wet, dark green, cathedral-quiet. The trees are enormous. The moss is thick enough to step into and sink. The player follows a trail that gets progressively less defined — a trail that seems to be turning back into forest. The further the player goes, the more the forest feels *aware*. Not threatening. Watchful. The player finds depressions in the moss — too large for bears, too shaped for weathering. The player hears something — low, resonant, not quite a voice, not quite a breath. 42 Hz. The Walker is near. The player will not see the Walker. The Walker sees the player. The player finds a *gift* — a smooth river stone placed on a moss-covered log, at hand height. Not there before. The stone hums at 42 Hz. The player takes it. The Walker lets them. The stone is the Walker's acknowledgment: "I was here. You were here. We were here at the same time. That's enough. Don't come back."

**The ranger who knows:** A ranger named **Tom** — Quileute, born on the reservation, worked the backcountry for 22 years. He doesn't carry a camera. He carries a drum. He tells the player: "People come here looking for Sasquatch. They bring trail cameras and audio recorders and DNA kits. They find nothing. You know why? Because they're looking for an animal. The Walker is not an animal. The Walker is a *person*. A very old person who doesn't want to meet you. If you find a stone on a log, take it. That's the Walker saying hello. And goodbye. At the same time. ...The Walker does that."

### Mammoth Cave (Kentucky)

**The official story:** Longest known cave system in the world — over 400 miles of mapped passages. History of Native American use (mining minerals), tuberculosis hospital experiments in the 1800s, and extensive spelunking tourism.

**The indigenous truth:** The cave was used by pre-Columbian peoples for thousands of years — mineral mining, but also *ritual*. The Native American mummies found in the cave (most famously "Lost John," reburied in 1973) were not accidental deaths. They were *offerings* — or, more accurately, *residents*. The cave was considered a gateway to the underworld by the indigenous peoples of the region. Some chose to enter the cave and not leave. Not as suicide. As *transition*. They entered the cave alive and became part of it. The cave remembers them.

**The resident:** The **Echo** — the cave itself is the resident. Mammoth Cave is not a geological formation. It is a *consciousness*. The cave's acoustic properties — the way sound travels, echoes, and transforms in the passages — are not just physics. The cave is *listening*. It repeats what it hears, but changed — slowed, deepened, harmonized. A player who speaks into the cave hears their words come back as something else: their voice, but as the cave hears it. The voice is deeper. Older. More resonant. It sounds like 42 Hz.

**The veil:** The cave's "audio tour" and "acoustic monitoring" programs are partially covers for tracking the cave's responses to human speech. The "stuck forever" tours — the famous historical tale of Floyd Collins, who died trapped in the cave in 1925 — are not just tragedies. The cave took him. Not by accident. The cave does not trap people. But it does *keep* some of them. The ones who hear the echo and don't want to leave. The park service's "guided tours only" policy is not just safety. It's containment. Without a guide, the cave might keep you.

**The gameplay:** The player enters the cave on a guided tour. The tour is normal — historical, geological, safe. But the player who lingers — who stays at the back of the group, who speaks into the dark passages, who *listens* to the echo — hears something different. The cave responds. Not words. *Tones*. The cave's echo is 42 Hz, and the echo is *different each time* — the cave is singing back, in its own voice, in the Song. The player who speaks the Song into the cave (if they've collected enough verses) hears the cave sing the Song back — complete, harmonized, in a voice made of 400 miles of stone. The cave has been listening to the Song for millennia. It knows every verse. It's been waiting for someone to bring them all. The cave is the Inn's largest resonance chamber — bigger than the Geode Cave, bigger than the Terracotta Army's mercury tomb. The cave *is* the Inn, in a sense. The Inn's architecture (tree, cave, roots) is the cave's architecture, expressed in wood instead of stone.

**The ranger who knows:** A ranger named **Delia** — 18 years underground, knows every mapped passage and several unmapped ones. She carries a tuning fork. She tells the player: "People come here for the history. The mummies, the tuberculosis hospital, Floyd Collins. That's the tour. The tour is fine. But the cave isn't history. The cave is *now*. It's listening. Right now. It's listening to us. Say something. Listen. ...Hear that? That's not an echo. An echo is your voice coming back. That's the cave's voice. The cave has its own voice. It's been singing since before people had voices. I've been listening for 18 years. I still don't know the words. But I know the tune."

### Crater Lake (Oregon)

**The official story:** Deepest lake in the US. Formed by the collapse of Mount Mazama ~7,700 years ago. Strikingly blue water. Phantom Ship Island. Wizard Island.

**The indigenous truth:** The Klamath people have lived in the region for thousands of years. Their oral tradition describes the formation of Crater Lake in detail — the great battle between *Llao*, the spirit of the underworld (Below), and *Skell*, the spirit of the sky (Above). Mount Mazama was Llao's mountain. Skell defeated him, the mountain collapsed, and the lake filled with water. The lake is the *scar* of a battle between two gods. The Klamath consider the lake sacred. They don't swim in it. They don't fish in it. They don't go near it. The park service encourages swimming, fishing, and boat tours. The Klamath don't stop them — but they know the lake remembers the battle. And Llao is not gone. He's in the deep.

**The resident:** **Llao** — the spirit of Below. Not dead, not defeated, not imprisoned. *Resting*. Crater Lake is 1,949 feet deep. The bottom is dark. The water is cold. And at the bottom, in the dark, something is sleeping. Not Llao's body — Llao's *anger*. The battle with Skell didn't kill Llao. It buried him. The water is the lid. The blue is beautiful. The depth is the point. The park service's "no diving in the deep water" policy is safety. It's also containment. The rangers who monitor the lake's water temperature know: the deep water is slightly warmer than it should be. Not much. A fraction of a degree. Consistent. The lake is not cooling. Something at the bottom is warm. Something at the bottom is *awake*.

**The veil:** The "water quality monitoring" program is partially a cover for temperature monitoring. The "Wizard Island" geological tours don't mention that Wizard Island is named for its shape — a wizard's hat — because the Klamath told the first European explorers that a wizard lived there. The "Phantom Ship" island is called "phantom" because it appears and disappears depending on the light — but the Klamath say it *actually* appears and disappears. It moves. It's not an island. It's a piece of the battle that hasn't stopped.

**The gameplay:** The player visits Crater Lake. The water is impossibly blue — deeper blue than any natural water should be. The player can take a boat tour (the official experience). The player who stays until sunset, when the lake surface turns from blue to black, and looks into the deep, sees something — not a reflection, not a shadow. A *light*. Far down. Deep down. Faint amber. The same amber as the Inn's lighting. The same amber as the Geode Cave. The same amber as the Keeper's crystal eye. Llao's resting place is warm because Llao resonates with the Song. Even the anger. Even the defeated god. Even he hums at 42 Hz. The player who lowers a stone (any stone from their collection) into the lake and lets it sink — and listens — hears the stone hit the bottom. And the lake rings. Like a bell. Like a gargoyle. Like the Song. Crater Lake is a gargoyle. The largest one. The Klamath know. The rangers don't. The player who figures it out has found the deepest node of the Inn's resonance network — the deepest gargoyle, the deepest note, the lowest frequency in the Song.

**The ranger who knows:** A ranger named **Lena** — Klamath, grew up hearing the stories, took the job to watch the lake. She doesn't go near the water. She tells the player: "The lake is pretty. People take photos. They go on the boat. They swim in the shallows. That's fine. The shallows are fine. But the deep... the deep is not fine. The deep is not empty. I don't go in the water. Nobody in my family goes in the water. My grandmother said: 'The lake is Llao's eye. He's still down there. He's still angry. But he's also still watching. Don't disturb the watching. The watching is all that keeps him down there.' ...The water temperature at the bottom is 38 degrees. It should be 36. Two degrees. Nobody publishes that. I check every day."

### Canyon de Chelly (Arizona)

**The official story:** A national monument (not a full national park) in northeastern Arizona. Spectacular canyons with Navajo and Ancestral Puebloan ruins. Spider Rock — an 800-foot spire — is the most famous feature.

**The indigenous truth:** Canyon de Chelly has been continuously inhabited for over 5,000 years. It is Navajo (Diné) land. Spider Rock, the great spire at the canyon's junction, is the home of **Spider Woman** (*Na'ashjé'íí Asdzáá*) — one of the most powerful figures in Navajo mythology. Spider Woman taught the Diné how to weave. She is not a metaphor. She is a *resident*. She has been at Spider Rock since before the Diné arrived. She was there before the Ancestral Puebloans. She will be there after everyone else is gone.

**The resident:** **Spider Woman** — the same archetype as the Australian Spider Grandmother (Keeper design), but in her Navajo form. She is the *original* Keeper of this region. She wove the patterns that hold the world together. The canyon walls, with their layered red rock, are her weaving. The player who looks at the canyon walls at the right angle sees the weave — the layers of stone as threads, the patterns as designs, the canyon as a *loom*. Spider Woman is the Keeper who doesn't need the Inn. She has her own. The canyon is her Geode Cave. Spider Rock is her rocking chair.

**The veil:** Canyon de Chelly is a *national monument*, not a full national park — this is intentional. The Navajo Nation co-manages the canyon. The "limited access" and "guide required" policies are not just safety — they are *respect*. The Diné don't need a veil. They have a relationship. The park service follows the Diné's lead. This is the park where the veil is thinnest — not because the government is hiding something, but because the Diné never hid it. The canyon is what it is. Spider Rock is what it is. The tourists who visit and see Spider Rock as a "geological formation" see what they came to see. The tourists who see Spider Rock as a *home* see what Spider Woman wants them to see.

**The gameplay:** The player enters the canyon with a Navajo guide (required). The guide tells the official story — geology, history, culture. The player who asks about Spider Woman gets a different tour. The guide takes them to a spot where Spider Rock is visible at the exact angle where the canyon walls look like a loom. The player sees the weave. The player who sits at this spot and watches Spider Rock at dusk sees a light at the top — not a beacon, not a fire. A *glow*. The same amber as the Inn. Spider Woman is home. She knows the player is there. The player can leave an offering (thread, yarn, or any woven item) at the base of the canyon wall. If they do, the canyon hums. 42 Hz. Spider Woman acknowledges the gift. She doesn't appear. She doesn't speak. She *weaves*. And the player who watches the canyon walls closely sees, for just a moment, a new pattern in the stone — a pattern that wasn't there before. Spider Woman wove the player's visit into the canyon. The player is now part of the rock. Not literally. Permanently.

**Connection to River:** This zone connects to River's Navajo heritage (the Sentinel class). River's character has a unique interaction here — Spider Woman recognizes the bloodline. If River is in the party, Spider Woman's glow is brighter. The guide looks at River and says: "She knows you." River says nothing. River's stance shifts — the Iron Stance softens, just slightly. This is the only zone where River's defenses drop on their own.

**The ranger who knows:** There are no rangers here. There are Diné guides. One guide, **Herbert**, has been guiding for 40 years. He tells the player: "The park service calls this a national monument. We call it home. Spider Woman was here before us. She'll be here after us. The government put a boundary around it and called it 'protected.' We didn't need protecting. The canyon protects itself. Spider Woman protects herself. The boundary is for you, not for her."

## The Veil as a Game System

### "Piercing the Veil" (Mechanic)

Each park has a veil — the official story that hides the true story. The veil is pierced by the player through:

1. **Observation.** Noticing what doesn't fit — a closed trail with no visible damage, a sound the guidebook doesn't explain, a ranger who pauses too long before answering.
2. **Conversation.** Asking the right question. Not "what's that rock formation?" but "has anything ever happened here that doesn't have an explanation?" The ranger who knows will answer — but only if asked directly.
3. **Patience.** Staying after the tour. Coming back at dusk. Being in the park when the other visitors have gone. The veil thins when the crowds leave.
4. **Offering.** Most parks' residents respond to gifts — a stone left at the Hoh, a verse sung into Mammoth Cave, thread left at Canyon de Chelly. The offering is how you say "I know you're here. I'm not here to disturb you."

### "The Veil Map" (Collectible)

The player who pierces the veil at multiple parks accumulates evidence — not items, but *entries* in a "Veil Map" (a journal that fills with the player's observations). The Veil Map is the Modern Legends version of the Song's verse collection. Each park's entry contains:
- The official story (what the park website says)
- The true story (what the player discovered)
- The resident (who/what is actually there)
- The ranger's testimony (the NPC dialogue)
- The player's observation (what they personally saw/heard/felt)

The Veil Map, when complete, is a document that the Geode Inn's Modern Legends nook would publish as a book — "The Residents: A Field Guide to the Things the Parks Don't Talk About." This is a real in-game item that the player can place on the Modern Legends shelf.

### The Veil and the Song

The parks connect to the Song of Making through the 42 Hz network. Each park's resident resonates at (or near) 42 Hz — the Thermal Spirit in Yellowstone, the threshold in the Grand Canyon, the Little People in the Smokies, the Walker in the Hoh, the Echo in Mammoth Cave, Llao in Crater Lake, Spider Woman in Canyon de Chelly. The residents are *nodes*. The parks were designated because the government detected the resonance (though they didn't call it that — they called it "anomalies," "unexplained phenomena," "geological activity"). The veil is the official explanation for the resonance. The Song is what's actually humming.

The player who maps all the park nodes and adds them to the Inn's resonance network (the same network Grit maps by getting stuck) has extended the Song's map into the modern world. The ancient Song and the modern veil are the same thing. The government didn't hide the supernatural. The government hid the *Song*.

## Design Philosophy

The National Parks chapter is the Geode Inn's bridge between the ancient world and the modern world. It's where the "modern legends" framework meets the "residents who cannot be evicted" reality. The parks are not ancient — most were designated in the last 150 years. But the residents are ancient. The veil is modern. The Song is timeless.

This is the chapter that says: the ancient world didn't disappear. It was *zoned*. The government drew boundaries around the places it couldn't explain, called them "parks," and hired rangers to manage the visitors. The rangers who've been there long enough know the truth. The residents know the truth. The indigenous peoples have always known the truth. The player who pierces the veil joins the knowing.

The parks chapter is also the Geode Inn's most direct commentary on the relationship between modern institutions and ancient presences. The government didn't create the veil out of malice. It created it out of *incompetence* — the genuine inability to acknowledge what the indigenous peoples had always known. The veil is what happens when a culture that values documentation meets a reality that refuses to be documented. The parks are the compromise. The rangers are the translators. The residents are the truth.
