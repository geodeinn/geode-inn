## OVERVIEW

Each facet of the Geode Inn — each floor, each zone — is rendered in the visual language of a specific artist. The artist's style defines the zone's color palette, composition, texture, and emotional register. The Linework-as-UI system (heavy cel-shaded, soft flowing, sharp/angular, no-outline, fragmented) overlays on top of the artist's style, determining how characters and objects are rendered within each zone.

**The 14 Artists:**

## LEVEL 1: COSMOLOGY — J.M.W. TURNER

**The Artist:** Turner's late paintings are pure LIGHT. Forms dissolve. The sun is a presence, not a shape. Skies become oceans of luminous color. The boundary between sea and sky, between earth and heaven, between matter and light — erased.

**Why Turner for Cosmology:** The Cosmology level is the Inn's highest point — the canopy, the stars, the gods. The Allfather's Observatory. Bramble's window nook. The 42Hz source above. Turner's dissolving forms match the no-outline cosmological linework — everything is defined by light, not by edges. The gods don't have boundaries. The stars don't have outlines. The Cosmology is the level where the player stops seeing shapes and starts seeing LIGHT.

**Color Palette:**
- Turner's late palette: luminous gold (#E5C158), dissolving amber, warm white, deep sunset orange
- The night sky: deep indigo (#1A0B2E) with Turner's signature luminescent edges — stars not as points but as soft, spreading halos
- The Allfather's Observatory: Turner's "Golden Light" phase — everything bathed in warm, directional light that erases shadows

**Key Visual Elements:**
- **The Allfather's Observatory** — a domed space where the walls are so suffused with light that the architecture dissolves. The player can't tell where the ceiling ends and the sky begins. Turner's "Interior of a Great Hall" energy — vast, luminous, dissolving
- **The Vanir Garden (Freyr & Freyja)** — Turner's landscapes: flowers that are splashes of color rather than detailed forms. Green that's more FELT than seen. Freyr's golden-hour glow is Turner's late sunlight
- **Bramble's Nook** — the quietest corner. Turner's watercolor washes — a few brushstrokes that suggest a window, a desk, a presence. Bramble's nook is the most Turner-like space: minimal, luminous, defined by the quality of light rather than the density of detail
- **The 42Hz Source** — the Sun visible through the Observatory's lens. Turner's "Sun of Venice" — the sun as a pure, spreading circle of light, not a disk but a radiance. The 42Hz frequency is visible as a Turner light effect: a golden aura that spreads from the sun and touches everything

**Linework:** No-outline (cosmological). Everything is defined by Turner's light. Characters are luminous shapes, not outlined figures. The Allfather is a presence of light, not a drawn form. Bramble is a soft glow in a window.

### KANDINSKY DRAFT (RETIRED) — WASSILY KANDINSKY

**The Artist:** Kandinsky painted MUSIC. His abstract compositions are visual symphonies — geometric shapes (circles, triangles, lines) arranged in relationships like notes in a composition. He theorized that colors and forms have inherent musical equivalents. His paintings ARE frequencies made visible.

**Why Kandinsky for Clockwork Skies:** The Clockwork Skies are the Inn's mechanical/rhythmic level — the Arcana ship, the 50 BPM heartbeat, the crew at their stations, the Canopy City. Kandinsky's visual music is the perfect match: the gearwork IS a Kandinsky composition. Each gear is a circle, each lever is a line, each crew station is a geometric element in the composition. The 50 BPM heartbeat is Kandinsky's rhythm — the painting pulses.

**Color Palette:**
- Kandinsky's "Improvisations" palette: deep blue (#1D4E5B), fiery red-orange (#CC4422), bright yellow (#FFD700), emerald green (#2D8659), pure black, pure white
- The Arcana ship: geometric shapes in Kandinsky's primary colors against a deep blue sky
- The Canopy City: platforms as Kandinsky circles, each a different color, connected by lines (the Silk Road routes)

**Key Visual Elements:**
- **The Arcana** — the ship IS a Kandinsky painting. The hull is a deep blue curve. The sails are triangles. The gearwork is circles within circles. The Captain's bell is a golden circle. The whole ship is an "Improvisation" — a composition of geometric forms in dynamic relationship
- **The Crew Stations** — each station is a Kandinsky element. The Helm is a circle (control). The Rigging is a triangle (action). The Lookout is a vertical line (observation). The Engine is a square (stability). The crew are geometric forms moving through a geometric composition
- **The 50 BPM Heartbeat** — the painting PULSES. Every 1.2 seconds (50 BPM), the entire scene subtly shifts — colors brighten, shapes expand, the composition breathes. The heartbeat is visual, not just audible. Kandinsky's rhythm made literal
- **The Canopy City** — nine circular platforms arranged in a Kandinsky composition. Each platform is a different color. The Silk Road routes are lines connecting the circles. The city is a MUSICAL SCORE seen from above — the circles are notes, the lines are the staff, the routes are the melody

**Linework:** Sharp/angular (guardian style). Kandinsky's forms are precise — clean lines, geometric accuracy. The crew are angular figures, their cel-shading matching Kandinsky's bold color blocks.

## LEVEL 3: MAIN FLOOR — HENRI MATISSE

**The Artist:** Matisse is JOY. Color for its own sake. Flowing forms that celebrate being alive. The "Joy of Life" is his thesis. Late Matisse — the paper cutouts — is the peak: simple, bold, colorful shapes arranged with the confidence of someone who has spent a lifetime learning how to simplify.

**Why Matisse for the Main Floor:** The Main Floor is the Inn's heart — the arcade, the market, the bar, the baby dragon, the social hub. It's where the player returns to. It's HOME. Matisse's joy is the Main Floor's emotional register: warm, colorful, alive, welcoming. The flowing lines (soft flowing linework) match Matisse's organic, curving forms.

**Color Palette:**
- Matisse's cutout palette: ultramarine blue, cadmium red, tropical green, sun yellow, coral orange
- The Main Floor is the WARMEST zone in the Inn — amber, gold, deep red, warm brown. The hobbit-hole cozy aesthetic with Matisse's color confidence
- The arcade: each arcade machine is a different Matisse color — a bold block of cadmium red, tropical green, or ultramarine

**Key Visual Elements:**
- **The Arcade** — Matisse cutout style. Each game station is a bold, colorful shape against the Main Floor's warm background. The arcade is a Matisse composition — simple shapes, vivid colors, arranged with playful confidence
- **The Market** — flowing Matisse forms. Market stalls as curving, organic shapes. Goods as splashes of color. The market is the "Joy of Life" — abundance made visible
- **The Baby Dragon's Geode** — the dragon's cave is a Matisse cutout: a simple, organic, colorful shape. The dragon is a curving line, a flowing form, a splash of warm color. The geode is a Matisse spiral — the same organic curve Matisse used in his cutout compositions
- **The Wanderer's Table** — the quest board is a Matisse still life: objects arranged on a surface, each a different color, each a different shape. The Wanderer (Odin) is a flowing line, a gentle curve, a warm presence

**Linework:** Soft flowing (lore style). Matisse's forms are organic, curving, confident. Characters on the Main Floor are rendered in flowing lines — no sharp angles, no rigid geometry. The Main Floor is where the Inn feels most ALIVE.

## LEVEL 4: CAMPGROUNDS — ALPHONSE MUCHA

**The Artist:** Mucha defined Art Nouveau — flowing organic lines, decorative borders, botanical elements, elegant female figures framed by ornamental halos. His posters are literally advertisements for crafts, performances, and products. The Campgrounds ARE a Mucha poster.

**Why Mucha for the Campgrounds:** The Campgrounds are the Inn's Ren Faire — market stalls, artisan shops, Jay's tavern, Solstara's bead-configurator. Everything is handcrafted, decorative, organic. Mucha's Art Nouveau is the aesthetic of CRAFT — of beautiful, functional objects decorated with organic forms. The Campgrounds' market stalls are Mucha posters; the Ren Faire IS Art Nouveau.

**Color Palette:**
- Mucha's palette: muted earth tones, olive green, dusty rose, mustard yellow, deep burgundy, cream, gold accents
- The Campgrounds are EARTHY — the outdoor palette. Not the Main Floor's warm interior, but the colors of an outdoor market: canvas, wood, stone, foliage
- Mucha's signature gold appears in the Campgrounds' decorative elements — the stall signs, the lantern frames, the bead-board metalwork

**Key Visual Elements:**
- **The Market Stalls** — Mucha posters. Each stall is framed by a Mucha-style decorative border — flowing vines, ornamental corners, a circular panel above the stall with the artisan's name. The stalls are Art Nouveau advertisements for the artisans' work
- **Jay's Tavern** — a Mucha interior. Flowing wood grain, decorative ironwork, botanical prints on the walls. Jay is a Mucha figure — tall, elegant, framed by ornamental elements. The wolf is a Mucha animal — a stylized, flowing form
- **Solstara's Shop** — the bead-configurator. Mucha's decorative precision applied to jewelry. The shop is an Art Nouveau jewelry display — beads arranged in Mucha-style ornamental patterns. The Bead Board itself is a Mucha composition tool — the tray is an ornamental frame, the dips are decorative elements
- **The Blacksmith's Forge** — Mucha's metalwork aesthetic. The forge is heavy, functional, but DECORATED — Art Nouveau ironwork. The Blacksmith's tools are beautiful objects. The copper/silver/gold chord is a Mucha color palette: copper (#B87333), silver (#C0C0C0), gold (#FFD700)

**Linework:** Heavy cel-shaded (tradesman style). Mucha's figures have bold outlines — the Art Nouveau outline. Jay, Solstara, the Blacksmith — all rendered with Mucha's confident outlines and cel-shaded color blocks.

## LEVEL 5: STAIRCASE — M.C. ESCHER

**The Artist:** Escher's work is about IMPOSSIBLE GEOMETRY — staircases that go nowhere and everywhere, tessellations where one form transforms into another, spaces that fold back on themselves. "Ascending and Descending" — the infinite staircase. "Sky and Water" — birds becoming fish. "Relativity" — three gravities in one space.

**Why Escher for the Staircase:** The Staircase is the Inn's spine — the transitional space, the alchemical bridge. The ascending-key system (music rises as you climb) is Escher's "Ascending and Descending" made audible. The Staircase connects all 9 levels — it's a space that contains all directions at once. Escher's impossible geometry is the Staircase's architecture: stairs that seem to go up and down simultaneously, landings that connect to multiple floors, a space that doesn't follow normal physics.

**Color Palette:**
- Escher's palette: black, white, and gray tones, with selective color accents
- The Staircase is the Inn's most MONOCHROMATIC zone — stone gray, wood brown, iron black. The only color comes from the alchemical elements and the gargoyle frequencies (each gargoyle's frequency has a color: Watcher=amber, Listener=blue, Caller=gold, Sage=green, Gatekeeper=adaptive, Keeper=adaptive)
- The ascending-key system adds color as the player climbs: each landing is a slightly warmer tone than the one below, so the Staircase visually warms as the player ascends — a gradient from cool gray (bottom) to warm amber (top)

**Key Visual Elements:**
- **The Staircase Itself** — Escher's impossible geometry. The stairs curve, double back, connect to multiple landings. The player climbs and the perspective shifts — the player is never sure if they're going up or down. The Staircase is not a straight vertical shaft; it's a FOLD — a space that contains all directions
- **The Clockmaker's Station** — an Escher tessellation. The gears interlock in patterns that transform — one gear shape morphs into another as the player watches. The Clockmaker's workspace is an Escher print: tools that are also shapes, shapes that are also tools. The master gear is an Escher "Circle Limit" — a circle filled with interlocking forms that diminish toward the edge
- **The Gargoyles' Perches** — each gargoyle perches on a landing where the staircase seems to fold. The gargoyles are the fixed points in the Escher space — they don't move because they ARE the architecture. The gargoyles are the load-bearing elements of the impossible geometry
- **The Alchemist's Scale** — an Escher balance. The Scale's two pans exist in different geometric spaces — one pan is on one side of an Escher fold, the other pan is on the other side. The player places materials in one pan and they appear transformed in the other. The Scale IS an Escher transformation: input becomes output through impossible geometry

**Linework:** Fragmented (crisis style) near the bottom (the failing gearwork), transitioning to sharp/angular (guardian style) near the top (the restored gearwork). The Staircase's linework CHANGES as the player climbs — the visual instability at the bottom resolves into geometric clarity at the top.

## LEVEL 6: CATACOMBS — FRANCISCO GOYA

**The Artist:** Goya's Black Paintings — painted directly on the walls of his house, dark, intimate, haunted. "Saturn Devouring His Son." "The Witches' Sabbath." Goya's late work is the art of someone who has seen too much and can't unsee it. Dark grounds, thick paint, figures emerging from shadow. But also: Goya's etchings — "Los Caprichos" — precise, satirical, layered with meaning.

**Why Goya for the Catacombs:** The Catacombs are the Inn's archive — ancient, deep, the weight of accumulated knowledge. The Crone's measuring. Beverley's library. Charles's natural history. The Visitors' Shelf. The Catacombs are the level where the Inn's history is HEAVIEST — where the weight of everything the Inn knows becomes physical. Goya's Black Paintings are the aesthetic of weight: dark, intimate, powerful. The Catacombs are the Inn's Goya room.

**Color Palette:**
- Goya's Black Paintings palette: deep brown-black, ochre, earth red, charcoal, cream-white highlights
- The Catacombs are the Inn's DARKEST interior — not gothic-dark (the Undercity has that) but EARTH-dark. The color of soil, stone, old paper, old ink. The color of time
- Candlelight: warm amber highlights against the dark ground — the only light source. The Catacombs are lit by candles and oil lamps, and the light is Goya's light: a small warm circle in a vast darkness

**Key Visual Elements:**
- **The Crone's Archive** — Goya's "Saturn" energy. The Crone is a Goya figure — emerging from the dark, measuring, weighing, preserving. The Crone's Hopkins gaze is a Goya detail: the eyes are the brightest thing in the painting, the one element that catches the light
- **Beverley's Library** — Goya's etching style. The library is a Goya etching: precise, detailed, layered. The shelves are dense with detail — each book a small element in a complex composition. Beverley is a Goya figure: small, precise, intimidating
- **Charles's Natural Archive** — Goya's "Still Life" energy. Charles's specimens — stones, crystals, minerals — are arranged like Goya's still lifes: dark grounds, dramatic lighting, objects that carry weight. Charles's collection is the Catacombs' Goya still life
- **The Visitors' Shelf** — Goya's "Witches' Sabbath" energy, but softer. The shelf holds the belongings of visitors who came to the Inn and left something behind. The objects are arranged in a Goya composition — emerging from shadow, each with a story, each with a weight

**Linework:** Heavy cel-shaded (tradesman style) for the objects and shelves — the archive is solid, real, heavy. Soft flowing (lore style) for the Crone's measuring — the work of archiving is gentle, careful. The contrast: solid objects in a flowing space.

The 84Hz frequency (the death octave) is visible as a Dalí effect: the air itself seems to thicken, to slow, to become viscous. The player's movement slows. Time becomes a medium, not a constant

Sharp/angular (guardian style) for the Unseelie Court — everything is crystalline, rigid, sharp. ## HOLLOW EARTH — GEORGIA O'KEEFFE

**The Artist:** O'Keeffe's paintings are about the ESSENCE of natural forms — bones, flowers, deserts, skies. Stripped to their purest shape. Enormous close-ups that make the familiar alien. Luminous. Spare. O'Keeffe painted the bone because the bone outlasts the body — permanence made visible.

**Why O'Keeffe for the Hollow Earth:** The Hollow Earth is the Earth's essence — the crystal interior, stripped to pure form and light. O'Keeffe's bones-against-sky paintings are the Hollow Earth's aesthetic: white forms against deep space, luminous and spare. O'Keeffe's flower close-ups — enormous, detailed, luminous — match the crystal formations' scale and beauty. The Hollow Earth is the Earth's O'Keeffe painting: the inside, seen close, stripped to essence.

**Color Palette:**
- O'Keeffe's palette: bone white (#F5F0E8), sky blue (#87CEEB), desert tan (#D2B48C), deep shadow, pure black
- The Hollow Earth's crystal: bone white, with O'Keeffe's subtle warm undertones — the crystal is not cold white but warm, alive
- The underground ocean: O'Keeffe's sky blue — the water IS the sky in the Hollow Earth, the inverted heavens
- The Resonance Engine: O'Keeffe's "Pelvis with Distance" — a bone-white form against deep blue, luminous, spare, enormous

**Key Visual Elements:**
- **The Crystal City** — O'Keeffe's "Bone Series" energy. The crystal spires are bone-white forms against the Hollow Earth's deep violet-dark. The city is SPARE — no unnecessary detail, no ornamentation. The crystal's natural beauty is the design. O'Keeffe's aesthetic: don't decorate what's already perfect
- **The Resonance Engine** — O'Keeffe's "Pelvis with Distance." The spire is a bone-white hexagonal form, enormous, luminous, seen from a distance against the violet-dark. The spire is the Hollow Earth's O'Keeffe moment: one perfect form, enormous, luminous, alone
- **The Echoes** — O'Keeffe's figure studies. The memory-ghosts are luminous shapes, defined by the quality of their light, not by outlines. The Echoes are O'Keeffe figures: spare, essential, more PRESENCE than person
- **The Crystal Gardens** — O'Keeffe's flower close-ups. The crystal formations in the Dark Reaches are enormous, detailed, luminous — O'Keeffe's close-up technique applied to crystal. The flowers bloom at 42Hz harmonics, and the visual is O'Keeffe: one bloom, enormous, filling the frame, luminous against the dark

**Linework:** No-outline (cosmological style). O'Keeffe's forms are defined by light and color, not by edges. The crystal city is the Cosmology's underground mirror — both no-outline, both defined by luminosity.

## WORLD GRID — CLAUDE MONET

**The Artist:** Monet's landscapes are about LIGHT and ATMOSPHERE. The same haystack looks different at dawn, noon, and dusk. The same water lily pond changes with every passing cloud. Monet doesn't paint objects — he paints the light that touches objects. The world IS the light.

**Why Monet for the World Grid:** The World Grid is the OPEN WORLD — the landscape the player explores. The day/night cycle, the seasons, the weather — these are Monet's subjects. The World Grid changes with the time of day and the weather the way a Monet painting changes with the light. The same landscape looks different at dawn, noon, dusk, and night. The same region looks different in spring, summer, autumn, and winter. The World Grid is a living Monet series.

**Color Palette:**
- Monet's palette: soft blues, greens, pinks, lavenders, warm yellows — the Impressionist range
- Each region has its own Monet palette:
  - The Moorlands: Monet's "Haystacks" — golden greens, misty blues, soft amber light
  - The Desert: Monet's "Sunflowers" — warm ochres, deep blues, golden light
  - The Bamboo Forests: Monet's "Japanese Bridge" — soft greens, water blues, lavender shadows
  - The Fjords: Monet's "Ice" series — cool blues, whites, deep greens, aurora-lit purples
  - The Savannah: Monet's "Wildflowers" — golden yellows, warm browns, deep greens
  - The Songline Desert: Monet's "Rocks at Belle-Île" — red earth, deep blue sky, stark shadows

**Key Visual Elements:**
- **The Light** — the World Grid's light is Monet's light. It changes. Dawn is soft pink and lavender. Noon is bright and warm. Dusk is golden and amber. Night is deep blue with silver moonlight. The light is the most important visual element — it defines the mood of the entire World Grid
- **The Ley Lines** — visible (with Ley Line Vision) as golden threads in the landscape. The threads glow against the Monet palette — warm gold against soft blues and greens. The ley lines are the one element that DOESN'T change with the light — they glow the same gold at dawn, noon, dusk, and night. They're the World Grid's constant
- **The Inn on the Horizon** — the tree's canopy, visible from anywhere on the World Grid, is a Monet distant form: soft, warm, luminous. The Inn doesn't have hard edges from a distance — it's a warm glow on the horizon, a Monet light effect. The closer the player gets, the more defined it becomes. From a distance, it's pure light. Up close, it's architecture
- **The Water** — Monet's "Water Lilies." The World Grid's rivers, lakes, and oceans are Monet water: reflective, shimmering, full of color. The water doesn't just reflect the sky — it has its own color, its own life. The 42Hz frequency is visible in the water as a subtle golden tinge — the water carries the Song
- **The Weather** — Monet's "Rouen Cathedral" series. The same landscape in different weather: fog softens everything, rain deepens the colors, the ley line storm turns the whole scene golden. The weather IS the painting — it transforms the World Grid the way Monet's light transforms the Cathedral

**Linework:** Soft flowing (lore style). Monet's world has no hard edges — everything is defined by light and color, not by outlines. The World Grid is the LORE layer made explorable: the softest, most flowing linework in the game.

## THE GORGON'S GARDEN — AUBREY BEARDSLEY

**The Artist:** Beardsley's illustrations are black-and-white, ornate, gothic, and ELEGANT. His work for "Salome" — the deadly woman who demands a head — is the definitive Art Nouveau gothic. Flat black shapes, intricate white patterns, decorative borders that seem to grow like vines. Beardsley's women are elegant, dangerous, and perfectly composed.

**Why Beardsley for the Gorgon's Garden:** The Gorgon's Garden is the Inn's Gothic poison garden — deadly beauty behind iron gates. Medusa is the preserver — elegant, dangerous, composed. Beardsley's Salome is Medusa: the woman whose gaze kills, rendered in flat black-and-white, ornate and deadly. The Gorgon's Garden is a Beardsley illustration: flat, ornate, monochrome, and beautiful in a way that makes you uncomfortable.

**Color Palette:**
- Beardsley's palette: BLACK and WHITE. That's it. The Gorgon's Garden is the ONLY zone in the Geode Inn with no color.
- The exception: the plants' 84Hz glow. The poison plants glow at a cold, sickly frequency — but the glow is rendered as a Beardsley pattern, not as color. The glow is a PATTERN in the black-and-white composition — the way Beardsley used patterns to indicate different textures and surfaces
- The only true color in the garden: Medusa's eyes. When Medusa looks at the player, her eyes are gold — the same Klimt gold from the Undercity. The one warm point in a black-and-white world. The one living thing in a garden of stillness

**Key Visual Elements:**
- **The Garden Walls** — Beardsley's decorative borders. The walls are covered in ornamental patterns: black vines on white stone, white flowers on black iron. The patterns are DENSE — Beardsley's work is claustrophobic in its detail. The walls feel like they're growing, closing in, even though they don't move
- **The Plants** — Beardsley's flat shapes. Each plant is a black silhouette against a white background (or a white silhouette against a black background). No internal detail — the plants are FLAT, the way Beardsley's figures are flat. The plant's identity is in its SHAPE, not its color
- **Medusa** — Beardsley's Salome. Tall, elegant, composed. Rendered in flat black-and-white with ornamental detail. Her snakes are Beardsley's vines — decorative, flowing, dangerous. Her gaze is the one color: gold. When the player sees Medusa's eyes, the black-and-white world has one warm point, and it's the most dangerous thing in the garden
- **The Frozen Vine** — Beardsley's "The Peacock Skirt." The frozen vine is an ornamental pattern — black vine on white stone, perfectly preserved, perfectly still. The vine is the most Beardsley object in the garden: decorative, flat, beautiful, and DEAD
- **The Gate** — Beardsley's decorative ironwork. The gate is an ornamental composition: black iron, white stone, a skull-and-crossbones rendered in Beardsley's precise line work. The gate is a FRAME — the way Beardsley framed his illustrations with ornamental borders. The garden is the illustration; the gate is the frame

**Linework:** Heavy cel-shaded (tradesman style) — but ONLY in black and white. No color cel-shading. The Beardsley aesthetic is flat, high-contrast, ornamental. Characters in the garden (Medusa, the Gatekeeper gargoyle, the player) are rendered in black-and-white with bold outlines — the only zone where the player's own appearance changes to match the environment.
